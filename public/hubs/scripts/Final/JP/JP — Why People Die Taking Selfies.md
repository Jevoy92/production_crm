## CHAPTER 1: THE FELT MOMENT

[VISUAL: Cold open. A canyon overlook, golden hour — stock or B-roll. A pair of yellow composition brackets fades onto the frame, the kind your camera app draws. The brackets will recur all episode — the device. Cut to Jevoy at the kitchen island.]

Be honest with me for a second. Have you ever taken one extra step backward — for the shot?

Edge of a trail. Hotel balcony. Side of the road in front of something beautiful. The phone comes up, the frame appears, and for the next four seconds your whole world is that little rectangle. Get the angle. Fit the mountain in. One small step back—

[VISUAL: The yellow brackets snap onto Jevoy's face. He lowers the phone in his hand.]

Almost all of us have done it. Almost all of us were fine.

But since 2011, researchers have been counting the people who weren't. One peer-reviewed study documented 259 deaths in just six years — drownings, falls, trains. The real number is almost certainly higher, because death certificates say "drowning," they don't say "drowning while composing a shot." It got serious enough that in 2015, the Russian government — the actual Interior Ministry — published a public safety pamphlet about it. There's a real government document that exists in this world whose message is, roughly: *a cool selfie could cost you your life.*
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Bansal et al. — 'Selfies: A boon or bane?', J. Family Medicine & Primary Care (AIIMS Bhopal), 2018.]

Now — this is not the video where I shake my head about narcissism and "kids these days." Come on now. The data says the average victim is around twenty-three years old and most of them weren't doing anything that looked reckless from inside their own experience. That's the part that should stop you.

So here's the investigation. I'm going to show you a famous experiment with a gorilla — a literal person in a gorilla suit — that explains exactly what happens to your brain inside that little rectangle. I'm going to walk through what the numbers actually say, clean-eyed, no lectures. And I want to chase the bigger question underneath it: what does it mean that the most dangerous predator our attention faces in 2026... is a frame?
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Simons & Chabris — 'Gorillas in Our Midst,' Perception, 1999.]

[VISUAL: Brackets flicker onto the empty counter beside him. Hold.]

## CHAPTER 2: THE MECHANISM — THE GORILLA

[VISUAL: Jevoy stands. Insert: a basketball passing between hands, tight crops. Never show the actual study footage — describe it.]

In 1999, two psychologists, Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris, ran what became one of the most famous experiments in the history of attention research.

The setup is almost silly. They filmed two teams passing basketballs — one team in white shirts, one in black — and asked viewers to count the passes made by the white team. Simple. Count the passes. People focus, they count, most get it about right.

Except halfway through the video, a person in a full gorilla suit walks directly into the middle of the frame, faces the camera, beats their chest, and walks off. Nine seconds of gorilla. Center screen.

**About half the viewers never see it.** Not "barely noticed it." *Never saw it.* When researchers rewound the tape, people accused them of switching videos.

They called it **inattentional blindness**, and here's the cold mechanic underneath it: attention is not a spotlight that dims everything else a little. For things outside its beam, it can be a blackout. When your brain commits to one visual task — counting passes... or composing a shot — unattended objects don't get processed *less.* Some of them don't get processed *at all.* The gorilla is invisible. The wave behind you is invisible. The edge is invisible.

And the selfie is arguably the most aggressive attention trap ever put in a pocket, because it's a *double* task. You're composing the frame — that's the counting task — and you're evaluating your own face inside it, which we know from the watched-brain investigation recruits a whole second monitoring program. Frame plus face. There is no third channel left for the world. The rectangle doesn't just hold your attention, bro. It *evicts* everything outside it.

[VISUAL: The brackets appear around a tight crop of his eyes. The background behind him falls out of focus — practically, in-lens.]

That's not stupidity. That's architecture. Your attention was built with a hard ceiling, and the phone found it.

## CHAPTER 3: THE EVIDENCE — WHAT THE NUMBERS ACTUALLY SAY

[VISUAL: Clean infographic beats as he talks. Numbers on screen. No incident footage — silhouettes and maps only.]

Let's do the numbers properly, because this topic usually gets either mocked or moralized, and it deserves neither.

The study I keep citing — Bansal and colleagues, 2018, in the *Journal of Family Medicine and Primary Care*, out of AIIMS Bhopal in India — counted media-documented cases from October 2011 to November 2017. **259 deaths across 137 incidents.** Mean age: about 23. Roughly three-quarters of the victims, men. The top causes: drowning, transport — meaning, mostly, trains — and falls. The highest counts: India, then Russia, the United States, Pakistan.

An earlier project from Carnegie Mellon and IIIT Delhi — researchers literally titled it *"Me, Myself and My Killfie"* — tracked 127 deaths in just two and a half years and found something that should reframe the whole conversation: many victims weren't alone. Group selfies. Friends, together, all inside the same rectangle at the same time. Five pairs of eyes, zero on the water.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Lamba et al. — 'Me, Myself and My Killfie,' Carnegie Mellon / IIIT-Delhi, 2016.]

And sit with the *kinds* of places in the data. Cliff edges at national parks. Beaches with riptides. Train tracks — because tracks photograph beautifully, they have leading lines, every photography tutorial on earth says so. In 2018, a married couple — travel bloggers — fell from Taft Point in Yosemite, a ledge thousands of feet over the valley that appears in thousands of photos a year. They were photographers. Experienced ones. This is the detail that keeps surfacing in case after case: these were not people who didn't understand cameras. They were people who understood cameras so well that the frame had become the most real thing in their field of vision.

[VISUAL: The yellow brackets drift across a wide landscape shot, and everything outside the brackets slowly desaturates to gray. Hold the image.]

That's the picture the data paints. Not recklessness. *Absorption.* The rectangle stays in color. The world goes gray.

## CHAPTER 4: THE SKEPTIC

[VISUAL: Jevoy pulls the stool over. Sits. Fair, direct.]

Now, the skeptic has been doing math this whole time, and the skeptic's math is not wrong. Let me make their case with full respect.

The skeptic says: "Jevoy. 259 deaths over six years, on a planet where people take — what, hundreds of billions of photos a year? More people die from bee stings. Way more die in bathtubs. By any honest base rate, the selfie is one of the safest activities humanity has ever invented. This is a moral panic with a yellow rectangle on it. You're doing the thing the news does — taking a vanishingly rare tragedy and making everyone afraid of their own phone."

And on the base rate, the skeptic is simply right. I'll say it plainly: statistically, you will almost certainly never be harmed taking a photo. The count is small, even granting serious undercounting. If this video were trying to scare you off photography, it would be dishonest, and you should unsubscribe.

But here's what the base-rate argument quietly concedes — and it's the entire point. Nobody dies from a bee sting because the bee *captured their attention so completely they forgot a cliff existed.* The deaths aren't interesting because of their count. They're interesting because of their *mechanism.* They are the canary in the coal mine — the rare, vivid, fatal edge of something that is happening to all of us constantly at non-fatal doses. The same blackout that takes a photographer off a ledge takes a driver's eyes off the road at seventy miles an hour — and *that* one kills thousands. It takes a parent's eyes off the pool. It takes four friends' eyes off the tide. The selfie death is not a statistic problem. It's a *demonstration.* It's the mechanism showing us, in the starkest currency there is, what the rectangle can do to a human field of vision.

The skeptic wins the argument about fear. The mechanism wins the argument about attention. We're keeping both.

## CHAPTER 5: THE FLIP

[VISUAL: First music of the episode — low, warm. Lighting tightens.]

So here's the flip.

The question I started with was *why do people die taking selfies* — and the honest answer is: because they're people. Because attention has a hard ceiling, because the frame is a task that fills it, and because no amount of intelligence or experience raises that ceiling. The gorilla study was run on university students with perfect eyesight and nothing at stake. Half of them went blind to a chest-beating gorilla. The ledge never stood a chance.

But notice what that answer does. It moves the problem from *character* to *architecture* — and that changes everything downstream. Because if it's character — if it's vanity, stupidity, "kids these days" — then the solution is judgment, and judgment has never saved anyone standing on a ledge. But if it's architecture, then the solution is *design.* You don't shame a blind spot. You build mirrors for it.

And the deepest part, the part I can't stop turning over: *why is the frame so strong?* Why does the rectangle beat the cliff? Because the frame isn't just a picture — it's the audience. The moment the brackets come up, you're not standing at the canyon anymore. You're standing in front of everyone who will ever see this photo — the watched brain, the imagined crowd, the looking-glass self. The cliff is real, but the audience is *realer.* Three hundred thousand years of wiring says the eyes watching you matter more than almost anything. The phone just found a way to put those eyes between you and the ground.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Charles Cooley — the looking-glass self, 1902.]

People don't step backward off ledges for a photo. They step backward for the *people inside* the photo. That's not vanity. That's the oldest hunger we have, pointed somewhere lethal.

## CHAPTER 6: THE PRACTICAL

[VISUAL: Music low. He stands, moves. The yellow brackets follow him until — at the end of this chapter — they vanish.]

So what do we do — those of us who are statistically safe but mechanically identical? Three rules. Small ones. They're really attention rules wearing camera clothes.

**One: frame last.** The order matters more than anything. Look first — full seconds, real looking — pick where you'll stand, check what's behind you, *then* raise the phone. Never walk while the brackets are up. Never step *backward, ever,* with the frame open. The rule is five words: feet first, frame last. The rectangle gets your attention only after the world has signed off on your position.

**Two: one task per moment.** The gorilla study's real lesson for daily life: you don't multitask attention, you *ration* it. Driving is a full tank. Walking a trail edge is a full tank. Watching your kid in the pool is a full tank. The frame is also a full tank. So treat capture as its own moment — stop, take the photo, put the phone away, return to the world — instead of letting the rectangle run in parallel with your life like a second engine stealing fuel.

**Three: name the audience.** Before the important shots — the risky ones, the ones at the edge of something — ask the watched-brain question out loud: *who is this for?* Sometimes the answer is beautiful: your mother, your future self, the friend who couldn't come. Take that photo, carefully. But sometimes the honest answer is "the crowd in my head." And you're allowed to decline that crowd. They were never standing on the ledge with you. You don't owe them a single step.

[VISUAL: The yellow brackets fade off the frame entirely. For the first time in the episode, the shot is clean. Hold.]

## CHAPTER 7: THE INSIGHT

[VISUAL: Back at the island. Music swells gently. Direct to lens, slow.]

Here's where I land.

Two hundred fifty-nine people walked to the most beautiful places on earth — canyons, coastlines, mountaintops — and in their last moments, they were looking at a screen showing them the place they were already standing. That's not an indictment of them. It's a mirror for all of us — the clearest one we have. Because most of us are doing the gentle version every day: present at the dinner, inside the rectangle. At the recital, inside the rectangle. At the canyon, checking how the canyon looks.

The frame is a magnificent servant. It's how I make my living, you feel me — I point rectangles at things for a living. But the frame is a blind master. It will take everything you give it, and what you give it is subtracted — fully, not partially — from the world.

There's an old line — *be still, and know.* I used to read that as being about quiet. I don't anymore. I think it's about *attention.* Be still — stop composing, stop performing, stop framing — and *know.* Some things can only be known by an attention that isn't splitting itself for an audience. The canyon is one of them. The people you love are several thousand of them.

So take the photo. Feet first, frame last. And then put the rectangle down and let the place have all of you for a minute. That's not wasted footage. That's the original format.

If this question lives in you too — what the frame is doing to us, and what we do about it — stay close. That's what we're investigating here. One case at a time.

[VISUAL: He sets the phone face-down on the counter. Clean final frame, no brackets. Cut to black.]
