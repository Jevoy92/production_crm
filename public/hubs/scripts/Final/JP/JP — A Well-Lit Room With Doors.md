# JEVOY PALMER — "A Well-Lit Room With Doors"
## Cinematic Investigative Monologue (staged) | ~12 min | Jevoy Palmer main channel
## The Recorded Animal · Approach: GLASS-BOX conceit, friend dials in · Delivery: propulsive, theatrical, funny, sharp
### Voice-calibrated: bro-ban ✓ friend = recurring character (dials in via phone/Zoom) ✓ no verdict — the audience decides ✓ actionable CTA ✓ faith quiet ✓ plain language ✓ · Standalone · shares concept + research with MYB/PH

> **STAGING (the gag):** Jevoy is filming himself inside a glass-walled space — a glass meeting room, a storefront window, a sunroom, a bus shelter — somewhere everyone outside can see in. The comedy engine: he treats this fishbowl like a courageous "experiment in radical visibility," while clearly being mildly uncomfortable that strangers keep looking at him through the glass. Then he physically moves the demonstration to a normal room with blinds and doors. The "friend" (the counter) dials in on **FaceTime** from somewhere absurdly mundane and argues for total transparency. Recurring character, unlimited runway.
> **PRODUCTION:** needs a glass location + a 2nd shoot for the friend's video-call footage. B-roll: strangers glancing through the glass, blinds opening/closing, a porch light, three closed doors, the phone screen.

[COLD OPEN]
[VISUAL: Inside a glass-walled room — a storefront or glass office. Jevoy sits in the middle, perfectly visible from the street. People walk by outside and glance in. No music. He looks dead into the lens.]

**JEVOY:**

I'm filming this inside a glass box. On purpose. Everyone walking by can see me right now.

[VISUAL: A stranger outside slows down, looks in, keeps walking. Jevoy gives a small, awkward wave.]

This is supposed to be the dream, right? Total visibility. Nothing to hide. Everybody can see everything.

[BEAT]

And it's making my skin crawl a little. Watch — I can't even decide where to look. Every few seconds somebody glances in, and some animal part of my brain flinches. Nowhere to put my eyes. Nowhere to not be seen.

Which is the experiment. Because the entire internet keeps telling you this — the glass box — is the only way to be seen. Tear the walls down. Bleed online. Show everything, or you're hiding.

So today I want to figure out if that's actually true. Is being seen the same thing as being exposed? Because I don't think it is. I think we mixed up two completely different things, and it's the reason the most talented person you know refuses to press record.

And it's a real cost, that refusal. Because here's the trap people fall into. They go, "I'll just let the work speak for itself." Sounds humble. Sounds safe. Except the work doesn't have a voice. It can't explain itself. It can't tell anybody why it's good, or who it's for, or that it even exists. Only you can do that. So "let the work speak for itself" is usually just a polite way of staying in the dark forever.

Here's what I'm going to prove, so you can hold me to it. There's a way to be wildly visible and still keep your whole private life. It's not a compromise. It's the way humans have always done it. Stick with me — I've got the research, a guy on FaceTime who wants me to knock all my walls down, and a much better room than this one.

---

## THE FRONT STAGE AND THE BACK STAGE

[VISUAL: He stands, walks out of the glass box, and into a normal warm room — a window with blinds, a couch, doors. Visibly relaxes. Music still absent.]

Okay. That's better. Let me show you the study that runs all of this. It's old, and it's perfect.

[SOURCE CARD on screen: Goffman — 'The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life,' 1959.]

1959. A sociologist named Erving Goffman. He just watched how regular people act around other people. And he noticed we all do the exact same thing.

He said everybody has a front stage and a back stage. Basically — the front stage is where you perform. The version of you the world sees. The back stage is where you drop it. Where you get ready, and rest, and stop being "on."

Think of a waiter. Warm, smiling, great energy at your table. "How is everything tonight?" Front stage. Then he pushes through the kitchen door — and the smile drops, the shoulders fall, he leans on the counter for one second and just breathes. Back stage. Same person. Two completely different rooms, three feet apart, separated by one swinging door.

And here's the thing — you don't want the waiter to bring the kitchen to your table. You don't want him crying about his rent while you're ordering dessert. The door between those rooms isn't him being fake. It's him being good at his job, and staying a whole person while he does it.

[BEAT]

And here's the part that matters. Goffman wasn't describing fake people. He was describing *everybody.* Healthy people. The front stage isn't a lie. The back stage isn't a dirty secret. It's just how a human stays a human. Every person who has ever lived kept a back stage.

Think about the people who are *most* visible. The biggest stars on the planet. The ones whose faces you'd know anywhere. A lot of them are famously, fiercely private. You know their work, their voice, the version they put on stage. And you know almost nothing about their actual living room. That's not a contradiction. That's the whole skill. The most-seen people on earth are usually the ones with the most carefully kept doors.

Everybody has doors. Including you. Including me. Even my friend who's about to call and tell me doors are cowardly.

---

## THE NERVOUS SYSTEM SAYS NO

[VISUAL: He gestures at the blinds, the doors.]

So why does pressing record feel like a threat? Why does your whole body lock up before you post something real?

There's a name for it.

[SOURCE CARD on screen: Ashforth, Kreiner & Fugate — boundary theory, Academy of Management Review, 2000.]

Year 2000. Three researchers — Ashforth, Kreiner, and Fugate. They studied the invisible lines we draw between the parts of our lives. Work over here. Home over there. The private self behind that door. They called it boundary theory.

And the finding is simple. When those lines are clear, you feel steady. Safe. Like yourself. When they blur — when work crawls into your bed and the whole world is in every room at once — that's when the stress floods in. Not from being seen. From having nowhere left that's only yours.

They found there's a real trade in it, too. Keep your rooms very separate, and yeah, it takes a little effort to move between them — but nothing blurs, and you feel safe. Let everything blur into one big room, and sure, it's less effort up front — but now there's no edge anywhere, and the stress never turns off. Most people online chose the blur without ever choosing it. They just took the doors off because someone told them that was brave, and then wondered why they felt scraped raw all the time.

You can feel this one in your own week. Work texts at the dinner table. Your phone buzzing with the office while you're trying to be a parent. That specific irritation — that's a boundary being crossed. Two rooms bleeding into one. It's not that you hate your job. It's that the wall came down, and the body hates a missing wall.

So that dread before you hit record? That's not weakness. That's a healthy nervous system feeling a door start to come off its hinges. And the body does the smartest thing it knows. It says *no.* It picks the dark room. Safe. And completely invisible.

And this is the trap, because the dread is honest but it's aimed at the wrong thing. Your body thinks the only options are the glass box or the dark. It never got told there was a third room. So it picks the dark every time, and calls it protecting your peace. But it's not peace. It's just the lights off. And the thing you were built to share is sitting in there with you, in the dark, helping nobody.

---

## THE DEFENSE DIALS IN

[VISUAL: His phone buzzes. He looks. "Of course. Here he is." He answers a FaceTime and holds the phone up to the lens. Picture-in-picture: the FRIEND, on a treadmill, barely out of breath, fully confident.]

So — this is the guy who calls me every time to argue. Today he's on a treadmill. And he thinks I'm teaching people to hide. Go ahead, you've got till your cooldown.

[VISUAL: FRIEND, on the phone screen, walking.]

**FRIEND (video call):**
"You're dressing up hiding and calling it wisdom. There's research, by the way. 2018 — Bruk, Scholl, and Bless. The beautiful mess effect. They ran seven studies. We judge our *own* vulnerability way more harshly than other people do. When you share the messy thing, you feel weak. But the person watching? They see courage. So show the mess. The fear. The kitchen. That's what connects. Your cute little curated living room is just another mask, my friend. — Anyway, I'm hitting my intervals, gotta go."

[VISUAL: Jevoy lowers the phone. Beat. To lens.]

[SOURCE CARD on screen: Bruk, Scholl & Bless — the beautiful mess effect, JPSP, 2018.]

And — I hate it — he's right. That study is real. Seven experiments. We really do read our own openness as weakness, while everyone watching reads it as brave. And hiding everything behind a flawless front really does cost you. I'm not going to bury good evidence to keep my room tidy.

So now I've got a problem. Goffman says keep your doors. My friend says open the window and show the mess. Two real studies, pointing opposite ways. Somebody has to be wrong.

---

## THE RECONCILIATION

[VISUAL: He walks to the window. Puts a hand on the blinds.]

Except nobody's wrong. And this is where the whole thing turns.

They were never describing the same move. A window is not a missing wall.

Watch. [He opens the blinds, slow.] This is the beautiful mess effect. Opening *one* window. On purpose. Showing one real, chosen thing — a fear, a mistake, a hard lesson you've actually lived. The light comes in. People can see you're home. That's brave. People feel it. My friend is completely right about that.

[He gestures at the three closed doors behind him.] And this — the doors — those stay shut the whole time. That's Goffman. That's boundary theory. The rooms that are only yours. The window let the light in. The doors are still your doors. Both, at once. Nobody had to lose anything.

Now watch what the glass box does. [He gestures back toward where he started.] No blinds. No doors. No control over a single thing. Strangers staring in while you eat, fight, fall apart. That's not bravery. That's just exposure with no off switch.

You can open a window and keep your doors. Actually, the open window only feels safe *because* you still have the doors. Take the doors off and the open window stops being brave. It just becomes the glass box. And nobody can breathe in a glass box.

So my friend and I were never fighting. He was describing the window. I was describing the doors. The mistake everyone makes is thinking it's one switch — all open or all closed. It was never one switch. It's a window *and* doors.

And here's the tell, the way you know the difference in real life. A chosen window leaves you feeling *more* like yourself. You shared the hard lesson, and you stood a little taller. A wall that got taken leaves you feeling raw. Scraped. Like you said too much and can't take it back. Same act, from the outside. Completely opposite, on the inside. The window heals. The missing wall just bleeds.

Vulnerability is a window you choose. Exposure is a wall someone took. Two completely different things. And the whole game is knowing the difference.

---

## THE TURN — WHAT THE DARK COSTS

[VISUAL: He sits. Quieter now. The room is warm.]

So here's where it flips for me. We spend all our fear on what the open window might cost us. The trolls. The judgment. The people from high school. We never once count the cost of the dark room.

And the dark room has a cost. A brutal one.

When you keep every light off to stay safe, you're not just hiding yourself. You're hiding the thing you carry. The skill. The answer. The exact help somebody is searching for, tonight, right now.

And here's the hard part. They will not wait in the dark for you. They'll find the lit window down the street. They'll hire the person who turned on one lamp — even if that person knows less than you and cares less than you. Not because they're better. Because they were findable, and you weren't.

Picture it. Somebody out there has the exact problem you solve in your sleep. They're up at midnight, a little scared, hoping somebody good shows up. And you — the most qualified person for that exact moment — are sitting in a dark room with the blinds shut, calling it peace. They walk right past your house. It looks empty. So they knock on the lit door down the street. And you never even knew they came by.

Your dark room was never protecting you. It was just making you impossible to find. And it's quietly costing the one person who needed exactly what you know.

[BEAT]

The brave thing was never the glass box. And it was never the dark, either. The brave thing is one well-lit room — with doors you keep without apology.

---

## THE RECUSAL

[VISUAL: First music of the video fades in — low, warm. He leans back.]

And I should be honest, because I'm not standing on the far side of this. I sit in the dark room too.

I'm building right now, out loud, before I've got it all figured out. And every time I go to press record, the wave hits. *Who do you think you are? You don't have the followers, the proof, the polish.* And right behind it, the quieter one — that being seen means I'll have to hand over everything. Every door. My family. The parts that are just mine.

So for a long stretch I picked the dark. Felt safe. Stayed invisible. Useless to the person who needed the thing I actually know.

And I overcorrected for a while, honestly. When I did finally start showing up, I swung toward the glass box. Shared too much. Made my own raw stuff into content before I'd even processed it. And it didn't feel brave. It felt like a hangover. Like I'd handed strangers a key to a room I wasn't done living in yet.

What I'm learning, slowly, is that I had two rooms confused. I don't have to tear the house open to turn on a lamp. I can stand in one lit room, say one true thing about the work, and keep my hand on every door I choose. The door that says *my marriage.* The door that says *my kids.* The door that says *this part is just mine, and being a good professional doesn't cost me this.* That's not hiding. That's just being a person who has doors. Like every person who ever lived.

[VISUAL: Music fades under.]

---

## YOUR MOVE · [CTA]

[VISUAL: He stands. Direct to lens. Behind him: the open window, the closed doors.]

So I'm not going to tell you which doors to keep. That's yours. But you can't keep sitting in the dark and calling it protection. So let's do something, right now, down in the comments.

Three parts:

**One — name a door.** Drop a comment with one door you're keeping closed — the room that's just yours, the part of your life the internet doesn't get. Say it out loud. There's no shame in it. There's wisdom in it. I read every one.

**Two — turn on one light.** This week, share exactly one real, chosen thing about your work — one story, one lesson, one true thing — and not one inch more. Open one window, keep your doors. Then come back and tell me what it felt like to be seen without feeling stripped. That's the comment I'm actually waiting on, because I'm learning this in real time too.

[BEAT]

**And three** — if somebody in your life is brilliant and hiding in the dark, too private to ever be found — don't lecture them. Just send them this. Consider it someone gently turning on a lamp in the next room.

There's an old line I keep circling. The most watched man who ever lived — crowds following him everywhere, pulling at him, needing things, constantly. And the line repeats, over and over in the story: he withdrew. He slipped away to a quiet place, by himself. He kept a back stage. Not when it all fell apart — on purpose, in the busiest, most demanding seasons, when the crowds wanted the most. The most visible person in history guarded his doors the hardest. The withdrawing wasn't him hiding from the work. It was the thing that let him keep doing it. You come back from behind the door with something to give. Keep your doors. They're not a wall against people. They're where the light gets refueled.

[VISUAL: He reaches over and clicks on a small lamp. Then, behind him, we see the three doors — still closed. He gives a small nod. Cut to black.]

So turn on a light. Keep your doors. You were never built for a glass box. You were built for a well-lit room.
