## OPEN — THE HIDING GAME

[VISUAL: Cold open. A normal street. The camera is set up on a tripod, pointed at an empty spot. Then — Jevoy peeks out from behind a parked car. Crouched. Whispering. Like the lens might spot him.]

[BEAT]

Okay. I'm not coming out yet. Just talk to me from there.

[VISUAL: He stays low, peeking over the hood.]

You see this? This is me, a grown man, hiding from a camera I set up myself. On a Tuesday. And I want you to know — this is not a bit I'm doing *to* you. This is genuinely how it feels in my body every single time. There's a thing I want to say out loud, and there's a glowing rectangle that wants to keep it forever, and something old in me goes: *get down. Don't let it see you.*

[VISUAL: He ducks lower as a stranger walks past, oblivious.]

And here's the thing — you do this too. Maybe not behind a car. But you've got the idea for the video. The post. The thing you know would help somebody. You pick up the phone. You look at the lens. And then you put it down and tell yourself the lighting's bad.

So today I want to actually figure out why we hide. Why being seen feels like danger. And — this is the part that's going to get me to stand up — whether the thing we're so scared of is even out there at all.

Because there are three studies on this. The first one explains why hiding feels so smart. The second one is going to prove the fear is *right.* And the third one is going to prove the fear is lying to your face about the one number that matters. Stay down here with me. We're gonna crawl through this together.

## INVESTIGATE — WHY HIDING FEELS LIKE THE SMART MOVE

[VISUAL: Still crouched. He settles in, elbows on the hood.]

Let me start with why this feels so reasonable. Because it does. Hiding feels like the responsible adult choice. And there's a Nobel Prize that explains exactly why.

Two psychologists — Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky. They found something called loss aversion.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Kahneman & Tversky — loss aversion / prospect theory, 1979.]

Here it is, plain. The pain of losing something is about twice as strong as the joy of gaining the same thing. Lose a hundred dollars, it stings. Find a hundred dollars, it feels nice. But the sting is about double the nice. Your brain is wired to protect what you already have. Loss hits harder than gain. Every time.

[BEAT]

Now watch what that does the second you go to post something. Your brain runs the math, fast, in the dark. And it only sees the losses. The mean comment. The view count that stays at eleven. The old coworker who screenshots it and laughs. The friend who thinks you've changed. Those are vivid. Those are loud. Your brain flags every one of them in bright red. *Danger. Do not proceed. Get down behind the car.*

And the gains? The client who finally finds you. The person you help. The door that opens. Those are real too. But they're quiet. They're maybe. They're someday. Your brain barely counts them.

So you hide. And it doesn't feel like fear. It feels like *strategy.* It feels like you're being careful. That's loss aversion. It dresses up as wisdom. And we fall for the costume every single time.

[BEAT]

And notice how specific the brain gets about the losses. It doesn't just say "this is risky." It writes the whole scene. It casts the one person from high school who'll laugh. It writes their exact caption. It plays you the comment, the screenshot, the group chat. It's a tiny film studio, and it only makes horror movies, and it only screens them for you, at night, in your own head. Meanwhile the good outcome — the person who watches at 2 a.m. and thinks *finally, someone who gets it* — that one doesn't get a movie. It doesn't even get a trailer. The brain won't spend the budget on it. So the fear feels like a documentary, and the upside feels like a rumor.

That's the rig. Same evidence on both sides. One side gets a film crew. The other side gets silence.

## INVESTIGATE — THE INVOICE YOU NEVER SEE

[VISUAL: He shifts, peeks around the back of the car.]

Now here's what makes it worse. The cost of hiding is real. It's just silent.

Picture an old balance scale. Two brass plates. On one side, pile up every fear of being seen. The judgment. The cringe. The mean comment. That side drops with a thud. It feels heavy. It feels real.

The other side — the cost of staying hidden — looks empty. Because you can't see what you're losing. You don't feel the client who went with someone else because they could actually find them. You don't feel the trust you never built. You don't hear the silence of the people who needed you and never knew you existed.

[BEAT]

But imagine if every one of those printed out a receipt. Every time you hid, a little slip dropped onto the empty plate. *Thwack.* Client who never found you. *Thwack.* The room you were the best-kept secret in. *Thwack.* Another day invisible.

If you could see those receipts pile up, that plate would slam the table so hard it'd crack. You're not avoiding loss by hiding. You're just choosing the loss you can't see over the one you can. A slow, quiet bleed instead of a sharp, loud sting.

So that's the case so far. Loss aversion makes the visible risk feel huge. And it makes the invisible cost feel like nothing. We hide, and we call it being smart.

And here's how good the disguise is. Nobody walks around saying "I'm too scared to be seen." We say responsible-sounding things. *I'll post when it's polished. When the timing's right. When I've lost the weight, fixed the lighting, finished the rebrand.* Every one of those is the fear, in a nicer outfit. It learned a long time ago that "I'm scared" gets argued with, but "I'm being strategic" gets respected. So it stopped saying the true thing. It started saying the smart-sounding thing. And we believed it, because we wanted to. Hiding is so much easier to live with when you can call it a plan.

Which sounds like a closed case — *fear is dumb, just be brave.* Except no. Because somebody's about to prove the fear isn't dumb at all.

## THE FRIEND DIALS IN

[VISUAL: Jevoy pulls out his phone, still crouched. "Hold on, before I talk myself into something stupid, let me call my guy." He video-calls the friend, who picks up from somewhere absurdly casual — a couch, a barbershop chair. Picture-in-picture.]

This is the friend who argues with me about everything. And I called him on purpose, because I can feel myself about to say "fear is fake, just post," and he will not let me get away with that. Go ahead.

[VISUAL: FRIEND on the phone screen, leaning in.]

**FRIEND (video call):**
"Thank you. So my friend down there behind a car wants to tell you the fear isn't real. The fear is *extremely* real. And there's science.

2001. A team led by Roy Baumeister publishes a paper. The title says the whole thing: *Bad Is Stronger Than Good.* Across the board — relationships, money, reputation, first impressions — one bad thing outweighs many good things. It's not close.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Baumeister, Bratslavsky, Finkenauer & Vohs — 'Bad Is Stronger Than Good,' 2001.]

So think about a business. One bad video, one cringe clip, one tone-deaf post — that can outrun a hundred good ones. People remember the bad one. They forward the bad one. So protecting yourself? That's not weakness. That's *math.* My friend wants you brave. I want you to keep your job. Bad is stronger than good. Stay behind the car. Okay, I'm up next for a fade, I gotta go."

[VISUAL: Jevoy lowers the phone. Beat. Still crouched. To lens.]

And — I hate it — but that study is real. And it's strong. Bad genuinely is stronger than good. We're wired to remember the wound, not the kindness. So the fear of being seen isn't some glitch. It's pointing at a real thing. One bad moment really can cost more than a hundred good ones.

So now I'm stuck. Loss aversion says hiding feels smart. Bad-is-stronger-than-good says the fear is pointing at something real. Both true. So maybe I should just... stay down here forever.

[BEAT]

Except there's a third study. And it's the one that finally gets me off the ground.

## THE TURN — THE SPOTLIGHT IN YOUR HEAD

[VISUAL: He's still crouched, but he goes still. This is the centerpiece. Let it land slow.]

2000. Three researchers — Gilovich, Medvec, and Savitsky. And the setup is so simple it's almost mean.

They take a college student. They make him put on a deeply embarrassing t-shirt. A big photo of Barry Manilow on the front. Then they walk him into a room full of other students and have him sit there.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Gilovich, Medvec & Savitsky — the spotlight effect, JPSP, 2000.]

Then they pull him out and ask one question: how many people in that room noticed your shirt?

The students were *sure* everyone clocked it. On average, they guessed about half the room. Fifty percent. They could feel the eyes. The burning. The whole room judging the Manilow.

[BEAT]

Then the researchers asked the actual room. How many of you noticed his shirt?

About a quarter. Half of what he thought. And honestly, plenty of those barely cared.

They called it the spotlight effect. Basically, we think there's a spotlight on us. There isn't. Everybody else is starring in their own movie, worried about their *own* shirt. You are a background extra in almost every life but your own.

[VISUAL: He slowly — finally — starts to stand up. Half-crouched.]

So watch what this does to the whole case. The fear was never lying about whether judgment exists. My friend's right — bad is stronger than good, judgment is real. The fear is lying about the *size.* It tells you the whole room is watching. The data says it's a quarter, half-listening, about to forget. The spotlight you're hiding from is mostly running inside your own head.

[VISUAL: He's standing now. Out from behind the car. A little exposed. Glancing around.]

Now stack all three together. Loss aversion makes the risk feel double. The invisible cost of hiding feels like zero. And the spotlight effect inflates the audience to twice its real size. Three errors, all pointing the same way — *get down.* No wonder we hide. The math was rigged from the start.

## THE TURN DEEPENS — STEPPING OUT

[VISUAL: He takes a few steps into the open, onto the sidewalk. People walk past. Nobody looks. Nothing happens.]

Look at this. I'm standing in the open now. Full view. And — nothing. That guy didn't look up. She's on her phone. The street just... keeps going.

That's the spotlight effect in real life. The disaster I was crouched behind a car to avoid? It didn't show up. Because the room was never as full, or as focused, as the fear promised.

[BEAT]

And here's the part I want you to actually feel. The bravest-looking people online aren't braver than you. They just stopped believing the spotlight was as bright as it feels. They got one piece of data the hiders never get — they posted, and the catastrophe didn't come. The comment was kind, or there was no comment at all. And the world kept moving. That's it. That's the whole difference. Not courage. Just better information about how much anyone's really watching.

And think about it from the other side for a second. When *you* watch somebody post something a little awkward — do you sit there and judge them for an hour? You don't. You feel a flicker of *good for them,* maybe, and then your thumb keeps moving and they're gone from your mind before the next video loads. You give everyone else that grace automatically. You're just the one person on earth you refuse to give it to. Everyone's the main character of their own movie, and a background extra in everyone else's. Including yours. Especially yours.

You can't get that information from behind the car. You can only get it by standing up once. The fear writes a check the reality never cashes. But you don't find that out until you let it bounce. And the wild part is, it only takes one. One post where the sky doesn't fall, and the spotlight in your head dims a little — permanently. Hiding is the only thing that keeps the lie alive.

## THE RECUSAL

[VISUAL: First music of the video — low, warm. He walks slowly down the sidewalk, in the open now.]

And I have to be honest about my own hiding. Because I do this, and I do it for a living.

I point cameras at myself all day. You'd think I'd be cured. I'm not. There are things I know would help somebody that have sat in my drafts for months. Not because I don't have the gear. I *am* the gear. I hide for the same reason you do. Some old part of me runs the rigged math and says: stay small, stay safe, don't let it see you.

And when I get quiet about why — under the "I'm busy," under the "the timing's not right" — it's smaller and more tender than I want to admit. It's the fear that if I'm fully seen, and somebody decides they don't like it, then a real part of me takes the hit. Bad is stronger than good. I feel that in my chest. So I hide the light, and I call it humility.

But humility was never the real name for it. I checked. Real humility serves the room. This thing only ever serves me — it keeps *me* safe, comfortable, unexposed, while the person who needed the thing I know stays stuck in the dark. That's not humble. That's just fear wearing humility's coat because the coat looks better in the mirror. I caught myself doing it. Calling cowardice a virtue so I'd never have to fix it. And the second I named it honestly, it got a lot harder to keep crouching.

[BEAT]

But I keep landing on this. I don't think we were built to hide. I think the instinct to stay unseen is real, and old, and sometimes even kind — it's trying to protect you. It's just working off bad numbers. And there are people on the other side of your hiding, in the dark, looking for exactly the thing you keep behind the car. Your fear of being seen is quietly costing them the thing you were made to hand them.

[VISUAL: Music settles under. He stops, fully in the open, calm.]

## THE CLOSE · [CTA]

[VISUAL: Direct to lens. Standing in full view. He picks the camera off the tripod, holds it close.]

So here's where I land. We hide because the math is rigged. The risk feels double. The cost of hiding feels like zero. The audience feels twice its real size. And almost none of it survives you standing up once.

So I've got three things for you, and I just did the first one on camera, so it's your turn.

**One — name it.** Drop one line in the comments: the thing you've been hiding. The video you haven't made. The post that's been sitting in your drafts. Just name it out loud, right here. Naming the thing you're crouched behind the car to avoid is how you find out it's smaller than it looks.

**Two — stand up once.** This week, post the one thing you've been hiding. Or send it to one person, if the whole room feels like too much. Then come back and tell me what actually happened. Not what you feared. What *happened.* I bet the catastrophe doesn't show. That comment — the "I posted it and the sky didn't fall" comment — that's the one I'm waiting on. Because I need to know it's possible too.

[BEAT]

And if somebody you love is crouched behind their own car right now — convinced the whole room is watching, hiding a light somebody out there needs — don't lecture them. Just send them this. Sometimes that's the kindest way to say: *come out. It's safe out here. Half the room isn't even looking.*

The spotlight was always mostly in your head. Come stand in the open with me. Watch how little happens.

[VISUAL: He sets the camera back down, steps fully into frame, in the open, and just... stands there. Calm. The street moves around him. Cut to black.]
