## CHAPTER 1: THE DASHBOARD AT MIDNIGHT

[VISUAL: Cold open, no music. A dark office. A face lit only by a screen. On the screen: an analytics dashboard — views, likes, follower count. The cursor refreshes the page. Refreshes again. Cut to Jevoy in the Palmer House studio.]

What if I told you that the exact mechanism that walks photographers off cliff edges is, right now, walking businesses off a different kind of ledge — and the rectangle they're staring into is their analytics dashboard?

Here's the scene. A business owner finally commits to video. Posts consistently. And then the second addiction starts — the one nobody warned them about. The numbers. Views, likes, watch time, followers. The dashboard becomes the rectangle. They check it at lunch. They check it at midnight. A video "performs" and they feel like the business is winning. A video flops and the whole strategy feels broken — even in weeks when the actual business, the real one with invoices and clients, had nothing to do with either number.

And slowly, without one single decision anyone could point to, the content starts changing. Topics get picked because they travel, not because they're true. The hook gets louder than the substance. The expertise that built the business gets sanded down into whatever the rectangle rewards. One step backward at a time. For the shot.

Now — this is not a video telling you metrics don't matter. I run title scores on my own videos, bro. Data is half this company.

Here's what we're doing instead. I'm going to show you the attention science underneath all of this — what a frame does to everything outside it — and then a piece of *business* research with an ugly name and a twenty-million-dollar price tag that explains exactly how a measure quietly murders a mission. And then I'll show you the dashboard we actually build for clients — the one with the cliff marked on it.

## CHAPTER 2: THE MECHANISM — THE FRAME EATS THE WORLD

[VISUAL: Jevoy stands, walks the studio. Insert: the yellow selfie brackets from the flagship episode, now drawn around a views counter.]

Quick version of the science, because it's simple once you see it.

In 1999, Simons and Chabris asked people to count basketball passes on a video, and while they counted, a person in a gorilla suit walked through the middle of the frame and beat their chest. About half the viewers never saw the gorilla. Not missed it — *never saw it.* Inattentional blindness: when attention commits to one task, things outside that task don't get processed less. Some don't get processed at all.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Simons & Chabris — 'Gorillas in Our Midst,' Perception, 1999.]

Now here's the translation, and I want you to feel how exact it is.

Your attention as a business owner is the same instrument. It has the same hard ceiling. And a metrics dashboard is a *counting task* — the purest one ever designed. Bright number, goes up, goes down, refreshes on demand. The moment your strategic attention commits to that count, the gorillas start walking through your business unseen. The client who watched one quiet video and booked a $4,500 engagement — invisible, because she didn't like or comment. The referral partner who sends you your best work because your videos make you easy to vouch for — invisible. The wrong-fit audience that a "viral" video just flooded you with — invisible. The dashboard shows you passes. It does not show you gorillas.

And gorillas, in business, are where all the money is.

## CHAPTER 3: THE EVIDENCE — THE UGLY WORD AND THE WELLS FARGO LESSON

[VISUAL: At the table. A single sheet of paper, face down — the hero prop. He'll turn it over at the end of the chapter.]

Business research has a name for what happens next, and it's one of the most useful ugly words I know: **surrogation.**
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Choi, Hecht & Tayler — surrogation research (accounting), and Harris & Tayler, HBR 2019.]

Accounting researchers — Choi, Hecht, and Tayler — documented it in controlled studies: when people are given a measure that *represents* a strategy, they don't treat the measure as a stand-in. Over time, mentally, the measure *replaces* the strategy. The map eats the territory. People stop asking "is the strategy working?" and start asking "is the number moving?" — and they genuinely stop noticing those are different questions. It's not corruption. It's not laziness. It's the cognitive default — surrogation is inattentional blindness for strategy. The metric is the counting task. The mission is the gorilla.

You already know the catastrophic version of this story. A major American bank decided that *accounts opened* was the measure of its deep-relationship strategy. Reasonable proxy, on paper. Then the measure replaced the mission at scale — and employees opened millions of accounts customers never asked for. Billions in fines. A reputation that needed years of repair. Nobody at the top wrote "betray the customers" on a whiteboard. They wrote a *number* on it, and the number did the rest.

There's even an older law for this — a British economist named Goodhart, 1975: *when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.* The instant you aim at the proxy, the proxy disconnects from the thing it was supposed to proxy.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Goodhart's Law (Strathern's formulation, 1975).]

[VISUAL: He turns the paper over. It reads, large: VIEWS ≠ TRUST.]

Now bring it home to your content. Views were always a *proxy* — a rough stand-in for "the right people are finding us and starting to trust us." Useful signal, weak proxy. The moment views become the *target*, Goodhart's law fires: you can absolutely make views go up while trust goes down. Louder thumbnails, borrowed controversy, topics that travel but don't convert — the number climbs, and the business it was supposed to represent quietly walks toward the edge. Views are the selfie of business video, my friend. The picture of the thing, mistaken for the thing.

## CHAPTER 4: THE STRONGEST OBJECTION

[VISUAL: Apple box. Eye level. The objection gets the floor.]

Now the strongest objection, and it's a good one, because it comes from the people who've actually grown.

"Jevoy, this is what gatekeepers say when their numbers are small. Attention is *literally* the front of the funnel. Zero views means zero clients — that's not a proxy, that's arithmetic. The creators and brands that won — Hormozi, MrBeast, every breakout company channel — they obsessed over packaging, thumbnails, CTR, retention. You score your own titles with ViDIQ — you showed us the scores! So which is it? Do the numbers matter or not?"

Every word of that is true, and I'm not going to weasel out of it. Reach is real. Packaging is real. A video nobody clicks is a sermon in an empty room — I've said it myself, and I'll keep scoring my titles, and you should too. Distribution is not beneath you; it's the price of being findable.

But watch the move the objection makes — it's the surrogation move, in real time. "Views matter" slid into "views are the goal." Those are different sentences. The first one keeps views as an *instrument* — checked, used, subordinated to the question "are the right people trusting us more?" The second one hands the steering wheel to the proxy. And here's the test that separates them, one question: **when a video gets big numbers from the wrong audience, do you celebrate?** If yes — the metric has already replaced your mission, and you're optimizing a business you don't actually own. The plumber whose skit got two million teenage views did not gain two million potential customers. He gained a number... and a new content strategy that serves the number. One step backward at a time.

Even MrBeast — the most metrics-obsessed creator alive — is aiming at *his* actual mission: entertainment at maximum scale. His metric and his mission point the same direction. Yours probably don't. That's not a flaw. You're not in the views business. You're in the trust business. Different cliff.

## CHAPTER 5: THE FLIP

[VISUAL: First music — low, warm. Tight on Jevoy.]

So here's the flip.

You don't fix this by caring less about numbers. You fix it by changing *which number gets the wheel.* There's a rule from the psychology of attention — "feet first, frame last" — position yourself in reality before you raise the rectangle. The business version is the same five-word shape: **mission first, metric last.** Define what the content is actually for — in client language, not platform language — *then* choose the one or two numbers that genuinely track it, and demote everything else on the dashboard to weather.

And when you do that, something almost embarrassing happens: most of the numbers you've been refreshing at midnight turn out to be *someone else's mission.* Views are the platform's mission — they sell ads against your watch time. Followers are the platform's mission. Engagement is the platform's mission. None of them invoice anybody. The numbers that are *your* mission are quieter and slower and they don't refresh on demand: strategy calls booked. "I watched your videos and felt like I knew you" said out loud in a first meeting. The close rate on leads who arrived warm. Time-to-trust, collapsing.

We blamed the founder for being addicted to the dashboard. The problem was the dashboard. Rebuild it.

## CHAPTER 7: THE INVITATION

[VISUAL: Music holds. Crew silhouettes. Direct to lens.]

Here's where I'll leave it.

There's an old piece of shepherd economics — *know well the condition of your flocks.* Not the *count* of your flocks. The *condition.* Herders understood that difference thousands of years before Goodhart wrote it down: the number tells you how many sheep you have; attention tells you whether they're alive. Your views are a count. Your clients' trust is a condition. Only one of them can be refreshed at midnight, and it's the one that matters less.

So here's the homework, and then the invitation. Tonight, look at your dashboard and ask one question of every number on it: *whose mission is this?* Yours, or the platform's? Be ruthless. Most dashboards don't survive the question.

And if you want help building the one that does — book a strategy call with Palmer House. Real conversation, not a sales call dressed up as one. Bring your analytics, bring your last ten videos, and bring the sentence you *wish* your content was achieving. We'll find the gap between your numbers and your mission — and we'll aim the cameras at the mission.

The link is below. Feet first, frame last.
