## CHAPTER 1: THE TWO PHOTOS

[VISUAL: A phone held to the lens. A blurry photo, then a sharp one. Pull back to Jevoy in the Palmer House studio.]

**JEVOY:**

Picture two photographs. The first one is blurry. Out of focus. You can tell there are people in it, maybe a building, but you can't make anything out. You look for a second, you squint, and you hand it back. It's too much work.

The second one is tack sharp. You can see the expression on the face. The texture of the jacket. The time on the clock behind them. You look at that one *longer.* You understand the story instantly. Your shoulders drop. You didn't decide to trust it. Your body decided for you.

[BEAT]

Your message works exactly the same way. And most businesses are handing customers the blurry one, then wondering why nobody leans in.

You've felt it. You explain what you do, and you watch their eyes go soft. The polite nod. The "I support you but I have no idea what you're saying" nod. That nod is a customer squinting at a blurry photo and quietly handing it back. So today I want to show you why that happens, and how the businesses people actually remember hand over a sharp one.

## CHAPTER 2: THE MECHANISM — APPLE VS. NUTRITION

Here's the study. It starts with one word.

When I say "apple," your brain doesn't define it. It *shows* it to you. The red skin, the crunch. Instant. A picture.

Now I say "nutrition." And your brain stalls. It goes searching for a definition, then an example. There's no picture for "nutrition." It's just fog with a dictionary entry.

A researcher named Allan Paivio found out why, back in 1971. He called it dual coding.
[SOURCE CARD: Allan Paivio — Dual Coding Theory / the concreteness effect, 1971.]

Basically, concrete words get filed twice in your brain — once as a word, once as a picture. Abstract words only get filed once. So the concrete one comes back faster, sticks harder, and feels more true. The brain doesn't want impressive. It wants the picture. It wants "apple."

And there's a ladder under this. A linguist named S. I. Hayakawa drew it in 1939 — the ladder of abstraction. At the top, the big cloudy words. Wealth. Success. Solutions. At the bottom, things you can hold. A hundred-dollar bill. A gold medal. A specific tool.
[SOURCE CARD: S. I. Hayakawa — the ladder of abstraction, 1939.]

[BEAT]

When we describe our business, we climb straight to the top of the ladder. We think the value lives up there. But connection happens at the bottom. You have to bring a customer to the ground before you point at the sky. "Financial freedom" is the top. "Buying groceries without checking your balance" is the apple. One they squint at. One they feel.

## CHAPTER 2.5: ONE NAME BEATS A MILLION

Here's how powerful this is. It can beat your customer's own math.

There's a study from 2007 — Small, Loewenstein, and Slovic. They asked people to donate to fight hunger. One group got the statistics. Millions starving. Staggering, true numbers. The other group got one girl. A name — Rokia. A photo. Just her.
[SOURCE CARD: Small, Loewenstein & Slovic — the "identifiable victim effect," 2007.]

The people who saw the one girl gave more. A lot more. And the millions — the bigger, truer, more urgent number — actually made people give *less.* Because the heart can't picture a million. There's no image for it. But one girl with a name and a face is an apple. You can see her. So you act.

[BEAT]

Now hear that as a marketer. "We help businesses everywhere grow" is the famine statistic. Nobody reaches for their wallet over "everywhere." But "we got a flooded basement pumped out at 2 a.m. for a couple with a newborn upstairs" — that's the one girl. That's the sharp photo. The customer can see it, feel it, and trust it. One real story about one real person beats every wide, blurry claim about "everyone" you could ever write. Specific is how you get someone to actually move.

## CHAPTER 3: WHY WE BLUR IT ON PURPOSE

Here's the part that really matters for your business.

Most business messaging lives at the top of the ladder. "We deliver innovative solutions." "We empower brands to optimize their potential." Fog, all the way up. And the customer does to you exactly what you do to a blurry photo. One squint, and they hand it back. They scroll on.

I watched a friend do this. Brilliant guy, real software, two years of work. He pitched it to me for ten minutes — "synergistic workflow optimization," "holistic ecosystem integration." And I had to stop him. "What does it actually do?"

He took a breath and said, "It stops you from typing the same client info into three different apps."

I just looked at him. "Why didn't you say *that?*"

Because the plain version felt too small. He built it for two years. "It saves you from typing twice" felt like an insult to the work. So he reached for the big words to make it sound as big as it felt to build. And every one of them made him harder to understand, not more impressive. He handed me a blurry photo and asked me to focus it. I almost didn't bother. Your customers won't either.

## CHAPTER 4: THE HONEST OBJECTION

Now there's a fair argument against everything I just said. And I'm going to make it properly, because if I skip it, you should stop trusting me.

You could say: "Jevoy, that's fine for simple things. But what I do is complex. Nuanced. If I boil it down to one specific sentence, I'm leaving out ninety percent of the value. I'm dumbing it down."

And there's real science behind the power of the big picture. There's a line of research — construal level theory — showing abstract thinking is how the brain handles big, far-off goals. Vision. The long game.
[SOURCE CARD: Trope & Liberman — construal level theory, 2010.]

Think about it. Martin Luther King didn't say "I have a twelve-point plan." He said "I have a dream." That's the top of the ladder, and it moved a nation. You can't build something that matters on tools and tactics alone. The objection is right that abstraction has power.

But here's where it breaks. Listen to what King said *right after* that line. He didn't stay at the top. He came down to the ground. The red hills of Georgia. Children holding hands. Specific places, specific faces. The dream was the roof. Every image he hung under it was an apple. The abstraction told you where you were going. The concrete details were the only way you got in the door.

---

## CHAPTER 5: THE STAIRCASE

So put the two together and you get the whole thing.

It was never concrete *or* abstract. It's a staircase. Abstraction is the destination. The concrete detail is the door.

Lead with the sky and people glaze. Lead with the ground, then point up, and they follow you all the way to the roof. Same order, every time. Specific first. Big second.

Picture two businesses pitching the same thing. One says, "We provide sustainable, scalable solutions for growing companies." Your eyes glaze. The other says, "We stopped a 12-person team from losing two hours a day to copy-paste." You're in. *Then* they say "we help companies scale" — and now it lands, because you gave it a picture first.

Same business. The blur lost. The sharp photo won. Not because it was smaller. Because it had a door. And here's the part that should sting a little — broad doesn't mean you reach more people. Broad means invisible. When you speak to everyone, you speak to no one. The specific business is the one that gets remembered.

And this is costing you money you never see. A confused customer doesn't email you to say "I didn't understand your homepage." They just leave. Silently. There's no alert for "person who squinted and scrolled on." It's the most expensive thing in your business, and it's completely invisible. Somebody lands on your page, gives you a couple of seconds, and their brain asks one question — "do I get this, yes or no?" If the answer is no, they're gone, and they take their wallet to the competitor whose sentence was sharp. You didn't lose them because your work is worse. You lost them because they couldn't tell what your work *was.* And here's the gut-punch — you probably paid more to lose them. The fancy, foggy version usually cost more to make than the plain, sharp one would have.

---

## CHAPTER 6: THE FLIP

So here's the flip.

You don't have a complexity problem. You have a focus problem. You've been handing customers the blurry photo — "solutions," "synergy," "we empower" — and asking *them* to do the work of bringing you into focus. And they're tired. They have their own blurry photos. They will not focus yours.

[PAUSE]

The fear under it is that the specific truth is too small. That "we save you two hours a day" isn't impressive enough for how hard you worked. But specific is exactly what makes you universal. The most specific story is the most relatable one — because a real detail is a door anyone can walk through. The fog feels safe. The fog is just forgettable.

And there's a quieter fear underneath that one. When you get specific, you draw a line. "This is exactly what I do, for exactly these people." Which also says, "and not for everyone else." That feels scary. It feels like turning customers away. So we stay broad to keep every door open. But broad doesn't keep the doors open. It just makes you invisible to everyone at once. The business that says "we do everything for everyone" gets hired by no one. The business that says "we do this one thing, better than anyone, for people like you" — that's the one that gets the call. The line you're afraid to draw is the exact thing that makes you findable.

The blurry photo holds your effort. Only the sharp one holds the customer.

## CHAPTER 7: WHAT PALMER HOUSE ACTUALLY DOES

This is where Palmer House Productions comes in. And I'll be straight about where we are. Young company, building in the open, out here in the Pacific Northwest. So we're not preaching from a finished mountaintop. We're finding our own apples in real time, same as you.

Palmer House is a translation company that happens to own cameras. We don't help you sound bigger. We help you come into *focus* — and then we point all that clarity at the sky.

Here's how it actually works, plainly. We don't start with "what's our brand vision statement." That's the top of the ladder, and it's where everyone gets stuck in fog. We start at the bottom. One question: what's the most specific, real thing you do for one real person? The apple. Then we build the picture around it.

And we build it to do three jobs, in plain terms. One — a concrete, specific moment, so the photo is sharp and the customer's body relaxes. Two — one real face or one real story, because the brain files a picture twice and a buzzword once. And three — *then* the bigger meaning on top, the freedom or the trust or the vision, so the apple opens up into the sky once you're already in the door.

And the hardest, most valuable part happens before we ever film. It's getting you to say the small thing. Every owner walks in with the forest — "we provide comprehensive solutions." And we just keep asking the same annoying question: "okay, but who, and for what, exactly?" Until the tree falls out. The one client. The one problem. The one moment it actually mattered. That sentence is almost always hiding in plain sight, and you've been talking right over it for years because it felt too small to be the headline. It was always the headline.

Let me show you what that looks like, fast. Say you're a financial advisor. The fog move is a reel about "wealth strategies for visionaries." People scroll past. The sharp move is one short film about a specific couple who finally stopped fighting about money at the kitchen table. Same business. One is a blur. The other, a stranger watches and thinks, *that's my kitchen table.* That's the call you get later.

Or say you sell something genuinely complex — software, consulting, the stuff that really is hard to explain. That's where owners fight us hardest. "Mine's too nuanced for one sentence." But complexity is not an excuse for fog. It's the opposite. The more complex your thing is, the *sharper* your front door has to be. Think of a cathedral. Wildly complicated to build — thousands of moving parts. But you don't walk a visitor in through the blueprints for the plumbing. You show them the front door. Specificity is the front door to your complexity, not a betrayal of it. Get them through the door with one sharp, simple thing, and *then* they'll happily explore every nuanced room you built.

Here's our one rule, and it's the same line I'll say to your face on a call. If you can't tell me the one specific thing you do for one specific person, we don't start filming. We start finding your apple. Because a beautiful video built on fog is just a gorgeous blurry photo. We're for the business that actually wants to be *seen.*

And that first conversation is usually worth more than people expect, before we shoot a single frame. Most owners have never been made to say the small version. They've rehearsed the big, impressive one a thousand times. So when we sit down and just keep gently asking "for who, exactly, and what changed for them" — the real story falls out. The one client. The 2 a.m. call. The thing they fixed that nobody else would. And they sit back, a little surprised, and say "huh — I never tell people that part." That part was the whole photo. They'd been hiding their sharpest image because it felt too ordinary to lead with.

So here's where I'll leave it.
