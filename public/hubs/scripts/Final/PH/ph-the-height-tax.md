## OPEN — THE TEMPTATION

Picture a founder at a laptop at midnight — and let's be honest, this founder has been most of us — hovering over the most loaded pronoun in small business.

"We."

Two letters. Heaviest word on the page. She's a company of one. Maybe one and a half — her cousin does the books. And she's writing the website, and every instinct she has says *we.* "We deliver." "Our team." "Our offices" — it's a desk in the spare room, and the office dog is asleep under it. And she's not trying to scam anybody. She's trying to survive a brutal truth she's already felt in her bones: when she says "I," the client's voice changes. When she says "we," the quote gets approved.

So tonight's question — and I'm going to fight it properly, both sides at full strength, because it deserves it; no strawmen in this building — is one of the oldest, most quietly agonized-over questions in small business: **should your business look bigger than it is?** Every founder has stood at this fork. Most picked a side without ever hearing the full case for either. Tonight you hear both.

And before you answer too fast in either direction, you should know two things. One: the science behind "look bigger" is real — there's a hundred years of psychology explaining why size signals work, and I'll show you the receipts, including the study that found tall people collect about eight hundred dollars a year *per inch* for nothing. And two: the case against it is also real, and it's not the moral lecture you're expecting — it's a business case, with teeth. Two rounds. A real fight. And a resolution at the end that I genuinely believe settles it. Let's go.

## ROUND ONE — THE CASE FOR LOOKING BIGGER

[VISUAL: On-screen: ROUND 1. The "pro" corner.]

The case for looking bigger opens with biology, because the bias it's exploiting is not a marketing invention — it's wiring.

In 2004, researchers Judge and Cable published the famous height study: every inch of height is associated with roughly $789 a year in additional earnings, controlling for age, gender, and weight. Tall people get paid more, promoted more, perceived as more leader-like — and there's zero evidence they *perform* any better. In his book *Blink*, Malcolm Gladwell surveyed Fortune 500 CEOs: about 58% are six feet or taller, versus 14.5% of American men. The conclusion is unavoidable: human beings pay real money for *size signals* that have nothing to do with substance. The brain reads "big" as "safe, established, capable" — and it's been doing it since following the biggest one in the group was a legitimate survival strategy.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Malcolm Gladwell — Blink (Fortune 500 CEO height survey), 2005.]

Now translate to business, says Round One. Your company has a height too — and clients read it in seconds, the same way they read a person. The polish of the website. The production value of the video. The confidence of the proposal. The "we." These are your inches. And here's a century-old mechanism that explains why they matter so much: in 1920, psychologist Edward Thorndike documented the halo effect — one positive impression bleeding into all the others. Officers who rated soldiers as physically impressive also rated them smarter and more loyal, automatically. One signal, autofilling the whole form. So when your business looks "tall" — polished, substantial — the client doesn't just think "nice website." The halo upgrades *everything*: more competent, more reliable, *worth more.* Size signals are pricing power.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Thorndike — 'A Constant Error in Psychological Ratings' (the halo effect), J. Applied Psychology, 1920.]

And it goes deeper than how others see you — the signals change how you see *yourself.* In 2012, researchers Hajo Adam and Adam Galinsky ran a study that became famous as "enclothed cognition": they gave people a white lab coat to wear and measured attention on careful, detail-oriented tasks. People wearing the coat performed measurably better — but only when they were told it was a *doctor's* coat. Same coat, described as a painter's coat? Effect gone. The garment carried a meaning, and the meaning upgraded the wearer. Round One's point: when your business wears the bigger suit, your clients aren't the only ones who believe it — *you* show up different. The polish isn't just costume. It's posture, internalized.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Adam & Galinsky — 'Enclothed Cognition,' J. Experimental Social Psychology, 2012.]

And Round One's closing argument is blunt: your competitors are already doing it. The agency with three people calls itself a "collective." The consultant has a "team" page with stock photos. The whole economy plays dress-up, the clients on some level *know* and grade on the curve anyway, and your little honest "I" is competing against everyone else's "we." Refusing to play isn't integrity, says Round One — it's unilateral disarmament. Fake it till you make it isn't a scam; it's the on-ramp.

[BEAT]

Strong round. Honestly. Real science, real logic, real money on its side. If I stopped the episode here, you'd go inflate the website tonight, and you'd feel smart doing it. Don't stop here. The second round is where it gets expensive.

## ROUND TWO — THE CASE FOR YOUR ACTUAL SIZE

[VISUAL: ROUND 2. The other corner.]

Round Two doesn't open with ethics. It opens with a question Round One can't answer:

*What happens when they find out?*

And before you say "they won't" — walk through how small business actually works. Your clients don't experience your website; they experience *you.* The discovery isn't an investigation, it's a Tuesday: the "team" cc line that's always the same one address. The "we'll have our editor handle it" followed by footage that arrives from your personal Dropbox. The client who calls the office line and hears the kids in the background. Small business is intimate by nature — that's literally its product — which means scale theater has the shortest run of any show on earth. In enterprise, the costume might survive a quarter. In your world, it survives until the second coffee.

Because they find out. The "team" that's one person becomes obvious by the second meeting. The "offices" become a Zoom background. And here's the brutal asymmetry Round Two wants on the record: the trust you gained from looking big was *rented* — the trust you lose when the inflation is discovered is *gone.* People forgive small. People do not forgive *deceived.* A client who learns you're a company of one thinks "scrappy." A client who learns you *pretended* to be a company of twelve now re-audits every other claim you ever made — your testimonials, your timeline, your price. One discovered inflation and the halo effect runs in full reverse: one false signal, autofilling the whole form with doubt.

And Round Two has a second argument that almost nobody makes, and it's my favorite: **small sells.** Not despite being small — *because* of it. Think about what the client actually gets from a company of one or three that no Fortune 500 vendor can offer: the founder answers the phone. The person who pitched is the person who does the work. Decisions take a conversation, not a committee. Care is not delegated. There are entire categories of buyer who have been burned by exactly the bigness Round One wants you to imitate — passed from account manager to account manager, invoiced by a machine — and they are actively *hunting* for small. When you disguise your size, you're hiding the exact feature they came for. You're a boutique cosplaying as a Costco, in a market full of people exhausted by Costcos.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Mita, Dermer & Knight — mirror-image preference, JPSP, 1977.]

And third: the inflation has a maintenance cost. Every meeting becomes a small performance. Every "we'll get our team on it" needs choreography. You took the one resource a small business has more of than anyone — *truth, told quickly* — and turned it into a liability you have to manage. That tax compounds worse than the height premium ever paid.

And if Round Two needs a closing exhibit, recent history wrote it in capital letters: Theranos. A company whose entire art form was scale signals — the gleaming labs, the board stacked with statesmen, the founder styled meticulously after Steve Jobs down to the turtleneck — wrapped around technology that did not do what the signals promised. For a while, the borrowed height worked *spectacularly*: nine billion dollars of valuation, magazine covers, the whole halo running at maximum. And then the truth surfaced, and the collapse wasn't proportional to the lie — it was *total*, criminal-trial total, because that's the asymmetry Round Two has been arguing all along: signals can rent belief, but the moment they're exposed as costumes, they convert every dollar of borrowed trust into a dollar of contempt, with interest. Nobody remembers Theranos as "a small lab that overreached." It's remembered as the *costume.* That's the downside case, at full scale, on the record.

## THE RESOLUTION — SIGNALS OF CARE, NOT SIGNALS OF SCALE

[VISUAL: First music — warm. Jevoy steps to the center, between the two "corners."]

So who wins? Here's the resolution, and it's not a compromise — it's a distinction the whole fight was missing.

Both rounds are arguing about "looking bigger" as if it's one thing. It's two things, and they only look alike. There are **signals of scale** — headcount, offices, "our team," years you haven't existed — claims about *size.* And there are **signals of care** — light, sound, clarity, design, preparation, the quality of your first three seconds — claims about *standards.* Here's the test that separates them: if the client found out the full truth tomorrow, would the signal become a lie?

"We have a team of twelve" — lie. Scale signal. Don't.

A beautifully lit video, a crisp proposal, a website that loads clean and reads clear? If they find out you're one person... the signal gets *stronger.* "One person did all this? Hire them immediately." That's the magic property of care signals — they're discovery-proof. Run anything through the found-out test before you publish it, and you'll never need a lawyer or an apology tour: would the full truth make this claim collapse, or make it more impressive? Scale signals collapse. Care signals compound. They're not borrowed inches; they're actual evidence of how you work, shown instead of claimed — and evidence only gets more convincing on inspection.

And here's the beautiful part: go back to the height research. The halo effect never asked for *bigness* — that was Round One's misreading. It asks for *one trustworthy signal* to autofill the form from. Care works as well as scale. Better, actually — because care predicts the client's future experience and scale doesn't. The tall CEO data proves people pay premiums for unearned signals; the move isn't to counterfeit an unearned one. It's to flood the zone with *earned* ones. Look excellent, not enormous. Be a small business the way a Leica is a small camera — nobody holds one and thinks "cheap." They think "concentrated."

Let me make the care-signal list concrete, because "look excellent" is useless without line items. Care signals your business can deploy this month, every one of them honest: video and photos with real light and clean sound — we judge craft by craft. A website that loads fast and reads clear — speed is care, made visible. Proposals designed like you'll be judged on them, because you will be. Response time — answering in two hours is a size-independent signal that out-punches any "team" page ever built. Specificity in your materials — real numbers, real process, real faces — because vagueness reads as small even when it's hiding something big, and specificity reads as established even from a company of one. And the founder's own presence, on camera, calm and clear: paradoxically the *biggest* signal on the list, because big companies can't offer the founder at all. Every item on that list survives the found-out test. Most of them cost under a hundred dollars. None of them require a single false pronoun.

So the founder at midnight? Here's her answer. Write "I." Then make every other pixel on that page testify that this particular "I" runs at a standard most "we"s can't match. The pronoun is honest. The presentation is ferocious. That's the whole strategy.

## WHAT PALMER HOUSE DOES + THE INVITATION

[VISUAL: Frame widens — the crew, the studio.]

And this is exactly the work, so let me say plainly where we stand — with the usual honesty: Palmer House Productions is a young company, building in the open, here in the Pacific Northwest. We're a video production company, though the truer description is that we're a translation company that happens to own cameras: we take what's actually true about a business and make it visible. And today's episode is the line we will not cross and the line we sprint along: we will never make your business look like something it isn't. We will absolutely make it look like *everything it is* — which, for almost every small business we meet, is dramatically more impressive than their current materials confess.

Because that's the real epidemic, by the way — not small businesses inflating. Small businesses *deflating.* Phone-propped videos and rushed websites that read as careless, attached to businesses that are actually meticulous. The work is tall; the signals slouch. Our job is posture, not stilts: the lighting, the sound, the first three seconds, the story told clean — care signals, stacked, until the halo does its hundred-year-old thing on your behalf, legitimately.

And one session is usually all it takes to flip the whole inventory, because care signals share a supply chain: the same honest hour on camera becomes the homepage video, the proposal clips, the social presence, the about page that finally sounds like the person clients actually meet. You walk in with materials that apologize for your size and walk out with a library that brags about your standards. Same business, same headcount, same pronoun — different posture everywhere a stranger looks.

So, the call to action, with homework. Book a strategy call — a real conversation, not a sales call dressed up as one. And bring this: *the one thing that makes your business look smaller than your work actually is.* The website you're embarrassed by, the video you never redid, the proposal template from 2019. We'll find where the slouch is, and we'll show you what standing up straight costs — usually a lot less than the inflation Round One was selling.

[VISUAL: Music holds. Closer.]

Last thing — the oldest piece of business wisdom there is, and it reads like it was written for the founder at midnight: whoever can be trusted with a little gets trusted with a lot.

Notice that's not a consolation prize — it's a *mechanism.* The "little" isn't a waiting room before the real thing starts. The little *is* the audition, and everyone's watching it: clients, referrals, the future version of your own company. Excellence at your true size is the only force in business that reliably produces the next size — and it's the one strategy that requires zero pretending, survives every discovery, and compounds instead of taxing you. Round One wanted you to borrow inches. The better play was always to grow them — in public, at a standard nobody can ignore.

Be small out loud, and be excellent louder. The market can hear both — and the rare business that broadcasts both at once is unforgettable, because almost nobody has the nerve. The inflated ones are everywhere and the excellent-but-invisible ones are everywhere; the honest-and-ferocious lane is wide open. Take it.
