## CHAPTER 1: THE SMUDGE

[VISUAL: Tight on a phone camera lens, a greasy thumbprint across the glass. Pull back to Jevoy in the Palmer House studio.]

**JEVOY:**

Pick up your phone. Don't unlock it. Just turn it over and look at the camera lens.

There's a smudge on it. There's always a smudge on it. We carry these things in our pockets. We hand them to friends with greasy fingers. And every photo you take this week passes through a thin film of yesterday. Then we wonder why the picture looks hazy.

Your message has the exact same problem. And no amount of better lighting fixes it. Not a fancier edit. Not a cleverer caption. Not until you wipe the lens.

[BEAT]

I scrolled business pages for two hours this week. Real companies. Companies I might actually hire. And page after page, I felt the same fog. The video was beautiful. The color was perfect. You could feel the effort coming off the screen.

And after two full minutes on one page, I had a scary thought. I had no idea what they did. None. I didn't know the problem they solved. I didn't know who it was for. I didn't know how to hire them, or why I should.

They spent everything trying to be admired. They forgot to be understood.

That's a smudge. It's just on the message, not the glass. And here's the thing — it's probably on yours too. Mine too, some weeks. So let me show you why it happens, and how the cleanest businesses wipe it.

It starts with a study about fonts.

## CHAPTER 2: THE MECHANISM — WHY EASY READS AS TRUE

In a long line of research, two psychologists, Rolf Reber and Norbert Schwarz, studied something called processing fluency.
[SOURCE CARD: Reber & Schwarz — processing fluency / judgments of truth.]

Basically, fluency just means how easy something is for your brain to take in. Easy in, easy to trust. That's the whole idea.

But here's the part that matters for your business. When something is easy to understand, the brain doesn't just find it easier. It rates it as more *true.* More trustworthy. More valuable. Easy to understand quietly becomes "probably right."

They proved it in a way that sounds made up. They took the same exact sentence and printed it two ways. One in a clean, easy font. One in a hard, messy font. Same words. People rated the easy one as more true. Nothing changed but the smudge on the glass.

So when a customer lands on your page and has to work to understand you, you're not just tiring them out. You're lowering how much they trust you. Every second of squinting feels, in their gut, like risk. And people do with risk what they always do. They scroll on.

[BEAT]

Now read that as a business owner. Your customer is standing at a foggy window. You think you need a bigger, brighter sign behind the glass. You don't. You need to wipe the glass. The clean lens doesn't just look better. It gets *believed.* And almost nobody is competing with you on that, because everybody's busy buying lenses.

## CHAPTER 3: THE SECOND SMUDGE — THE CURSE OF KNOWING

Here's the part that explains why smart people make the most confusing marketing on earth. And it's you. It's me too.

Let me show you with a quick experiment. In 1990, a researcher named Elizabeth Newton ran it at Stanford. One person taps out a famous song on a table. Happy Birthday, say. Another person just listens and guesses.
[SOURCE CARD: Elizabeth Newton — "tappers and listeners," Stanford, 1990.]

Before they tapped, she asked the tappers a question. How many listeners do you think will guess the song? They figured about half. Fifty percent.

The real number was about two and a half percent. One in forty.

Why so low? Because in the tapper's head, the whole song is playing. The melody, the words, the off-key part. It's obvious. But the listener only hears random knocking on a table. And the tapper *cannot* hear it the listener's way anymore. They know the song. They can't un-know it.

Economists gave that a name back in 1989 — Camerer, Loewenstein, and Weber called it the curse of knowledge. Basically, once you know something, you forget what it felt like to not know it.
[SOURCE CARD: Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber — "the curse of knowledge," 1989.]

[BEAT]

You are the tapper. You know your business cold. You know the jargon. You know the backstory and the philosophy. So when you describe it, your brain skips the basics. They feel obvious. They feel boring.

So you don't say "I help plumbers get more calls." That feels too plain for how hard you worked. You say you "architect growth ecosystems for home-service professionals." And you walk away feeling sharp. You just tapped Happy Birthday on a table and wondered why nobody sang along. That's the smudge, and you can't see it. Nobody can see their own.

## CHAPTER 4: THE ONE YOU SCROLLED PAST

Let me make this real, because right now it's still a study.

Picture two businesses in your feed. Same week. Both financial advisors.

The first one says, "I orchestrate bespoke wealth-accumulation strategies for high-net-worth visionaries." Your thumb doesn't slow down. You couldn't repeat that sentence if I paid you. It scrolls by like fog.

The second one says, "I help families build enough wealth to stop worrying about money before they retire." And you stop. You can see it. You can almost feel the relief of it — the bank balance you don't have to check first. Three weeks later, you still remember her. Even though you never followed, never liked it.

That's the whole thing in one comparison. The first one fogged the lens. The second one wiped it.

And here's what should sting a little. The fancy version probably took *longer* to write. The smudge wasn't the lazy option. It was just the forgettable one. This is what marketers call clarity, and clarity isn't a creative downgrade. Basically, it's the difference between being seen and being skipped.

## CHAPTER 4.5: WHAT THE FOG ACTUALLY COSTS

Let me put real numbers on this, because right now it still feels like a vibe.

A confused customer doesn't argue with you. They don't email you for clarification. They just leave. Quietly. You never see it happen. There's no notification for "person who didn't understand you and moved on." It's the most expensive thing in your business, and it's completely invisible.

Think about your homepage. Somebody lands on it. You get a few seconds, maybe less. In those few seconds, their brain is asking one question: "Do I get this, yes or no?" Not "is this impressive." Not "did they win an award." Just — can I understand it, fast. If the answer is no, they're gone, and they take their wallet with them.

[BEAT]

So every smudge has a price tag. The clever headline nobody understood — that's a customer. The "About" page full of big words — that's a customer. The reel that looked stunning and explained nothing — that's a customer. You didn't lose them because your work is bad. You lost them because they couldn't tell what your work *was.*

And here's the part that really gets me. You probably paid *more* to lose them. Better camera. Nicer designer. Fancier words. You invested in the glare. Meanwhile the business down the street with the ugly website and one clear sentence is getting the call. Not because they're better than you. Because they're *understood,* and you're admired. And admiration doesn't pay invoices. Understanding does.

## CHAPTER 5: THE HONEST OBJECTION

Now there's a fair argument against everything I just said. And I'm going to make it properly, because if I skip it, you should stop trusting me.

You could say: "Jevoy, come on. If I strip everything down to plain words, I'll sound like everybody else. I'll be boring. The whole point of branding is to stand out. Clean is forgettable."

And honestly? Part of that is true. There's even research on it. In 2011, a team found that students remembered material *better* when it was printed in an ugly, hard-to-read font. The little bit of friction made it stick.
[SOURCE CARD: Diemand-Yauman, Oppenheimer & Vaughan — disfluency and memory, 2011.]

So difficulty isn't always bad. A little friction can actually help people remember. That's real.

But here's where the argument breaks. That friction pays off *inside* — after someone already trusts you, already chose to pay attention. It does not pay off at the front door. At the door, confusion isn't intriguing. It's just a closed door.

So clarity and creativity were never enemies. They have an order. Clarity is the lens. Creativity is the light. You get them in first with a clean lens. *Then* you can get artful, weird, memorable — because now they're standing inside, and the friction lands as depth instead of as a barrier. Clear gets them in the door. Clever keeps them in the room.

---

## CHAPTER 6: THE FLIP

So here's the flip.

You don't have a creativity problem. You have a clarity problem. You've been buying better lenses — slicker video, prettier design, cleverer captions — when the thing blocking the sale was a smudge on the message the whole time.

And the scary part is the same as the studies. The fog doesn't just make you forgettable. It makes you *less trusted.* People feel the work it takes to understand you, and they read that work as risk. You spent the budget. You looked great. And nobody trusted you enough to call.

[PAUSE]

Real trust — the kind that makes someone choose you over the cheaper option — was never built by looking impressive. It's built by being understood. By one clear sentence a stranger gets in a second and repeats to a friend. A clean lens is the most generous thing a business can offer. It says: I care more about you getting it than about me looking smart.

And here's the quiet upside. The moment you get clear, you stop competing on the thing everyone else is exhausting themselves on. Everybody's in an arms race to look more polished. Better cameras, slicker edits, fancier words. It never ends, and the bar keeps moving. But almost nobody is competing on being *understood.* That lane is wide open. You can stop trying to out-pretty people who out-spend you, and just be the one business in the feed that actually makes sense. In a world this confusing, the clearest voice wins — and clear is cheaper than fancy.

The fog holds the compliments. Only clarity holds the customer.

## CHAPTER 7: WHAT PALMER HOUSE ACTUALLY DOES

This is where Palmer House Productions comes in. And I'll be straight about where we are. Young company, building in the open, out here in the Pacific Northwest. Which is the whole point. We'd rather wipe one lens clean than build ten beautiful, foggy ones.

Palmer House is a translation company that happens to own cameras. We don't help you look more impressive. We help you get *understood* — and then we make it beautiful, in that order.

Here's how it actually works, plainly. We don't start with "what should we film." That's buying a lens. We start with one question: what problem do you solve, and who for? Then we wipe the glass. We find the plain, true sentence under all the jargon — the one you stopped saying because it felt too simple. Usually that sentence is the whole business, and you'd buried it.

And we build it to do three jobs, in plain terms. One — clear enough that a tired stranger gets it in one second, so the lens is wiped. Two — true enough that they trust it, because easy-to-understand reads as honest. And three — beautiful enough that it sticks, with the creativity saved for *inside*, where friction turns into depth instead of a closed door.

And the order is everything. We always wipe the lens before we touch the lighting. Most production companies do it backwards. They make it gorgeous first, then try to bolt clarity on at the end, and it never quite fits. We refuse to shoot a single frame until the plain sentence is locked. Because a stunning video built on a foggy message isn't an asset. It's an expensive way to confuse people in higher resolution.

Let me show you what that looks like, fast. Say you're a financial advisor. The fog move is a slick reel about "wealth paradigms," and people scroll past. The clean move is one short film where you say, plainly, "I help families stop being afraid of money." Same business. One is glare. The other one, a stranger watches and quietly thinks, *that's who I want.* That's the call you get eight months later.

Or say you run an accounting firm. "Boring," right? The fog version is a post about "integrated financial compliance solutions." Nobody finishes reading it. The clean version is one line: "We make sure you never get a scary letter from the IRS." Same firm. One is a smudge. The other one, a stressed business owner reads it at 11 p.m. and exhales — because for the first time, they understood exactly what you take off their plate. That exhale is the whole sale. Every business has a sentence that makes someone exhale. Most have it buried under ten that make people squint.

Here's our one rule, and it's the same line I'll say to your face on a call. If you can't tell me what you do in one plain sentence, we don't start filming. We start with the rag. Because a gorgeous video pointed through a foggy message is just expensive glare. We're for the business that actually wants to be *understood.*

And honestly, that first conversation is usually the most valuable part. Most owners have never once been forced to say the plain version of what they do. They've said the impressive version a hundred times. So when we sit down and I just keep asking "okay, but what does that actually mean for the customer" — something cracks open. The real sentence falls out. And they sit back, a little stunned, going "huh, I've never said it like that." That moment, before we've shot a single frame, is the lens getting wiped. Everything we build after that is just pointing good light through clean glass.

## CHAPTER 8: THE INVITATION  ·  [CTA]

So here's where I'll leave it.

Somewhere on your website is proof you worked hard — beautiful, and foggy, and quietly costing you customers. That's not a failure. It just means the job was never a better lens. It was a clean one.

There's an old line about a vision of the highest stakes imaginable. And the instruction attached to it wasn't "make it grand." It was "make it plain — so the one who reads it can run." Plain, so they can move on it. Even the biggest thing anyone ever had to say came with that same note: don't make them squint. That's not dumbing it down. That's love for the runner.
