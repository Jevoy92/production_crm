# MINDYOURBIZNIZ — "The Clean Lens: Why Clarity Is More Powerful Than Creativity"
## Solo Podcast Monologue · ~15 min · Standalone

---

[COLD OPEN — room tone. A ceramic mug set down on wood. A breath.]

**JEVOY:**

You land on a company's page, and it's beautiful.

Cinematic video. Perfect fonts. A slow-motion shot of someone looking thoughtfully out a window. A caption that sounds like poetry. You can feel how much work went into it — the sweat, the agonizing over the exact right transition, the obsessing over the color grade. It's clever. It's gorgeous.

And you scroll. Thirty seconds. A minute. Two full minutes. And then you notice something a little terrifying: you have no idea what they actually do.

You don't know what problem they solve. You don't know who it's for. You don't know how to hire them, or why you'd ever want to. They poured everything they had into being *admired* — and forgot to be *understood.*

[BEAT]

Here's the line this whole episode comes down to, so let me just put it on the table now: clever gets admired. Clear gets acted on. And almost everybody building something right now is dumping all their energy into the first one while quietly starving the second.

[MUSIC FADES IN — a slow, warm, steady pad. Swells gently, then out.]

---

And I get it. I really do — I've fallen into this exact trap myself, so I know the texture of it. When you care about your work, you want it to look beautiful. You want it cinematic. You want people to look at what you built and think, "wow, that's genius." That craving is human.

But we're living in a moment where the bar for *looking* good has never been lower. Anybody can grab a template. Anybody can have AI spin up a poetic caption. Anybody can drop a trending sound under a slow-motion clip. So the whole internet is flooded with beautiful, confusing noise — everyone out-cleverng each other into a blur. And in the middle of all that, the single most radical, disruptive thing you can do is be almost shockingly, unapologetically *clear.*

---

Let me show you why this works, because it's not just a style preference — it's wired into the brain.

There's a thing psychologists study called processing fluency. Plain version: it's just how *easy* something is for your brain to take in. And the research is wild and consistent — when something is easy to understand, we don't only process it faster. We actually judge it as more *true.* More trustworthy. More valuable. Let me say that again, because the whole episode hangs on it: we mistake "easy to understand" for "true and valuable." Ease feels like honesty. Effort feels like risk.

So when you make people work hard to figure out what you're saying, you're not just tiring them out. You're quietly draining their trust. If they have to burn real mental calories decoding your clever metaphor, a little alarm goes off underneath: "this is hard... this might be risky... let's move on." And they move on. Not because your thing was bad — because it was *foggy.*

And here's what makes that so expensive: a confused person almost never tells you they're confused. They don't raise a hand and ask, "wait — what exactly do you do?" That's awkward, and they don't care enough yet to push through the awkwardness. So they just... leave. Quietly. No angry email, no feedback, nothing. Confusion doesn't argue with you. It doesn't even frown. It just closes the tab. Which means you never find out that the reason they didn't buy had nothing to do with your price or your product — it was one sentence they couldn't decode in the three seconds they were willing to give you.

And there's a second force underneath this, and it's the reason you and I keep doing it to ourselves. Psychologists call it the curse of knowledge. Once you know something deeply, you literally cannot imagine not knowing it. You know your business in your sleep. You know the jargon, the nuance, the whole philosophy behind why you do it the way you do. So when you sit down to write the post or build the page, your brain skips right over the basic stuff — because to *you* it feels obvious. Boring, even.

You think: "I can't just say 'I help plumbers get more leads.' That's too basic. I need to say 'I architect paradigm-shifting growth ecosystems for home-service professionals.'"

Come on now. We've all done it. We dress our value up in a tuxedo of big words because we're scared the naked truth of what we do isn't impressive enough on its own. But your customer isn't inside your head. They're standing outside a locked door. And instead of handing them the key, you're describing the metallurgical composition of the lock. Beautiful description. Door's still locked.

---

Here's the picture I want you to hold for the rest of this.

Imagine I'm holding up a thick pane of glass — but it's completely fogged over. Condensation, a little grime. Hold it to your face and you can make out shapes, light, shadow, but no detail. You can't read the sign on the other side. You can't see the path.

Now imagine I take a gorgeous, ornate, gold-leaf frame and wrap it around that fogged glass. I carve intricate designs into the wood, set little jewels in the corners. I spend hours making that frame an absolute masterpiece.

Does that help you see through the glass? Not even a little. It just gives you something pretty to stare at while you stay completely blind to what's on the other side.

That's exactly what we do when we put creativity ahead of clarity. We build a beautiful frame around a foggy message. We sweat the lighting, the angles, the fonts, the wordplay — and the lens itself is still dirty.

Now picture me taking a plain, ugly rag. No gold. No jewels. Just a rag. And I wipe the glass clean. Suddenly the fog is gone, the grime is gone, and you can see straight through. The path. The sign. All of it.

Creativity gets you attention. Clarity gives people *sight.*

Think of it like food. I love a great meal — I love when a chef knows exactly how to use spice to lift a dish. But creativity is the seasoning. Clarity is the meal. And nobody, no matter how hungry, wants to sit down and be served a plate of pure paprika. Give them the actual food first. Tell them plainly what they're eating, *then* earn the right to talk about the subtle notes of truffle oil.

---

Now, I've got a friend in my head who pushes back hard right here — maybe you've got the same one.

He says: "But creativity matters. Art matters. If I strip everything down to plain, clear, functional language, I'm going to sound like everybody else. I'm going to be boring. I'm going to lose my soul to marketing."

And I want to give that real respect, because it's a real fear — and honestly, it's mine too. I run a production company. I care deeply about beauty, about story, about the emotional punch of a great image. I am not up here telling you art is dead and we should all talk in bullet points.

The friend is right about one thing: a world with no creativity is sterile and grim, and if you sand off all your personality and flavor, you'll blend into the wallpaper and become a commodity.

But here's where he's wrong. He's assuming clarity and creativity are enemies — like they're on opposite ends of a seesaw, and to raise one you have to drop the other. That's a false choice. They're not enemies. They're partners — with a strict order of operations. Creativity is supposed to *serve* understanding, not replace it.

When you use creativity to *hide* your message — big words to cover that you're not sure what you're saying, flashy visuals to distract from a thin product — that's not art. That's camouflage. But true creativity, the kind that actually moves people? It *illuminates.* It's the metaphor that makes a hard idea suddenly click. It's the image that nails a feeling someone couldn't put into words. You don't have to choose between clear and creative. But you do have to decide which one is the foundation and which one is the house — because if you build a house of pure creativity with no foundation of clarity under it, it falls down the second the wind picks up.

---

So how do we flip it?

It starts with what you're secretly trying to do every time you communicate. For most of us, underneath, the goal is: "I want them to think I'm smart. I want them to be impressed by me." That's an ego goal. And ego is the enemy of clarity.

Flip the goal. The new one isn't "I want them impressed by me." It's "I want them to feel understood — and to understand exactly how I can help them." The second you make that switch, everything changes. You stop trying to sound like a philosopher and you start sounding like a guide. You drop the jargon and start using the actual words your customer uses when they're stressed about this problem at two in the morning.

Real example. Say you're a financial advisor. The clever, ego-driven version is: "I orchestrate bespoke wealth-accumulation paradigms for high-net-worth visionaries." What does that even mean? I'm scrolling past it — my brain can't decode it fast, so I don't trust it. The clear version: "I help families build wealth so they don't have to worry about money when they retire." Is it poetic? No. Will it win a literary prize? No. But it's a clean lens. I look right through it and see exactly what's on the other side. And *now* — once I know you can help me retire without fear — now you've earned the right to use all your creativity to paint me a beautiful picture of what that retirement feels like.

And this works in literally any field. Say you're a brand designer. The ego version: "I craft holistic visual identities that unlock brand resonance." The human version: "I make your business look like one people can trust." Say you're a therapist. Ego version: "I facilitate transformational journeys toward integrated selfhood." Human version: "I help you stop feeling stuck." Same work, both times. But one makes the reader squint, and the other makes them exhale and lean in. And that's the whole tell — squinting is friction, and friction is distrust. Exhaling is relief, and relief is the first inch of trust. Every foggy sentence asks the reader to squint. Every clean one lets them exhale.

Clarity is the hook. Creativity is the journey. Lead with the clean lens, and you actually build a safer container for your art — because once people know they're in good hands, they relax, they trust you, and *then* they'll follow you into the deeper, weirder, more creative rooms of your work.

---

[MUSIC FADES IN — a subtle, warm drone, barely there.]

And let me talk to the exhaustion for a second, because I know it's there.

If you've been running on the treadmill of trying to be the cleverest, most innovative, most cinematic brand in your space — you're tired. Of course you are. It's an exhausting game, because the bar never stops moving. There's always someone with a better camera. A sharper wit. A newer trend you're "supposed" to master by Friday. And that pressure to perform at genius level every single time is exactly what freezes you — you sit down to make something, decide it's not a masterpiece, and just... leave it in the drafts. Say nothing.

So here's your massive sigh of relief: you do not have to be a genius every time you open your mouth. You don't have to reinvent the wheel every post. You just have to be a *translator.* Take the value locked inside your head, or locked inside your business, and translate it into language the person who needs it can instantly understand. That's the whole job.

And notice what that does to your output, practically — because this is the part that gives you your life back. When the job is "be a translator" instead of "be a genius," you can actually make things again. That post that took you four days because every line had to be brilliant? It takes twenty minutes now, because the bar moved from "is this a masterpiece?" to "is this clear?" — and "clear" is a bar you can actually clear, today, on an ordinary Tuesday, with an ordinary idea. That's not lowering your standard. That's finally having a standard you can hit often enough to build something with. Genius is rare and unrepeatable. Clear is available to you every single day.

And there's a real humility in that. Clarity asks you to step out of the spotlight and put their understanding above your need to look smart. To say, "I'm willing to use simple words — even if it makes me look a little less sophisticated — because I care more about helping you than impressing you." And here's the beautiful irony: the moment you stop straining to look smart, people actually start trusting your authority. You stop being one more noisy, confusing brand in the feed and you become a place of *rest.* In a world that confuses them all day, you become the one person who makes sense. And people always come back to the place that makes sense.

[MUSIC settles back down.]

---

[Room tone returns.]

So if you're sitting there realizing you've been serving plates of paprika — that you've been building gorgeous gold frames around fogged-up glass — it's okay. We've all done it. Today's just the day you pick up the rag.

And here's the test to carry with you, because it cuts through everything: the real measure of anything you write was never whether it gets *admired.* It's whether the person reading it can *move* on it. Can they get it at a glance and go? Can they read your sentence and immediately know what to do next? That's it. If what you do actually matters, make it plain enough that someone can pick it up and run with it. That's not dumbing it down. That's respect for the person on the other side of the glass.

[PAUSE]

So here's what I'll leave you with — with you, not at you.

This week, wipe one lens. Pick one thing — your bio, your homepage's first line, the way you introduce yourself when someone asks what you do — and strip it to the studs. Find the simplest, truest sentence about what you actually do, the one a tired stranger could understand in one read, and put that first. Then come down to the comments and give me both: the foggy version you've been using, and the clean one you just wiped down to. That's the comment I'm waiting on, and I'll be down there reading them, wiping my own lenses right alongside you.

And the share — you know someone who's brilliant and completely impossible to understand. Someone serving paprika and wondering why nobody's eating. Send them this. Tell them their work isn't the problem. The fog on the glass is.

Put down the gold frame. Pick up the rag. Let people see straight through to the thing you actually do.

[ROOM TONE — the mug lifts. Out.]
