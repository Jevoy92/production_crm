[COLD OPEN]
[ROOM TONE — close, quiet. The mug set down on wood. A breath.]

**JEVOY:**

I want to ask you something today, and I want you to answer it slowly, because the fast answer and the true answer are usually different people.

If something went badly wrong in your life this week — really wrong — how long would it take before somebody *noticed?*

[BEAT]

Not how long before you told someone. That's a different question, and we covered our editing habits a few episodes back. I mean noticed. Unprompted. Who is tracking you closely enough — your moods, your rhythms, your too-long silences — that your absence would register like a missed heartbeat?

For some of you, a name came instantly, and I'm glad, and this episode is going to tell you why that name is doing more for your health than your gym membership. And for some of you... the question opened a quiet little trapdoor. Especially the builders. Especially the strong ones. Because here's the thing about running something — a business, a family, a ministry, a team: you become the one who *does* the noticing. Everyone's weight rests on your attention. And almost nobody is assigned to you.

[BEAT]

Now — this is not the "loneliness epidemic" episode you've heard before, the one with the headlines and the hand-wringing and "put down your phone." Come on now. We're going to do what we do here. There's a study where holding one hand changed what fear looked like inside a brain scanner. There's a meta-analysis — hundreds of thousands of people — that found weak social connection rivals *smoking* as a predictor of early death. And there's a man under a boulder in Utah whose story this whole week sits inside. Let's get into it.

---

## CHAPTER 2: THE MECHANISM

I want to tell you about Aron Ralston — the climber who spent 127 hours pinned in a slot canyon, and who spent his presumed-final days recording video messages to his family. And the question I keep pulling on is *why that helped.* Why does speaking to people who love you — people who aren't even there — change what a body can endure?
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Aron Ralston — 'Between a Rock and a Hard Place' (2004); Blue John Canyon, April 2003.]

The answer starts with a neuroscientist named James Coan. 2006. He put married women in a brain scanner under threat of mild electric shock and watched their fear circuitry light up. Then their husbands reached in and held their hands.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Coan, Schaefer & Davidson — 'Lending a Hand,' Psychological Science, 2006.]

And the fear activity went *down.* Measurably. Not their bravery — the actual neural cost of the threat. A stranger's hand helped some. The husband's hand helped most — and the better the marriage, the bigger the effect.

[PAUSE]

Coan built a theory out of this that I think about constantly. He called it **social baseline theory**, and the claim is this: the human brain *budgets* for other people. Like calories. Like oxygen. Your nervous system was built assuming people would be nearby — that's the baseline state, the one all the energy math runs on. Connection isn't a luxury add-on to a self-sufficient machine. The machine was *designed around* the connection.

Which flips the whole picture of what loneliness is, you feel me? Loneliness isn't an emotion. It's a *deficit state.* When you're alone — truly unheld in anyone's mind — your brain doesn't just feel sad. It re-budgets. Every threat costs more. Every hill is steeper — and I mean that literally: there's a study, Schnall and colleagues, 2008, where people standing alone judged a hill physically steeper than people standing next to a friend. The world is *rendered harder* by the absence of your people. That tired you can't sleep off? Some of it might not be your workload, bro. Some of it is running a life in solo mode that was engineered for tandem.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Schnall et al. — hill-slant perception, J. Exp. Social Psychology, 2008.]

And the stakes aren't poetic. 2010 — Julianne Holt-Lunstad's meta-analysis, three hundred thousand-plus participants across 148 studies: people with strong social relationships had roughly *fifty percent higher odds of survival* over the study periods. The comparison that made headlines around the world: weak social connection is in the same risk neighborhood as smoking up to fifteen cigarettes a day. As a *mortality* factor. Loneliness isn't a mood. It's exposure.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Holt-Lunstad, Smith & Layton — 'Social Relationships and Mortality Risk,' PLoS Medicine, 2010.]

---

## CHAPTER 3: THE IMAGE YOU CAN HOLD IN YOUR MIND

No props on a podcast. Just hold this picture with me.

Two climbers go into two canyons.

The first one — nobody knows where he is. That was Ralston's actual situation, and he says it plainly in his book: the foundational mistake wasn't the boulder. It was that he told no one. No note, no text. So when the boulder came, there was no version of rescue coming from the outside. Whatever happened next, he was the only resource in the equation.

The second climber is in the *same canyon* with the *same boulder* — but three people know the route, and one of them expects a call by sundown. Nothing about the rock changed, you understand? The physics are identical. But everything about the *experience* is different — the math of hope, the pacing of effort, what the body is willing to spend — because somewhere out there, a search has a start time. He is held in minds. The holding does work *at a distance.*

[PAUSE]

Now here's why I'm telling you this. Most of the builders I know — most of *us* — are climber number one *by choice.* Not in the mountains. In life. We go into our canyons — the cash crunch, the doubt, the marriage strain, the slow-failing thing we're not ready to admit is failing — and we tell no one the route. Not because nobody would care. Because we're the strong one. Because everybody already brings their boulders to *us.*

The question from the cold open, again: who would notice? Climber one and climber two aren't two kinds of people. They're two *decisions.* And you make the decision before the boulder, or the boulder makes it for you.

## CHAPTER 4: THE FRIEND WHO PUSHES BACK

And here's my friend, right on time, and today my friend sounds like every entrepreneur I've ever had coffee with:

"Jevoy, I hear you, but be real about the builder's life. The isolation isn't a *bug* — it's the price of the seat. I can't process my fears with my employees — that's not vulnerability, that's instability; they need me steady. I can't fully unload on my spouse — she's already carrying the family side, and half this stuff would just scare her. My old friends don't get the stakes; my new 'friends' might be networking. And honestly? Solitude *works* for me. Some of us recharge alone. The science even backs that — introverts exist. So this whole 'who holds you in mind' thing is sweet, but the seat I'm in doesn't come with that option. That's why they call it lonely at the top — it's a *description*, not a complaint."

[BEAT]

Every line of that deserves respect, so let me take it seriously. It's true: leadership requires a degree of containment — the captain doesn't get to panic out loud on deck, and that's not dishonesty, it's stewardship. It's true that solitude and loneliness are different animals — solitude is *chosen* aloneness that restores; the research never said being alone kills, it said being *unheld* does. You can be alone all week and deeply held. You can be surrounded all day and held by no one. The dose isn't people. The dose is *being known.*

But here's where the friend's math fails, and it fails on the friend's own terms — performance. Go back to Coan: the brain budgets threats based on available support. A leader running with zero hands in the scanner is paying *surcharge pricing on every single stressor* — every payroll, every hard call, every 2 AM what-if costs more, neurologically, than it costs the leader who is held. That's not softness economics. That's *capacity* economics. The contained captain still needs a harbor; containment without a harbor isn't strength, it's a slow leak with good posture. And "lonely at the top" — notice what that phrase actually is, bro. It's a *building code violation* repeated so often it became a job description. The top isn't inherently lonely. It's just where people stop assuming you need checking on. Which means the checking has to be *arranged.* Deliberately. Like everything else at the top.

---

## CHAPTER 5: THE FLIP

So here's the flip.

We treat being held in mind as something that happens *to* the lucky — a gift some people receive: warm families, old friends, good marriages. You either have your search party or you don't.

But look at what Ralston did in the canyon — the thing that made this whole week's investigation. He had no search party. Nobody knew where he was. So with a dying battery, he *built* one. He spoke to his family through a lens as if they were present — and his nervous system, which can't fully tell imagined witness from real, *answered.* Calm came. Resolve came, eventually, in the form of a vision of a son not yet born — a future witness, summoned from nothing, strong enough to power an impossible act.

Being held in mind isn't weather, you feel me? It's *architecture.* It can be built. Late, alone, in the dark, with bad tools — it can still be built. And if it can be built in a canyon at hour 100, it can be built in your life this week, at a fraction of the cost. The witness structure that saves climbers is the same one that holds founders, mothers, pastors, all of us: *somebody knows my route, and somebody expects my call.*

That's it. That's the whole load-bearing wall. Two sentences. Most of us have neither, and we call the draft coming through the walls "just how building feels."

## CHAPTER 6: THE EMOTIONAL PART

[MUSIC FADES IN — one low, warm pad. Under this chapter only.]

And I'll go first. Like always.

Building what I'm building — the company, this show, all of it — I've noticed I've become very good at a specific maneuver: being the noticer. I track my people. I check on the team, the family, the friends — I'm the "how are you *really* doing" guy. And I've slowly understood that being the noticer is, among other true things, a *brilliant hiding place.* Nobody audits the auditor. If I'm always the one asking, nobody gets far enough into my side of the conversation to ask back. The role is generous and real — and it is also armor, and I chose it partly *because* it's armor.

[BEAT]

And the cost shows up in a strange place. Not in crisis — I'm not in a canyon. It shows up in the *good* moments. Something wins — a project lands, a number comes in, a thing I prayed over actually happens — and there's this half-second where the joy goes looking for somewhere to land... and hovers. Because the people who'd celebrate don't know the stakes, and the people who know the stakes are the ones I manage steadiness for. So the win gets filed instead of *felt.* That's the quiet tax of being unheld, bro — it's not just that nobody catches your sorrows. It's that nobody catches your joys. Both bounce.

[PAUSE]

So the practice — present tense, in progress, same as every week: I'm building my two sentences. One person who knows my route — actually knows it, the real map with the real cliffs marked. And rhythms where somebody expects my call — not crisis calls, *scheduled* presence, because Coan's data says the hand has to be in the scanner *before* the shock to do its full work. Arranged holding. It felt unnatural for exactly three weeks. It feels like oxygen now. Which — according to the science — is precisely what it is.

[MUSIC FADES OUT — slow, under the next line.]

---

## CHAPTER 7: THE INVITATION

So here's the work this week. Two moves, both small, both buildable by Friday.

First: *drop a pin.* One person. Tell them where you actually are — not the trailer version, the route: "Here's what I'm carrying this season, here's the part that's heavier than it looks." You're not asking them to fix anything. You're filing a flight plan. Search parties can't look for someone they don't know is missing.

Second: *become someone's noticer — formally.* Pick one person you suspect is climber number one, and put a recurring fifteen minutes on the calendar with them. Not "let's catch up sometime." Scheduled. Expected. Because here's the beautiful mechanics of it: an *expected call* is a witness structure for both of you. The moment somebody expects your call, you are, by definition, held in mind. You can build for others the thing you're too proud to ask for, and it holds you anyway. That's not a loophole. I'm pretty sure that's the design.

[BEAT]

There's an ancient passage I kept returning to all week — one of the oldest cost-benefit analyses ever written, and it's about partnership: *two are better than one, because they get a good return for their work. If either of them falls down, one can help the other up. But pity anyone who falls with no one to help them up... Though one may be overpowered, two can defend themselves. And a cord of three strands is not quickly broken.*

If either of them falls. The oldest risk assessment in literature, and it lands exactly where the modern meta-analysis landed three thousand years later: the deadliest variable isn't the fall. It's falling *unheld.* And that cord of three strands — you, one real person, and whatever third strand you choose to braid in — that's the architecture. Not built in the canyon. Built before it, braided slowly, on ordinary Tuesdays, fifteen scheduled minutes at a time.

[PAUSE]

You were never supposed to be the only one holding the rope. Go braid the cord.

If that's the kind of conversation you've been needing — stay close. We're just getting started over here.

Thanks for being here. MindYourBizniz is a Palmer House conversation. New episode next week.

[ROOM TONE — the mug lifts. Out.]
