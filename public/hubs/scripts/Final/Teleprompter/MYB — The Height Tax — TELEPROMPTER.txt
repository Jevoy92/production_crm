First, the receipts. Because what makes height the perfect example isn't that the advantage is real. Everybody always suspected that. It's that scientists actually measured it. In dollars. With controls.

So. 2004. Two researchers gather up decades of workplace data. Timothy Judge and Daniel Cable. They control for age. They control for gender. They control for weight. And they ask one question. Does height, by itself, predict pay?

It does. And the number is one you hear once and never forget. About seven hundred and eighty-nine dollars per inch, per year.

Per inch. Think about that in a real life. Two people. Same talent. Same work ethic. Same field. One of them is six inches taller. So one of them collects an extra four or five grand a year. Every year. For decades.

There are people who got a raise for being tall every single year of their career. And never once knew it. Their height made more steady money than most people's savings. The inches never missed a quarter. The inches never had a bad year. The inches don't even know they're employed.

And it climbs all the way to the top. Malcolm Gladwell, researching his book Blink, surveyed the CEOs of the Fortune 500. The most fought-over chairs in American business. In the general population, about 14.5% of men are six feet or taller. Among those CEOs? Fifty-eight percent. One in seven on the street. Three in five in the corner office.

And nobody on any board ever wrote down "height" as a requirement. They didn't have to. The bias doesn't need a memo. It's wired in.

Here's the wiring. There's a name for it, and it's a hundred years old. In 1920, a psychologist named Edward Thorndike noticed something off in how army officers rated their soldiers. The men they rated as physically impressive also got rated smarter, more loyal, better character. One trait bleeding into all the others. Like the form only had one real question on it.

He called it the halo effect. Basically, your brain takes the one thing it can check in two seconds — tall, handsome, deep voice — and fills in everything it can't. Capable. Trustworthy. Leader. For most of human history, following the biggest one in the group was a decent survival bet. So the wiring stayed. And now it sits in boardrooms, handing out corner offices to altitude.

And before the tall guys get comfortable — it's not just you. The same machine pays out everywhere. People with easier-to-say names get judged more trustworthy and climb faster.
