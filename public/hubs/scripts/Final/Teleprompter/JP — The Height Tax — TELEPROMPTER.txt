There is an economy running underneath the regular economy, and nobody talks about it, because everybody who's winning in it didn't do anything to earn their position — and everybody who's losing in it can't point at anything to blame.
Here's the first number, and I need you to actually hear it. In 2004, two researchers — Timothy Judge and Daniel Cable — pulled together decades of data on height and income, controlling for age, controlling for gender, controlling for weight. And what fell out of the data is one of the cleanest, most uncomfortable findings in all of workplace research: every inch of height is associated with roughly eight hundred dollars a year in additional earnings.
Per inch. Per year. Compounding over a career.
So a person who's six feet tall, standing next to an equally talented person who's five-foot-six, is statistically collecting something like an extra five thousand dollars a year — for the inches. Not for skills. Not for output. For altitude. There is a salary line item that nobody signed off on, no HR department will acknowledge, and no performance review has ever mentioned, and it's been getting paid out, quietly, forever.
And here's the thing — height isn't even the only one. It's just the most measurable one. There's a whole roster of advantages like this: things people can see in two seconds, that you did absolutely nothing to accomplish, that are right now adjusting how much strangers trust you, follow you, and pay you.
So today we're counting them down — five unearned advantages, ranked from "huh, that's weird" to "okay, that's genuinely outrageous" — and then I'm going to show you the hundred-year-old psychology that explains why our brains keep paying these premiums. And stay for the end, because the flip on this one matters: this is not actually a video about tall people. It's a video about an envelope with your name on it that I don't think you've opened.
Five entries. Counting down from the strange to the outrageous. Let's count.
We start with the one that sounds fake: your name has a price.
2012 — three researchers, Laham, Koval, and Alter, published a series of studies on what they called the name-pronunciation effect. The finding: people with names that are easier to pronounce are judged more positively — more likable, more trustworthy — than people with harder-to-pronounce names. Same résumé. Same qualifications. The mouth-feel of the name moves the judgment.
And before you file that under "cute lab finding" — they checked it in the real world. They looked at American law firms, and attorneys with easier-to-pronounce names occupied higher positions in the firm hierarchy. The effect held even within groups — so it's not just bias against foreign names; among easily-categorized names, the smoother ones still rose faster.
Think about how absurd that is for a second. Somewhere there is a brilliant attorney being quietly out-promoted by a colleague whose primary professional advantage is that he's named Tom. Nobody decided this. No partner ever wrote "name too crunchy" in a review. The brain just... finds easy things pleasant, and pleasant things trustworthy, and trustworthy things promotable. We'll come back to that chain — it's the engine of this whole list.
Number four: the bass in a man's voice has a market value, and somebody calculated it.
2013 — researchers Mayew, Parsons, and Venkatachalam analyzed the voices of hundreds of male CEOs, actual recorded earnings calls, and matched voice pitch against company data. The finding: CEOs with deeper voices ran larger companies and earned more — the study put real dollar figures on it, and the differences were not small. Lower frequency, higher salary.
Now — is there any evidence that vocal cord thickness improves capital allocation? There is not. No shareholder has ever been saved by a baritone. But the deep voice reads as command, command reads as competence, and competence gets the bigger company. The voice arrived in the room before the strategy did, and the room had already voted.
And if you're wondering whether you can hack this one — that's the famous story about Margaret Thatcher, who actually took voice training to lower her pitch for exactly this reason. The most powerful woman of her era looked at this bias and decided it was cheaper to change her voice than to change everyone else's brains. She wasn't wrong. That's what makes this list so uncomfortable.
Number three you already suspected, but the receipts are older and bigger than you think.
1994 — economists Daniel Hamermesh and Jeff Biddle published the foundational study on what's now called the beauty premium: across professions — not modeling, not acting, regular jobs — people rated as more attractive earned meaningfully more than average-looking people, and people rated below average earned less. They called that second part the "plainness penalty," which is the most brutally named finding in economics.
And the effect shows up in places that should be immune. Attractive defendants tend to receive lighter sentences — there's a whole research literature on it. Attractive students get graded more generously. The premium follows people into rooms where, by every rule we claim to believe in, it should have zero jurisdiction. The face gets in the door before the case does.
And before anybody blames Instagram for this — psychologist Judith Langlois ran studies showing that infants do it. Babies, months old, who have never seen a magazine, never watched a commercial, never absorbed a single beauty standard — they gaze longer at faces adults rate as attractive. Months old! The bias isn't downstream of culture. Culture is downstream of the bias. We didn't learn this one. We shipped with it.
By the way — notice what these first three have in common. Name, voice, face. All visible or audible in the first two seconds. None of them chosen. All of them billing.
Number two: the Judge and Cable number from the open — the eight hundred dollars an inch — and now let me tell you why it deserves its own slot instead of just being the hook.
Because the researchers didn't stop at whether height paid. They mapped how. Height predicted how leader-like people were perceived to be, how much respect they were granted, how their performance was rated — the perception side of every ledger. And here's the sentence I want you to hold, because it's the most important sentence in this video: the premium shows up in the pay and in the perception. Nobody has ever managed to show it in the performance. They measured the paycheck. The results column never signed off.
The tall person isn't doing anything wrong, by the way — most have no idea the premium exists. That's what makes it eerie. It's not a scheme. It's weather. Invisible, ambient, settling on some people and not others, eight hundred dollars at a time. And run the career math on that weather: six extra inches, forty working years — that's somewhere in the neighborhood of two hundred thousand dollars of altitude income, before compounding. A house. For posture they were born with. If a bank did this, there would be hearings.
And number one — the stat that turns this from "interesting" to "how is nobody talking about this."
In the research for his book Blink, Malcolm Gladwell surveyed the CEOs of the Fortune 500 — the most competed-for jobs in American business, the ones with search committees and consultants and years of vetting. In the general American male population, about 14.5% of men are six feet or taller.
Among Fortune 500 CEOs? About 58%.
Six feet and up is one-in-seven in the country and three-in-five in the corner office. And above six-foot-two it gets crazier — under four percent of American men, about a third of those CEOs. Nobody on any search committee ever said "find me a tall one." They said "find me a leader" — and then their hundred-millisecond brains went looking for someone they had to look up at, because for three hundred thousand years, the big one usually was the safest bet to follow. The committee thought it was assessing strategy. Its wiring was assessing silhouettes.
And this is ancient. The oldest leadership search on record — it's in the book of First Samuel — Israel demands a king, and the text introduces the winning candidate with his qualifications, and listen to what they are: Saul, "as handsome a young man as could be found anywhere in Israel, and he was a head taller than anyone else." That's the résumé. Handsome, plus a head taller. They crowned him. It went badly — famously badly — and the do-over king was a shepherd kid whose own father didn't bother calling him in from the field for the audition. We have been hiring by the inch for three thousand years, and the first recorded case was already a cautionary tale.
So what's underneath all five? One mechanism, a hundred years old.
1920 — psychologist Edward Thorndike is reviewing how military officers rate their soldiers, and he finds the data is broken: officers who rated a man as physically impressive also rated him smarter, more loyal, better character — as if the rating sheet had one real question on it. He named it the halo effect: one visible positive trait bleeds into every judgment, like a master key. Tall unlocks "leader." Deep voice unlocks "competent." Pretty unlocks "innocent." Smooth name unlocks "trustworthy." The brain takes the one thing it can verify in two seconds and autofills the rest of the form.
But here's the crack in the engine, and it's your way out of despair on this. All these premiums are paid by perception. Every single study measured what people believed, granted, rated, paid. You know what none of them measured? Whether any of it was true. The tall CEOs were not shown to run companies better. The baritones did not out-perform the tenors. The halo is a confident, unanimous, ancient grading system — that has never once checked its own answers. Which means the unfairness is real, but the verdict isn't. The economy under the economy pays out on appearance. The actual game — building, creating, keeping promises, being worth trusting — still gets played above ground, where your inches were never the currency.
So here's the flip, and I told you at the top this wasn't really about tall people.
If the halo machine pays premiums on visible, unchosen, unearned traits... then run the obvious question: what's yours? Because you have one. Statistically, you have several. Everybody is standing on some patch of unearned high ground — the warm voice, the disarming laugh, the face people instinctively trust, the name that travels, the energy that makes rooms relax, the gift of being underestimated, which, ask anybody who has it, is a weapon. You didn't earn yours either. That's the point. Nobody earned their starting deck.
And here's what I notice about how people handle this. We are experts on the advantages we didn't get. We can quote the other person's inches from memory. Meanwhile there's an envelope with our own name on it — our own unearned premium — sitting unopened, because we're too busy auditing someone else's. The height tax is real, and being mad at it is fair, and you get about a day of that. Then the only move that pays is the inventory: where am I tall? What comes easy to me that's hard for everyone else — so easy I assume it doesn't count? That thing you're dismissing because you didn't sweat for it? That's not nothing. That's your eight hundred an inch. Open the envelope.
And if you're sitting there going "I genuinely don't have one" — respectfully, that's not humility, that's a measurement error, and we just spent ten minutes establishing that humans are terrible at measuring. The advantages on this countdown were all visible ones, which is exactly why scientists could study them. The invisible ones — the voice people calm down to, the memory for names, the ability to sit in chaos without flinching, the trust you generate in the first two minutes — those don't show up in salary regressions because nobody's figured out how to measure them yet. That doesn't make them smaller than inches. It makes them unpriced. And unpriced assets, any investor will tell you, are where the real opportunity lives.
Three moves before you go.
One: take the inventory. This week, ask two or three people who know you: "What do I make look easy?" Their answers will embarrass you — that's how you'll know they're right. The unearned advantage is invisible from the inside, always, because from the inside it just feels like normal. Nobody experiences their own tailwind. You only ever feel the headwinds — that's the physics of being a person — which is exactly why the inventory needs outside eyes.
Two: stop apologizing for your high ground. Using an advantage you didn't earn isn't cheating — it's stewardship. The tall guy isn't wrong for standing up straight. You're not wrong for having the voice, the warmth, the whatever-it-is. The waste isn't having it. The waste is leaving it in the envelope out of some weird guilt that nobody asked for.
Three: audit your own halo. You're not just collecting these premiums — you're paying them, every time you hire, vote, trust, or follow. So when you feel that instant "this person's got it" — pause one beat and ask what the evidence actually is. If the honest answer is "they're tall and their voice is nice"... congratulations, you just caught a three-thousand-year-old bug in the act. The people who catch it find the shepherd kids everyone else left in the field.
And here's the thing that humbles me about all of this — people have known about this bias for thousands of years. Ancient writers complained about it: the rich man in the fine clothes gets the good seat, the poor man in the rough clothes gets waved to the back, and everybody in the room thinks they're being fair while they do it. Same gold-ring reflex the Fortune 500 search committee runs. We've been catching ourselves at this, and writing it down, and warning the next generation about it, for as long as we've had writing — and we still do it, every single day, because the noticing is wired in. You can't stop the noticing. The whole assignment is one move long: don't let the noticing be the verdict. See the ring — then look for the person. Every system in this video runs on autofill.
