There's a researcher named Linda Henkel — stay in the auditorium, I'll keep this quiet — but she explains what's happening in your hand right now.
2014. She walks a group of students through an art museum. Tells them: some of these, just look at. Others, look at and photograph. The next day she asks them what they remember.
The photographed ones were gone. Fainter. Fewer details. The students remembered less about the art they'd captured than the art they'd simply stood in front of. She called it the photo-taking impairment effect — and her explanation is the part I can't put down.
She said the moment the shutter clicks, something in the brain quietly lets go. The machine has it. I don't have to carry this. It's called cognitive offloading. You didn't keep the moment and capture it. You captured it instead of keeping it. The photo was never a backup. Half the time, the photo is the thing that replaces the memory it was supposed to protect.
