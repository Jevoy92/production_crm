## PHASE 0 — THE TOPIC BRIEF

**TOPIC:** People form a verdict about you in a fraction of a second — before you've said a word — and more exposure makes them *more confident*, not more accurate.

**WHY IT'S INTERESTING:** Everyone has felt both sides of this — being sized up, and sizing up. The science is shocking in its specifics (elections predicted from faces in one second), it touches every meeting, date, interview, and video any of us will ever make — and it has one of the best wonder-flips in the whole catalog: the verdict is real, but two surprising things are also true: you get to *author* the first frame, and the courtroom is *kinder* than you assume.

**THE EVIDENCE (verified):**
- **🏆 HERO — Todorov, Mandisodza, Goren & Hall (2005, *Science*):** people shown pairs of unfamiliar political candidates' faces for ~1 second judged who looked more competent — and those snap judgments predicted roughly **70% of U.S. Senate race outcomes.** Strangers' split-second face-reads forecast actual elections. The single wildest finding in the catalog.
- **Willis & Todorov (2006, Princeton):** trust/competence/likability judgments form from a face in **100 milliseconds** — and longer exposure *raises confidence in the verdict without changing it.* The jury decides instantly, then spends the rest of the time getting surer.
- **Ambady & Rosenthal (1993), thin slices:** students shown silent clips of teachers — 30 seconds, then cut down to *two seconds* — predicted end-of-semester evaluations with unsettling accuracy. Two seconds, no sound.
- **The halo effect (Thorndike, 1920):** one positive trait (warmth, attractiveness, confidence) bleeds into every other judgment — competence, honesty, intelligence. The first impression isn't one verdict; it's a master key that opens all the other verdicts.
- **Boothby et al. (2018), the liking gap:** after real conversations, people systematically *underestimate* how much the other person liked them. The fast verdict exists — and it's reliably *warmer than you think.* (The redemption evidence — load-bearing for the flip.)

**REAL-WORLD EXAMPLES (named, verifiable):**
- **Susan Boyle, Britain's Got Talent, April 2009** — the most-watched snap-judgment reversal in history. An entire theater (and Simon Cowell's face) renders the verdict in seconds; the first note overturns it. Proof the verdict forms fast AND can be flipped by strong evidence — both halves of the science in one famous clip.
- The 2005 election-prediction study itself (tells like a heist story).
- Speed dating — the entire industry is built on the premise that minutes are enough; research backs that decisions form almost immediately.
- Job interviews — the well-documented pattern of interviewers deciding early and spending the remaining time confirming.
- Everyday: the way everyone "reads" a restaurant from the doorway, a contractor from the truck, a video from its first three seconds.

**THE THREE ANGLES:**
- **JEVOY PALMER (mechanism):** The investigation: how fast the verdict actually is (100ms), how deep it runs (elections), why time makes it *stickier* not fairer (confidence-without-accuracy), and the halo's master-key trick. Wonder turn: the first frame is the most concentrated creative real estate you own — hospitality, not a sentence you await; and Susan Boyle proves strong evidence can overturn the court.
- **PALMER HOUSE (business cost/opportunity):** Your business is thin-sliced before the pitch deck loads — the silent first seconds of your video, website, walkthrough ARE the pitch. The gift: thin slices are *cheap to design.* Light, warmth, the first three seconds of a real smile — tiny authored choices, outsized verdicts. First-impression design as the highest-ROI work in marketing.
- **MINDYOURBIZNIZ (inner life):** What it does to a person to know they're constantly, instantly assessed — the exhaustion of perpetual evaluation, the armor we build. The flip: put the fear itself on trial. Prosecution: the verdict is fast and real. Defense: the liking gap — the actual verdicts are consistently kinder than the imagined ones; and you yourself are a generous judge a hundred times a day without noticing. Verdict: the courtroom is real, and it's friendlier than the defendant believes.
