## PART 1: THE TWELVE LAWS

### Law 1 — Open on the viewer's life, not your topic.
Harris doesn't open with neuroscience. He opens with *your* 3PM slump. Leila doesn't open with confidence theory. She opens with "What if I told you confidence has nothing to do with feeling ready?" The first sentence must describe something the viewer has personally felt within the last week. The topic is the vehicle; the viewer's lived experience is the hook.

### Law 2 — Kill the cliché in the first 30 seconds.
Harris: "This isn't a video where I'm gonna tell you to just get more sleep or stop looking at your phone." Ali: "The problem isn't how much sleep you're getting — it's the anxiety around your sleep." Naming the advice the viewer *expects* — and dismissing it — instantly signals this video is different. Every script needs an explicit "this is not that video" beat.

### Law 3 — Make a promise with a visible spine.
Veritasium states the roadmap outright: "This video is about how to build an atomic bomb, the life of Oppenheimer, and why scientists worried about setting fire to the atmosphere." Three promises, escalating, the last one wild. The hook should promise 2–3 specific things, and the most curiosity-loaded promise goes LAST.

### Law 4 — Investigate, don't lecture. ("Let me show you," never "let me tell you.")
Harris's whole register is "Here's what I want to investigate." He builds a list on camera as he learns. Veritasium walks through Oppenheimer's life as a mystery (why did the genius never win a Nobel?). The host is a *fellow detective*, one step ahead of the viewer, not a professor. Use discovery language: "I found this," "here's where it gets weird," "this is the part nobody talks about."

### Law 5 — One recurring device that accumulates.
Harris's list ("I'm gonna jot this down"). Veritasium's countdown to Trinity. Dan Martell's numbered ledger. The device gives the viewer a *progress bar* — a reason to stay, because the thing on screen is visibly incomplete. Every script needs one object or structure that grows as the video progresses and pays off at the end.

### Law 6 — Time-travel or scale-shift to create perspective.
Harris invents Johnny Caveman and runs him through 300,000 years, the industrial revolution, and 2024 — same brain, three worlds. Veritasium spans 1925 Cambridge to Hiroshima to the Tsar Bomba. The fastest way to make a familiar thing strange again is to move it across time or scale. Every investigation needs at least one "zoom out" act.

### Law 7 — Analogies carry the science; the science earns the analogy.
Harris explains glutamate buildup, *then* gives you "your brain is a city and decisions produce trash that blocks the streets." Rule of thumb from the masters: one concrete analogy per abstract mechanism, introduced AFTER the real mechanism is stated, never instead of it. Cite the real source on screen ("check my sources — look at this paper specifically"). Owning the science builds trust; renting it builds nothing.

### Law 8 — Give the skeptic real dignity before the flip.
Harris voices the objection himself: "Okay okay, I get it — but I'm not running from tigers anymore. Shouldn't I be LESS tired?" Ali debunks his own past behavior (the blue-light glasses he paid for). Leila mocks her own mirror affirmations. The strongest scripts let the counterargument land hard enough that the viewer nods — and *then* dismantle it. A strawman skeptic kills trust; a steelman skeptic builds it.

### Law 9 — Story is the proof. Numbers are the seasoning.
Leila's 24 Hour Fitness story (down to the $5,000 bank balance and the bathroom before the first sale) does more than any statistic. Hormozi's friend who posted for 6 months then broke through. Veritasium's poisoned apple. One *real, specific, sensory* story per script — with numbers inside it (her $1,400 rent; Harris's 35,000 daily decisions; Matt Gray's 13,800x compounding). Specific numbers make claims feel investigated rather than asserted.

### Law 10 — Re-hook every 2–3 minutes.
The masters never coast. Harris drops a song clip, a sponsor pivot, a new chemical, a QR code. Veritasium turns from biography to bomb mechanics to "could it ignite the atmosphere?" Each chapter must end with a small open loop ("but there's one more chemical we have to talk about...") and each new chapter must reframe the stakes. Watch time is won at the seams between sections.

### Law 11 — The flip: redefine the problem, then make the solution feel inevitable.
Every sample script pivots from "here's the phenomenon" to "here's what it means for you" at roughly 60–70% through. Leila: confidence is an output, not an input. Hormozi: make the action the goal, not the outcome. Harris: it's evolutionary mismatch, not personal failure. The flip must *absolve* the viewer (it's not your fault — it's the mechanism) and then *re-empower* them (now that you know the mechanism, here's the move).

### Law 12 — End with identity, not summary.
Dan Martell: "you'll be the type of person who can do something for a long period of time." Hormozi: "it was never the goal — so year one feels like year eight." Matt Gray: "icons aren't born, they're built." The close isn't a recap; it's a mirror. Tell the viewer who they become if they internalize this. Then one soft, specific CTA — never three.

## PART 2: HOOK FORMULAS (FIRST 30–60 SECONDS)

Six proven openings, with the creator who proved them:

1. **The Quiet Universal** (Johnny Harris): "Year after year, more of us are quietly asking the internet, why am I so tired?" → A felt experience, framed as a shared secret. Best for Jevoy Palmer channel.
2. **The Contrarian Swap** (Leila Hormozi): "What if I told you confidence has nothing to do with feeling ready and everything to do with doing it scared?" → Take the assumed cause, replace it with the real one, in one sentence.
3. **The Stat-Empathy Open** (Ali Abdaal): "Adults in the US feel tired three days a week... but the problem isn't your sleep — it's the anxiety *about* your sleep." → Number, then twist.
4. **The Impossible Biography** (Veritasium): "He might be the most important physicist ever. He never won a Nobel Prize." → Paradox in two sentences; demands resolution.
5. **The Time Machine** (Dan Martell): "I'm 44. If I could go back and talk to my 20-year-old self..." → Authority via hindsight; works only with earned experience (use carefully at launch stage).
6. **The Dedication** (Matt Gray): "To everyone who's ever been told they can't — this video is for you." → Names the tribe before the topic.

**Hook assembly rule (all three Palmer scripts):** Felt moment (Law 1) → cliché kill (Law 2) → spine promise (Law 3) → first open loop. Total: 45–60 seconds, 120–160 words.

## PART 4: LANGUAGE & DELIVERY RULES

- **Short declarative bursts after long builds.** Harris: "Make a bunch of decisions, you feel tired even if you didn't lift a finger." Earn the punch with a wind-up.
- **Second person dominates.** Count the "you"s in any Hormozi script — they outnumber "I" 3-to-1 except inside personal stories.
- **Name the thing memorably.** "The founder's freeze." "Evolutionary mismatch." "Coffee naps." A named concept is a shareable concept — and a clip title.
- **Numbers with texture, not round numbers.** 35,000 decisions. 374,142 bunkers. $5,000 down to three months of runway. 13,800x.
- **Self-aware humor as pressure release.** Harris singing "Day Man," Ali mocking his own £100 lens coating. One laugh per act keeps gravity from becoming heaviness.
- **The 1-to-12 rule (Jevoy's own system):** every chapter must contain at least one 30–60 second passage that stands alone as a vertical clip — a named concept, an analogy, or a flip line.
- **Launch-stage honesty (non-negotiable):** discovery framing only. "Here's what I found" — never "in my years of experience."

## PART 5: TITLE & PACKAGING RULES

- Browse beats search for this genre. Package for curiosity (Harris's lowercase "Why you're so tired"), not keywords.
- Winning constructions from the data: **"Why you ___"** (felt experience), **"What everyone gets wrong about ___"** (misconception), **"The psychology of ___"** (Leila's 5.2x breakout), **"___ is lying to you / is a lie"** (Harris's shampoo video).
- Title and thumbnail split the work: title names the tension, thumbnail shows the felt moment. Never repeat one in the other.
- The title is a promise — the video must over-deliver on it by minute two (Veritasium's #1 rule: clickable, never misleading).
