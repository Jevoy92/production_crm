It starts with a man in a canyon.
In 2003, a climber named Aron Ralston got pinned alone in a slot canyon in Utah. Boulder on his arm. Five days. And when he realized nobody knew where he was, he took out a camcorder and recorded messages to his family. He filmed the things he knew and loved, for the people who'd come after him. Call it the goodbye tape.
Your business has a goodbye tape waiting to be made. Right now. While everyone's still here.
In the 1980s, a psychologist named Daniel Wegner noticed something about long-married couples.
Neither one remembered everything. But together, they remembered far more than two people should. One held the birthdays and the social map. The other held the finances and how the furnace works. And here's the key part. Each one reliably remembered who remembered what. Ask the husband about the holiday plans, and he doesn't know — but he knows instantly that she does.
Wegner gave it a name. Transactive memory. Basically, a memory shared across people, where each person stores some of the knowledge and a directory of who holds the rest. It's why losing a long partner includes a specific kind of devastation. Half your memory was stored in another person.
Now look at your business. It works exactly the same way. Nobody knows everything. But everybody knows that pricing goes to Maria, the old code lives in Devon's head, and only Sam can calm the Henderson account down. The company "knows" an enormous amount. But huge chunks of that knowledge have exactly one copy. Stored in one skull. Walking around on two legs. Free to leave.
Engineers have a blunt name for this. They call it the bus factor. Basically — how many people would have to get hit by a bus before the project dies? For most small businesses, the honest answer, for whole parts of the company, is one.
And here's the founder's version. Run the audit on yourself. In most owner-led companies, the person holding the most one-of-a-kind knowledge, the real single point of failure, is the person watching this right now. Your business knows almost everything through you. Which means your business is Ralston at hour zero. Full of skill. And nobody knows where anything is.
Let's do the honest math on what amnesia costs. Because it hides in parts of the P&L that never say "knowledge loss."
Start with replacement. The standard HR research says replacing an employee runs from half to two times their salary, once you count recruiting, ramp-up, and lost output. For your most knowledgeable person, look at the high end. Because you're not replacing labor. Labor you can buy back in a job market. You're replacing memory. And memory isn't for sale at any price. The new hire shows up smart, skilled, and blank. The directory entry that said "Maria knows" now points at nothing.
Then count the slower invoices. The client who drifts away eight months later — not angry, just gone — because the person who knew them left, and being re-explained to a stranger feels like a demotion. The mistake your company already made in 2021, made again at full cost in 2027, because the lesson lived in someone's scar tissue and the scar tissue resigned. The quality drift nobody can name. The plating standard. The "that's not how we'd say it." A hundred small judgments that were never written down anywhere except in one person's taste.
Let me make one of those real, because it's easy to nod past. The client who drifts. She doesn't call to complain. She just stops calling. Six months later you notice the account went quiet, and you can't quite say why. Here's why. The person who knew her — who remembered her kid's name, who knew she panics about deadlines and needs an extra heads-up — that person left. Now she's working with someone who's perfectly competent and completely new to her. And being known, then suddenly not, feels like a downgrade she can't name. So she drifts. That's not a service failure. That's a memory failure. And it never shows up on a single line of your P&L.
And here's why "just write it down" has failed you every time. This is the part nobody says out loud. Wegner's research explains it. In a working system, writing it down feels pointless. Why document what Maria knows? Asking Maria is faster. It's richer. It's more current. The system really is more efficient than any wiki — right up until the moment it isn't. Knowledge transfer feels optional in exactly the season when it's still possible. It only feels urgent once it's already too late. That's not your discipline failing. That's the design flaw. The system runs perfectly until it loses one node. And it gives you no warning — because the node was the warning system.

There's real truth in that. The philosopher Michael Polanyi said it best — we know more than we can tell. The wiki graveyard is real. I've seen yours. And yes, retention is strategy number one. Keep Maria. Pay Maria. I will never sell you recording as a substitute for being a company worth staying at.
But the objection smuggles in two errors.
First. Retention has a 100% failure rate at the horizon. People retire. People get sick. Spouses get jobs in Denver. Every node leaves eventually. The only question is whether the memory transferred first. Loyalty changes the date on the amnesia. It doesn't cancel it.
Second. "You can't tape wisdom" is true for text. It's false for video. And that difference is the whole reason this video exists. Polanyi's point is that this kind of knowledge resists being told. But it doesn't resist being shown. You can't write down how Maria handles an angry client. But you can sit her in front of a camera and ask: "Walk me through the Henderson save. What did you see? What did you say? Why that order?" And twenty minutes of her face — her pauses, her emphasis, her war stories — will transfer more real judgment than fifty pages of process docs.
Apprenticeship has known this for ten thousand years. The deep stuff moves person to person, by showing and by story. The young carpenter didn't learn from a manual. He stood next to the old one and watched his hands for a decade. Video is just the first tool in history that lets the showing and the story scale past the room. The new hire who never met Maria can still watch Maria's hands. The wiki failed because text was the wrong container. You don't document a master. You film one.
And notice what that does to the fear of losing people. It doesn't go away — you should still fight to keep your best ones. But it stops being a cliff. Right now, every key person who leaves takes a piece of the company with them, permanently. Film them while they're here, and leaving becomes a transfer instead of an amputation. The person walks out the door. What they knew stays in the building.
