There's a thing your body does that you never had to learn, and you've never been able to stop.
You watch a video of yourself. And it's fine. It's fine, it's fine — and then it hits the worst part. The stumble. The weird laugh. The face your face made. And in the same instant something in you pulls your eyes away from the screen. And your hand starts moving toward delete before you even decided to.
You know this flinch. Everybody knows this flinch. It's not just on camera, either. Somebody falls hard on the sidewalk and you turn your head. A scene in a movie gets too real and you check your phone. There's a part — in the footage, in the room, in yourself — that your eyes refuse to stay on.
And it shows up in the small stuff too. Somebody snaps a candid photo of you mid-laugh, and you grab the phone to delete it before anyone sees — and that's the photo your mom would've framed. You leave a voice note, replay it once, hate it, record it again flatter and "safer." Hear yourself on a recording and physically wince. Same flinch, every time. The most human version of you shows up, and your first instinct is to make it disappear.
Now, this is not the video where I tell you we've all become fake, over-edited, over-filtered phonies. Come on now. You've heard that take a hundred times. And I think it's almost exactly backwards.
Because here's what I found when I actually dug into this. The flinch is real. The instinct to cut the worst part is real and it's ancient. But it's wrong about which part is the worst part. So here's where we're going. I'm going to show you a sixty-year-old experiment about a spilled cup of coffee that flips everything you think makes you look bad. I'm going to show you why you are the single worst judge of your own footage — there's a study with a name I love. And I want to answer a question I can't put down. The part you keep deleting — what if it's the best thing you've got?
Let's get into it.
Let's start in 1966, with one of my favorite experiments in all of psychology.
A researcher named Elliot Aronson — with Willerman and Floyd — had people listen to a recording of a quiz contestant. A smart one. The guy is getting almost everything right. Impressive. And in one version of the tape, right at the end, the contestant spills a cup of coffee all over himself. Small, human disaster.
Here's the finding. The smart guy who spilled the coffee was rated more likable than the same smart guy who didn't. The blunder didn't lower him. It raised him. They called it the pratfall effect. Basically, competence plus a visible flaw beats flawless competence — because flawless reads as distant, and distant feels like you can't trust it.
Now one detail, because this is where people get it wrong. The spill only helps if the skill is already there. They ran it with a contestant who'd done badly, and when that guy spilled, people liked him less. So the mess isn't the magic. The mess is proof of a human, sitting on top of real ability.
So sit with what that means for the take you just deleted. You've got the competence. Years of it. And the stumble you cut? That was the coffee spill. You deleted the exact thing that turns "impressive" into "I trust this person."
And here's why flawless actually backfires, because it sounds backwards at first. When somebody is too smooth, your brain doesn't relax. It gets suspicious. A part of you goes — what are they hiding. Real people stumble. Real people lose the word. So perfect reads as performance, and performance reads as a wall. The spill isn't a crack in the wall. The spill is the door. It's the one second where the viewer goes, oh — there's an actual human in there. I can deal with a human.
Here's the part that gets me, though. You felt the flinch and looked away from that moment as if it were the worst part. Everybody else would have leaned in. Which means your eyes and their eyes are reading the same footage completely differently. And there's a study on exactly that.
In 2018, three researchers — Bruk, Scholl, and Bless — ran a series of studies on how people judge a person being vulnerable. Admitting a mistake. Asking for help. Showing something unfinished. And they found a clean, repeatable split, and gave it a name I can't get over. The beautiful mess effect.
Here's the split. When you picture yourself showing your own mess, you see weakness. Something to hide. When other people see that same mess from you, they see courage. Honesty. Something to respect. Same moment. Two completely opposite verdicts. And the harsh one only ever comes from the person in the video.
Basically, you are grading your own footage with the one rubric guaranteed to be wrong. And here's the deeper version that even people who know this study miss — it's not that you're being humble or modest. It's that you literally cannot see yourself the way a stranger sees you. You're stuck inside, watching the take with all the memory of how it felt to flub it. They're outside, watching a person be real. You will never have their eyes. That's not a discipline problem. It's a blind spot, and it's wired in.
And you can see it out in the world the second you look. Think about a blooper reel. The outtakes. People will watch the bloopers more than the polished thing, and love the people in them more, because that's where they finally look human. Think about a singer live, going for a big note, and their voice cracks — and the whole crowd roars louder, because for one second the perfect thing became a person.
The cleanest one I know: 2013, the Oscars. Jennifer Lawrence wins Best Actress, walks up the steps to accept — and trips. Full stumble, on the biggest stage in the world, in front of everyone. And the world didn't think less of her. People loved her more. That stumble became one of the most beloved moments of the night. The pratfall effect, live, on the worst possible stage to fall on.
She couldn't delete it. So we got to keep it. And it made her ours.
Now flip it the other way, because the proof is in the absence too. Think about the most polished person you follow. The one whose every frame is perfect, every word landed, every angle right. Do you trust them more? Or is there a small, quiet part of you that keeps them at arm's length — that suspects you're being shown a set, not a person? We don't bond with the flawless. We respect them, maybe. From a distance. But the people we actually let in are the ones who let something slip. The mess isn't the price of being loved. A lot of the time, the mess is the reason.
Now hold on. Because there's a real objection here, and it deserves the floor.
Somebody's thinking: "Jevoy, come on. There's a reason polish exists. The internet is permanent. People judge you in seconds. If the first thing they see is me face-planting, that's the version they keep — forever. You don't see luxury brands posting their bloopers. You don't see Apple showing the messy middle. Polish is the signal. And honestly, my polished stuff seems to do fine."
That's a real argument, and parts of it stand. First impressions are fast and they stick. There are places where flawless is the whole promise — if your surgeon spills the coffee, that's not charming, that's a problem. And random sloppiness — bad audio, no point, nothing to say — that's not vulnerability. That's just noise. The pratfall study itself says so. The spill only works on top of real skill. I will never tell you to post incompetence.
And the permanence worry is real too. I won't pretend it isn't. The internet does keep things. The bad take can resurface. But walk one more step with it, because the math doesn't go the way fear says it does. A single clip can be taken out of context, sure. One frame, frozen, with no story around it. You know what fixes that? Not silence. More footage. When there are forty minutes of you being a real, consistent person, no single awkward second can define you, because the pattern drowns it out. The people who get destroyed by one clip are almost always the people who only ever gave the world one clip. Hiding doesn't protect you from the worst frame. It just makes the worst frame the only frame.
But here's where the objection breaks. It treats polish and proof like the same signal. They're not. Polish says "I can afford production." Proof says "this is what I'm actually like." And a stranger — especially one who's been burned before — is starving for the second one. Because the first one has been used to lie to them their whole life. Every scammer has a polished page. Nobody fakes the stumble. The human moment is expensive to fake, which is exactly why people believe it.
And Apple, by the way? Apple spends millions filming its labs and its machining and its design process. The most controlled "messy middle" on earth. They didn't skip the kitchen. They built a window into it.
So no. We're not too fake. We're flinching at the exact thing that would make us believed.
So here's the turn.
We've been treating the flinch as good judgment. Cut the stumble, cut the weird laugh, cut the mess, until nothing in the footage can embarrass you. But run the research back. The stumble was likability. The unfinished, honest moment was courage. The flinch was pointed at exactly the wrong frame.
You weren't deleting your worst part. You were deleting your best part — and keeping the part nobody remembers.
Because think about what a stranger can actually check in a video of you. They can't verify your claims. "Ten years of experience," "great at what I do" — everybody types the same words. But they can verify a human. Whether the laugh is real. Whether a person is talking or a script is being recited. Whether you flinched at your own joke. That check runs automatically, in seconds, and it's nearly impossible to fake. The worst part is the part that passes it.
And here's the thing I can't let go of. If the flinch is the default — if looking away from your own raw self is the most natural thing in the world — then leaving it in is a choice. A small, almost defiant one. Not hitting delete is one of the few things a person does that runs straight against the wiring. Which is exactly why it lands so hard when somebody actually does it. The take you were brave enough to keep is the take that finds people.
And notice what that does to the word "vulnerable," because it's been worn down to nothing. We treat vulnerability like it's about spilling your secrets, crying on camera, trauma as content. It's not. In the everyday version, vulnerable just means: I let you see the take where I wasn't perfect, and I didn't apologize for it. That's it. The unedited laugh. The real "I don't actually know." The stumble you didn't cover. It costs almost nothing and it's the rarest thing online, because everybody's flinching at the same frame at the same time. The whole internet, all of us, looking away from the one part that would have worked.
So what do we do with this? Three moves. Not editing tips. Eye-line moves.
One: change the question you edit by. Right now, when you watch yourself back, you're secretly asking, "what's the most professional version of this?" That question deletes the best parts every time. Swap it for a better one. "What would make a stranger believe this is real?" Different question. Different cuts. Watch what survives.
Two: don't trust the flinch on day one. The beautiful mess effect means you literally cannot see your own footage straight. So make a rule. Anything you want to delete out of embarrassment — not confusion, embarrassment — you don't delete today. You let it sit a day, or you let one trusted person watch it first. Nine times out of ten, the thing you were sure was the worst part is the part they quote back to you.
Three: keep one coffee spill on purpose. This week, when you make something — a video, a post, a voice note — leave in one human moment you'd normally cut. The stumble. The real laugh. The "wait, let me say that better." Just one. Don't smooth it. Post it with the spill in it, and watch what comes back. And here's the quiet test that tells you you're doing it right. If a moment makes you wince a little, but it's true and it's on top of real work — that wince is usually the signal to keep it, not cut it. The wince is just the beautiful mess effect, live, in your own chest. You're feeling the weakness. They're about to see the courage.
That's the case. The spill makes you likable. The mess reads as courage. And you're the only one in the world who can't see it.
Here's where this lands for me.
I do this for a living, and I still feel the flinch every week. The take I can't watch. The frame I want gone. But I've started catching my own hand on the way to delete, and asking it one question: am I cutting this because it's confusing — or because it's me? And if it's just because it's me, more and more, I leave it in. Because that's the one a person might actually keep.
There's an old line I keep circling. People look at the outside, at the polished front. But the truer read looks past all that, at the heart. And I think that's the read we're all secretly starving for — to be seen at the level the editing can't reach, and not deleted for it.
So decide which one you're making. The flawless thing nobody remembers. Or the real thing somebody keeps.
So here's what I actually want from you. Three small things.
One. In the comments, tell me the one take you almost deleted — and what happened when you didn't. Or what you wish you'd kept. One line. I read every one.
Two. Do the rep. This week, make one thing and leave the coffee spill in. The stumble, the real laugh, the part that's a little too you. Then come back and tell me what came back to you when you didn't cut it.
And three. If somebody you love keeps hiding the best, realest part of themselves under a polished front — don't tell them to be more vulnerable. That word's worn out. Just send them this. Sometimes it's the kindest way to say: the part you keep deleting is the part we love.
