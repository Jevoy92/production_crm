So why does the sharp one win before you think? There's a study, and it starts with this.
When I say "apple" — this word, right now — your brain doesn't define it. It shows it to you. You see the red skin. Maybe you taste the crunch. It's instant. It's a picture.
Now I say the word "nutrition." And your brain... stalls. It has to go searching. Find a definition. Hunt for an example. There's no picture for "nutrition." It's just fog with a dictionary entry.
A researcher named Allan Paivio figured out why, back in 1971. He called it dual coding. Basically — concrete words get filed twice in your brain. Once as a word, once as a picture. Abstract words only get filed once. So the concrete one comes back faster, sticks harder, and feels more real.
The brain doesn't want impressive. The brain wants the picture. The brain wants "apple."
And there's a second piece. A linguist named S. I. Hayakawa drew this thing called the ladder of abstraction, back in 1939. Imagine a ladder. At the top: the biggest words. Justice. Wealth. Success. At the bottom: the things you can hold. A gavel. A hundred-dollar bill. A gold medal.
