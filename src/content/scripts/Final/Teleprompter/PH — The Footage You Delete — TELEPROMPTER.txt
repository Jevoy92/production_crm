Let's start in 1966, with one of my favorite experiments in all of psychology.
A researcher named Elliot Aronson, with Willerman and Floyd, had people listen to recordings of a supposedly brilliant quiz contestant — a person getting nearly everything right. Impressive. Competent. And in one version of the tape, right at the end, the contestant... spills a cup of coffee all over himself. Small, human disaster.
Here's the finding, and it's been replicated and named: the competent person who spilled the coffee was rated more likable than the same competent person who didn't. The blunder didn't lower his standing. It raised it. They called it the pratfall effect. Competence plus a visible flaw beats flawless competence — because flawless reads as distant, and distant reads as untrustworthy.
One crucial detail, because this is where people misuse it: the spill only helps if the competence is already established. A mediocre contestant who spilled got rated lower. The mess isn't the magic. The mess is the proof of humanity on top of demonstrated skill.
Now translate that to your content. Your business has the competence — years of it. And in the edit, you are systematically removing every coffee spill. Every stumble, every correction, every human moment. You're deleting the exact ingredient that converts "impressive" into "I trust this person."
That's the mechanism. Here's why you can't see it in your own footage.
In 2018, three researchers — Bruk, Scholl, and Bless — published a series of studies on how people judge displays of vulnerability. Asking for help. Admitting a mistake. Showing unfinished work. And they found a clean, repeatable asymmetry, and gave it a name I love: the beautiful mess effect.
When you imagine showing your own mess, you see weakness — incompetence, inadequacy, something to hide. When other people see that same mess from you, they see courage. Honesty. Something admirable. Same footage. Two completely different verdicts — and the harsh verdict only ever comes from the person in the frame.
Read that against your editing session. The clip you deleted because "I look like an amateur"? The research says your audience would have read it as brave and real. You are not a reliable judge of your own vulnerable footage. Nobody is. It's not a discipline problem — it's a documented blind spot. You're grading your coffee spills with the only rubric guaranteed to be wrong.
And there's a third piece, and this one comes from a business school, not a psych lab. Harvard's Ryan Buell has spent years studying what he calls operational transparency — what happens when businesses let customers see the work happening. In one of the foundational studies, with Michael Norton, people using a travel search site actually rated the service higher when it made them wait — as long as the site showed them the work it was doing while they waited. They called it the labor illusion: watching effort happen makes us value the result more, trust it more, even prefer it to instant.
