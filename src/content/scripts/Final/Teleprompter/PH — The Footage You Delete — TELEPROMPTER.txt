Let's start in 1966, with one of my favorite experiments in psychology.
A researcher named Elliot Aronson — with Willerman and Floyd — had people listen to a recording of a smart quiz contestant. A guy getting almost everything right. Impressive. And in one version of the tape, right at the end, the contestant spills a cup of coffee all over himself. Small, human disaster.
Here's the finding. The smart guy who spilled was rated more likable than the same smart guy who didn't. The blunder didn't lower him. It raised him. They called it the pratfall effect. Basically, competence plus a visible flaw beats flawless competence — because flawless reads as distant, and distant reads as "I can't trust this."
One detail, because this is where people misuse it. The spill only helps if the skill is already proven. A contestant who'd done badly and then spilled got rated lower. So the mess isn't the magic. The mess is proof of a human, sitting on top of real ability. This is the part that should free you up. We are not talking about being sloppy on purpose. We're talking about not deleting the human moment that lands on top of work you already know is good. The competence is the meal. The spill is the seasoning. Nobody wants a plate of just seasoning — but the meal without it tastes like every other meal in the row.
Now translate that to your content. Your business has the competence. Years of it. And in the edit, you are deleting every coffee spill. Every stumble, every correction, every human moment. You're cutting the exact ingredient that turns "impressive" into "I trust this person."
That's the mechanism. Here's why you can't see it in your own footage.
In 2018, three researchers — Bruk, Scholl, and Bless — published a series of studies on how people judge someone being vulnerable. Asking for help. Admitting a mistake. Showing unfinished work. And they found a clean, repeatable split, and gave it a name I love. The beautiful mess effect.
Basically, when you picture showing your own mess, you see weakness. Something to hide. When other people see that same mess from you, they see courage. Honesty. Something to respect. Same footage. Two opposite verdicts. And the harsh one only ever comes from the person in the frame.
Read that against your editing session. The clip you deleted because "I look like an amateur"? The research says your audience would have read it as brave and real. You are not a reliable judge of your own vulnerable footage. Nobody is. It's not a discipline problem. It's a documented blind spot. You're grading your coffee spills with the one rubric guaranteed to be wrong.
And there's a third piece, and this one comes from a business school. Harvard's Ryan Buell has spent years on what he calls operational transparency — basically, what happens when you let customers see the work happening. In one of the foundational studies, with Michael Norton, people using a travel site rated the service higher when it made them wait — as long as the site showed them the work it was doing while they waited.
They called it the labor illusion. Watching the effort happen makes us value the result more. Trust it more. Even prefer it to instant.
The restaurant with the open kitchen. The mechanic who films the repair. The tracker that shows your order being packed. Seeing the middle of the work — the unglamorous, sleeves-rolled-up middle — is not a behind-the-scenes bonus. It's one of the most reliable trust machines ever measured.
And what does almost every business publish? The plated dish. The finished website. The after photo. The middle — the part that builds the trust — goes straight into the DELETED folder.
Here's why this hits so hard, in plain terms. A finished result is a claim. "We did good work." Anybody can claim that. But the middle is proof. When a customer watches the hands actually doing the thing — the sanding, the wiring, the careful part most people rush — they're not being told you're good. They're watching you be good. And watching beats being told every single time. That's the whole reason a busy person will stop and watch four minutes of someone restoring a beat-up chair. Nobody told them it'd be worth it. They got to see it become worth it.
