Watch what happens with each one.
I just made the worst face of my life into this mirror. And the mirror already forgot. It holds nothing. I walk away, and I'm a stranger to it again.
Now the phone. Same face. But this one... kept it. It's in a folder now. On a server. Forever. I could send it to my mother. Somebody could play it at my funeral.
So here's what I want to figure out today. Both of these are mirrors. You look in, you see yourself, you fix your hair. But only one of them remembers. And I think that one little difference quietly changes how you act every time it's pointed at you.
Here's the part that got me, though. There are three studies on this. The first one is from 1972. The second one is from a factory in the 1920s. And the third one — the third one says the exact opposite of the first two, and the only way to make all three true is going to flip how you think about being on camera. Stay with me.
Let me start with the 1972 study, because it explains the whole thing.
Two psychologists, Shelley Duval and Robert Wicklund. They wrote about something called objective self-awareness.
Sounds heavy. It's basically this. Most of the day, you live inside yourself. You're the one having the experience. You don't watch yourself eat lunch. You just eat lunch.
But the second something makes you aware of yourself as a thing — a mirror, an audience, a camera — you flip. You stop being the one living it. You start watching yourself from the outside.
And that flip is the whole game. Because the version of you that lives on the inside — that's the good one. That's the one that wins the room. That's the one who said the smart thing three years ago that people still quote back to you. That version runs on instinct. It isn't checking itself. It's just being. And it's excellent, because you've had your whole life to practice being it.
The camera comes on, and you're not being that one anymore. You're watching it. From the outside. On a delay.
You feel it happen. Your face gets heavy. Your words come out a half-second late. Your eyes drift just past the lens, like there's something interesting back there. There isn't. That's the flip. The mirror that remembers turned you into your own audience.
And here's the cruel little loop. Watching yourself makes you stiffer. Feeling stiff makes you watch yourself harder. Which makes you stiffer. You can be great in a room with one human, and wood in front of a lens — same brain, same hour. Nothing's broken. You just got flipped.
Now — you might think this is a modern thing. A camera thing. It's not. We've got proof from a hundred years ago, and there wasn't a camera in sight.
The 1920s. A factory outside Chicago — Western Electric, a place called the Hawthorne Works. Researchers came in to study the workers. Simple question. Does brighter lighting make people work faster?
So they turned the lights up. Output went up. Makes sense, right? Then — and this is the part — they turned the lights back down. And output went up again.
That made no sense. Until they understood what was really going on. It was never the lights. It was the watching. The workers knew researchers were studying them. So they behaved differently. Not better. Not worse. Just... different. More careful. More aware.
They called it the Hawthorne effect. Basically, people change the second they know they're observed. And here's the part even people who know this study skip over — the workers weren't trying to fake anything. They couldn't help it. Being watched reached in and changed the doing, all on its own.
So sit with that. A hundred years ago. Grown adults. Doing a job they'd done a thousand times. And the simple fact of a clipboard in the corner changed how their hands moved.
Now hand those same people a phone with a red dot on it. Of course you freeze. You're not weak. You're a human being doing the most documented thing a human being does. The watching changes the doing. It always has.
And you've felt this in a hundred tiny ways. The wedding toast, where you wrote something true and funny in the car, and then you stood up and a flatter, stiffer version of you read it instead. The work call where the little green light comes on and suddenly your own voice sounds borrowed. Even the FaceTime where your own face is in the corner — and you keep glancing at the box, watching yourself talk, and somehow getting worse at the conversation you're actually in. That's the flip. That's a hundred years of factory workers, in your pocket, every day.
So we live in the most-watched moment in human history. Every phone is a clipboard in the corner. And nobody ever sat us down and explained why being seen makes us seize up. So we each privately decided the same wrong thing — I'm just bad at this. Billions of people, all reaching the same incorrect conclusion. That alone should've been the clue.
So that's the case so far. A lens flips you to the outside. Being watched changes how you act. Cameras make you worse.
Except — that can't be the whole story. And I'll tell you exactly why. Because if being watched makes people worse, somebody needs to explain athletes.

The man who solved this is Robert Zajonc, 1965. He looked at all of it — the chokers and the champions — and found the one rule underneath.
Here it is, plain. Being watched doesn't make you better. Being watched doesn't make you worse. Being watched makes you more of whatever you already are.
He called it your dominant response. Basically, the move you'd make automatically, without thinking. An audience cranks the volume on that one move. That's all it does.
So now both sides make sense. The sprinter has run that race ten thousand times. For him, fast is automatic. Fast is the grooved-in move. So the crowd cranks the volume — and he flies. The kid giving a speech for the first time? Fumbling is the automatic move, because he's never done it. So the crowd cranks the volume on that — and he chokes.
