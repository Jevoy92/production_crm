# MINDYOURBIZNIZ — "The Handprint in the Sidewalk"
## (take on "The Wet Cement") · Solo Podcast Monologue | ~14 min · ~2,500 spoken words
## The Recorded Animal · Theme 13 "permanence of what gets recorded young" · Approach: SINGLE-IMAGE / refrain · Delivery: hushed, second-person, slow, music under most of it
### Voice-calibrated: bro-ban ✓ plain language ✓ friend woven softly ✓ no verdict ✓ one returning image ✓ faith quiet ✓ actionable CTA ✓ · One anchor image — a child's handprint in a sidewalk — returned to like a chorus

---

[COLD OPEN]
[ROOM TONE — very close, very warm. Almost a whisper. One low pad already breathing underneath, the whole way.]

**JEVOY:**

Picture a handprint.

[BEAT]

It's in a sidewalk. A small one. A kid pressed their hand into wet cement, decades ago, while somebody laughed and somebody else said *quick, before it dries.* And it's still there. Same little fingers. Same crooked star they scratched next to it. The kid is grown now. Maybe gone. But the hand stayed.

Stay there with me. Don't leave that sidewalk. Because the whole episode lives in that handprint — in that strange, tender fact that a moment can set, and hold, long after everyone forgets the day it happened.

[BEAT]

There was a window, see. Ninety minutes, maybe. The cement was soft enough to take the hand, and firm enough to keep it. Press too early, it closes up. Press too late, it won't take. But right in the middle — it holds forever.

You had a window like that. When you were young and still soft. And the things that got pressed into you then — the photos, the videos, the first impressions, the little stories told about you at dinner — they set. And here's the quiet, heavy thing I want to sit in with you. You've been living inside that handprint ever since. And most of it, you didn't press yourself.

[BEAT]

So here's the question I'll leave in the wet cement, and we'll come back for it. The version of you that set when you were young — the one in the photos, the one in the stories — is that who you are? Or is it just the hand that happened to be there when the cement was soft?

---

I have to tell you about something four researchers did in 2002. Their names were Wade, Garry, Read, and Lindsay. And it explains the handprint better than I can.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Wade, Garry, Read & Lindsay — "A Picture Is Worth a Thousand Lies," 2002.]

They took real childhood photos of people. And then they made one fake one. They quietly dropped each person, as a child, into a hot air balloon. A ride that never happened. Then they showed everyone their stack of photos and asked, softly, *tell me what you remember about this one.*

[PAUSE]

Half of them remembered the balloon ride.

A ride that never happened. And not a blur. Some of them remembered how high it felt. Who held their hand. The wind. They were remembering a day that lived only in a photo.

[BEAT]

Sit with what that means. The photo didn't remind them of the past. The photo *became* the past. It pressed into the wet cement, and the mind built a whole afternoon around it, and it set. And after that, they couldn't tell the real childhood from the made-up one. Because to you, a recording and a memory feel exactly the same.

So here's the tender truth about that handprint, and about you. You don't really remember your childhood. You remember the photos of it. The ones somebody chose to keep. The good ones, the posed ones, the kept ones. Your earliest self isn't a memory. It's an edit. And you've been calling the edit your life.

[PAUSE]

And notice — in your oldest "memories," you can usually see yourself. Your own small face, from across the room. But you can't film your own life from the outside. So that's not a memory at all. That's a photo your mind rebuilt a whole day around, with you placed inside it like an actor. The bad days, the boring days, the ordinary Tuesdays where you were just becoming a person — those didn't get photographed. So they didn't set. They're gone. The cement only kept the moments somebody pointed a camera at.

---

There's a second thing, older and simpler, and it's why the *first* press matters so much more than the tenth.

In 1946, a man named Solomon Asch read people a short list of words about a stranger. Same words for everyone. Smart. Hardworking. Stubborn. Cold.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Asch — "Forming Impressions of Personality," 1946.]

But for half the people, he said the warm words first. For the other half, he flipped it. Same words, every one. The ones who heard "smart" first ended up *liking* the stranger. The ones who heard "cold" first did not. Same person. Just a different order.

[PAUSE]

Whatever lands first becomes the frame. And then everything after it gets bent to fit. The first impression doesn't just come first. It sets, like cement, and your whole story gets poured to match it.

So picture the child who got called "the shy one." Once. On one tired afternoon. In one photo where they hid behind a leg. That set. And then every quiet moment after became proof. The kid grew up reading their own life through a handprint somebody else pressed when they were four. The cement set around one word. And nobody could find the stick again.

[BEAT]

And here's a number that stays with me. Researchers found the average child now has almost fifteen hundred photos of them posted online before they turn five.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Nominet / Parent Zone — ~1,500 photos of a child posted online before age five.]

Fifteen hundred. Before they can hold the stick. Before anyone asks them how they'd like to be remembered. The handprint is getting pressed in by other hands, out of love, one photo at a time — and the kid won't even see the wet square until it's already hard. We're the first people in history to set our children before they can choose the pose.

---

Now there's a friend I have to let onto the sidewalk. He loves a camera, and he's been waiting to push back. Let him.

"You're making this too sad," he says, gently. "That handprint isn't a prison. It's a gift. I barely remember my grandmother's voice — but I have one short video, and when I'm aching, I play it, and she comes back. Photos don't trap us. They rescue what we'd lose forever. Don't you dare make the camera the villain."

[PAUSE]

And — quietly — he's right. I won't bury that to keep my point clean. A recording can be the only thread back to someone you cannot lose. The handprint can be the most precious thing on the whole street.

So how can it be both? How can the photo plant a balloon ride that never happened, and also hand you back your grandmother's laugh?

[BEAT]

Because it was never really about the camera. It's about *which* hand set in the cement. His grandmother's laugh is true. It matches the real woman. When that sets, it keeps something real, and that's a mercy. The balloon ride is false. When a false thing sets first, it becomes a false past, and you can't feel the difference. Same cement. One holds the truth. One holds a lie. The cement doesn't care which. It just keeps whatever was there when you were soft.

[PAUSE]

So I'm not going to tell you to stop taking the pictures. Keep your grandmother's laugh. Keep the true ones, the ones that match the real day. That's not the trap. The trap is letting one photo become the *only* one. The trap is mistaking the edit for the whole. A handprint can be a treasure or a cage, and the difference isn't the cement. It's whether the hand that set was honest, and whether it was ever really yours.

---

So come back to the handprint, because this is the part I most need you to hear, and I'll say it soft.

The danger was never the photo. It's the *only* photo. The one frame that became the whole story. The word said with love, a hundred times, until it dried into fact. The version somebody else pressed in while you were too young to choose the pose — that you've been living inside so long you think it's just you.

And the heaviest part is how invisible it is. Nobody remembers pouring it. The people who loved you didn't sit down and assign you a role. They just took sweet pictures and told a few stories. But those stories set. And now you carry them as truth. You even defend them — every time you explain yourself with the old line, you press your own hand back into the dried shape, and make it deeper.

[MUSIC SWELLS, just slightly.]

But here's the thing about cement. There's always a new square being poured. Somewhere on your street, right now, there's wet.

[MUSIC settles back under.]

---

And I'll go first, like always.

There's a version of me that set young. A handprint about what I'm allowed to want, who I'm allowed to be. I won't get into whose hand it was. And I catch myself, still, walking into rooms where the cement is fresh — where nobody's met me, where I could press in anything — and quietly copying the old dried shape anyway. Performing a version of me that some afternoon long ago decided I was.

That's the work I'm in now. Not breaking the old square. You can't break it. Just refusing to copy it into the wet one.

[PAUSE]

Because some of your cement is set, and that's just true. The childhood handprint. The version your people pressed in. You're not getting the stick back into that square, and pretending you can is a way to waste the years you do have.

But you are pouring fresh cement right now. Today. Every new room. Every new beginning. Every morning you wake up and get to decide who presses the hand. There's a soft square in front of you. The only question is whether *you* write something true in it — or let the old dried version do the writing again.

[PAUSE]

And it's quieter and smaller than you'd think. It's not a dramatic reinvention. It's the first day somewhere new, when you choose to show up as who you are now, instead of who you were labeled. It's letting a new friend meet the real you before the old story arrives. It's pressing one honest hand into one soft square, on purpose, before the ninety minutes run out. You don't have to undo the whole street. You just have to stop copying the old print into every new square you find.

---

So here's the gentle work, and it's smaller than it sounds.

Find one wet square this week. One room where you're still new. And press in one true thing on purpose. One honest sentence about who you actually are now — not who you were poured to be. Let it set as yours.

[BEAT]

There's an old line I kept near this whole episode. It's about growing up — about putting away the small, childish version of a thing, and how for now we only see a dim reflection, but one day we'll be known fully, face to face. Being set in cement sounded like a sentence to me, for a long time. But there's a kind of being fully known that isn't a trap at all. It's a relief. The whole difference is whether the thing that set was true.

[PAUSE]

The handprint's still there in the sidewalk. The kid is grown. But somewhere on the street, the cement is still wet.

[BEAT]

You can still press your own hand in. A true one, this time. The one nobody assigned you.

---

So here's the only thing I'll ask of you, two small things.

First, in the comments — tell me your earliest "memory," and then tell me honestly: do you actually remember it, or do you just remember the photo? Type "real" or "photo." I think the "photo" pile is going to be huge. And I think naming it, out loud, loosens its grip a little.

And second — this week, find one wet square. One fresh start, one new room. Press in one true thing on purpose, and come tell me what you wrote, and whether it held. Not for me. For you. But I'll be down there reading every one, because I'm still learning to do this too.

And if someone you love is still living inside a handprint somebody else pressed before they could choose — a sibling, a friend, a kid who got labeled and never got the stick back — don't argue them out of it. Just send them this. Sometimes that's the kindest way to say: the cement's still wet somewhere. You can write something true.

[ROOM TONE — the pad fades. A breath. Out.]
