# MINDYOURBIZNIZ — "Who Holds You in Mind"
## Solo monologue · hushed, second-person · 2,000–3,000 spoken words · Standalone
## Approach: SINGLE-IMAGE / two climbers, one canyon · Delivery: close, slow, music under most of it
### Voice-calibrated: bro-ban ✓ plain language ✓ friend woven softly ✓ no verdict ✓ one image returned to ✓ sensory + "basically" ✓ actionable CTA ✓ faith quiet ✓

---

[COLD OPEN]
[ROOM TONE — very close, very warm. Almost a whisper. One low pad breathing underneath, the whole way.]

**JEVOY:**

Answer this slow.

[BEAT]

If something went badly wrong in your life this week — really wrong — how long before somebody noticed?

Not how long before you told someone. That's a different question. I mean noticed. On their own. Who is watching you closely enough — your moods, your silences, the too-long pauses — that your absence would land like a missed heartbeat?

[BEAT]

For some of you, a name came right away. I'm glad. Hold onto it. By the end I'll tell you why that name is doing more for your health than your gym membership.

And for some of you, the question opened a small trapdoor. Especially if you're the one who builds things. Especially if you're the strong one. Because here's what happens when you run something — a business, a family, a team. You become the one who does the noticing. Everyone leans their weight on your attention. And almost nobody is assigned to you.

[BEAT]

So stay with me. Picture a canyon. We're going to spend this whole episode in that canyon, because everything I'm trying to say happens there.

---

I have to tell you about a man named Aron Ralston.

[SOURCE CARD on screen: Aron Ralston — 'Between a Rock and a Hard Place' (2004); Blue John Canyon, April 2003.]

April 2003. He's hiking alone in a slot canyon in Utah. A boulder shifts and pins his arm against the wall. Eight hundred pounds. He's there for five days. And for most of those days, he believes he's going to die in that crack in the earth.

He had a camcorder in his bag. And with his free hand, he started recording. Messages to his mother. To his father. He told them he loved them. He said the grateful things out loud — the things we usually save for a eulogy and never say to a living face.

And here's the part I keep pulling on. He said later those recordings were some of the most peaceful moments of the whole ordeal. Talking to people who weren't there, through a lens, somehow steadied him.

So that's the question here. Why would that help? Why does speaking to people who love you — people who aren't even in the room — change what a body can endure?

---

The answer starts with a neuroscientist named James Coan.

[SOURCE CARD on screen: Coan, Schaefer & Davidson — 'Lending a Hand,' Psychological Science, 2006.]

2006. He put married women in a brain scanner. He told them they might get a small electric shock. And he watched the fear part of the brain light up, just from the waiting. Then he had each woman's husband reach in and hold her hand.

The fear went down. Not her bravery. The actual cost of the fear, in the brain, turned down by one hand. A stranger's hand helped a little. The husband's hand helped most. And the better the marriage, the bigger the drop.

[PAUSE]

Coan built an idea out of that, and I think about it constantly. Basically, your brain budgets for other people. Like calories. Like air. Your nervous system was built assuming people would be near you. That's the normal setting — the one all the energy math runs on. Connection was never a luxury bolted onto a self-sufficient machine. The machine was designed around the connection.

Which flips what loneliness even is. Loneliness isn't really an emotion. It's a deficit. When you're alone — truly unheld in anyone's mind — your brain re-does the math. Every threat costs more. Every hill gets steeper.

And I mean that literally. There's a study, Schnall and her colleagues, 2008. People standing alone judged a hill to be physically steeper than people standing next to a friend. The world is rendered harder by the absence of your people.

[SOURCE CARD on screen: Schnall et al. — hill-slant perception, J. Exp. Social Psychology, 2008.]

That tired you can't sleep off? Some of it isn't your workload. Some of it is running a life in solo mode that was built for two.

And the stakes aren't poetic. In 2010, a researcher named Julianne Holt-Lunstad pulled together 148 studies. Over three hundred thousand people. People with strong relationships had about fifty percent better odds of surviving the study periods. The line that made headlines: weak social connection sits in the same risk range as smoking. As a *survival* factor. So loneliness isn't a mood. It's exposure.

[SOURCE CARD on screen: Holt-Lunstad, Smith & Layton — 'Social Relationships and Mortality Risk,' PLoS Medicine, 2010.]

---

Now hold the picture. This is the image to leave with you, so stay in it.

Two climbers go into two canyons.

The first one — nobody knows where he is. That was Ralston, exactly. He says it plainly in his book. The first mistake wasn't the boulder. It was that he told no one. No note. No text. So when the rock came down, there was no rescue forming anywhere out in the world. Whatever happened next, he was the only resource in the equation.

The second climber is in the same canyon. Same boulder. Same arm. But three people know his route, and one of them expects a call by sundown.

Nothing about the rock changed. The physics are identical. But everything about the experience is different. The math of hope. The pace he can spend his strength at. What the body is willing to endure. Because somewhere out there, a search has a start time. He is held in minds. And the holding does its work at a distance.

[PAUSE]

Here's why I'm telling you this. Most of the builders I know are climber number one. By choice. Not in the mountains — in life. We walk into our canyons. The cash crunch. The doubt. The marriage strain. The slow-failing thing we're not ready to call failing. And we tell no one the route. Not because no one would care. Because we're the strong one. Because everybody already brings their boulders to us.

So the question from the start, again. Who would notice?

Climber one and climber two aren't two kinds of people. They're two decisions. And you make that decision before the boulder — or the boulder makes it for you.

---

And here's the counter-voice in my head, right on time. Today it sounds like every founder I've ever sat across from.

"I hear you. But be real about this life. The isolation isn't a bug. It's the price of the seat. I can't process my fears with my employees — that's not honesty, that's instability, and they need me steady. I can't fully unload on my spouse — she's already carrying her half, and most of this would just scare her. My old friends don't get the stakes. My new friends might be networking. And honestly? I recharge alone. Introverts are real. So this 'who holds you' thing is sweet. But the seat I'm in doesn't come with it. That's why they call it lonely at the top. It's a description, not a complaint."

[BEAT]

Every line of that deserves respect. So let me take it seriously.

It's true that leading takes some containment. The captain doesn't get to panic out loud on deck. That's not dishonesty. That's stewardship.

And it's true that solitude and loneliness are different animals. Solitude is chosen aloneness that fills you back up. The research never said being alone kills you. It said being *unheld* does. You can be alone all week and deeply held. You can be surrounded all day and held by no one. The dose was never people. The dose is being known.

But here's where the friend's math breaks. And it breaks on the friend's own terms — performance.

Go back to Coan. The brain budgets fear based on the support it can feel. A leader with zero hands in the scanner is paying full price on every single stressor. Every payroll. Every hard call. Every 2 a.m. what-if costs more, in the body, than it costs the leader who's held. That's not soft. That's capacity. The contained captain still needs a harbor. Containment with no harbor isn't strength. It's a slow leak with good posture.

And "lonely at the top" — listen to what that phrase actually is. It's a design flaw that got repeated so often it turned into a job description. The top isn't naturally lonely. It's just the place where people stop assuming you need checking on. Which means the checking has to be arranged. On purpose. Like everything else at the top.

---

So here's the turn.

We treat being held in mind like weather. Like something that happens to the lucky. Warm families. Old friends. A good marriage. You either have your search party or you don't.

But look again at what Ralston did in that canyon. He had no search party. Nobody knew where he was. So with a dying battery, he built one. He spoke to his family through a lens like they were there. And his nervous system — which can't fully tell a real witness from one it imagines — answered. Calm came. And eventually resolve came, in the shape of a vision of a son not yet born. A future witness, made out of nothing, strong enough to power an impossible act.

Being held in mind isn't weather. It's architecture. It can be built. Late, alone, in the dark, with bad tools — it can still be built. And if a man can build it in a canyon at hour 100, you can build it in your life this week, for a fraction of the cost.

The structure that saves climbers is the same one that holds founders and mothers and pastors and all of us. It's just two sentences. Somebody knows my route. And somebody expects my call.

That's the whole load-bearing wall. Two sentences. Most of us have neither. And we call the cold coming through the gap "just how building feels."

---

[MUSIC SWELLS, just slightly.]

And I'll go first. Like always.

Building what I'm building, I've gotten very good at one move. Being the noticer. I track my people. I check on the team, the family, the friends. I'm the "how are you really doing" guy. And I've slowly understood that being the noticer is, among other true things, a brilliant hiding place. Nobody audits the auditor. If I'm always the one asking, nobody gets far enough into my side to ask back. The role is generous, and real. And it's also armor. And I picked it partly because it's armor.

[BEAT]

And the cost shows up somewhere strange. Not in a crisis. I'm not in a canyon. It shows up in the good moments. Something wins. A project lands. A thing I prayed over actually happens. And there's this half-second where the joy goes looking for somewhere to land — and it just hovers. Because the people who'd celebrate don't know the stakes. And the people who know the stakes are the ones I keep steady for. So the win gets filed instead of felt.

That's the quiet tax of being unheld. It's not only that nobody catches your sorrows. It's that nobody catches your joys either. Both bounce.

[PAUSE]

So the practice, present tense, same as every week. I'm building my two sentences. One person who actually knows my route — the real map, with the real cliffs marked. And rhythms where somebody expects my call. Not crisis calls. Scheduled ones. Because Coan's data says the hand has to be in the scanner *before* the shock to do its full work. It felt unnatural for about three weeks. It feels like oxygen now. Which, going by the science, is exactly what it is.

[MUSIC FADES under the next line.]

---

So here's the work this week. Two moves. Both small. Both doable by Friday.

First. Drop a pin. One person. Tell them where you actually are — not the trailer, the route. "Here's what I'm carrying this season. Here's the part that's heavier than it looks." You're not asking them to fix anything. You're filing a flight plan. A search party can't look for someone they don't know is missing.

Second. Become someone's noticer — on purpose. Pick one person you suspect is climber number one. Put a recurring fifteen minutes on the calendar with them. Not "let's catch up sometime." Scheduled. Expected. Because here's the beautiful part — an expected call is a witness structure for both of you. The moment somebody expects your call, you are, by definition, held in mind. You can build for someone else the thing you're too proud to ask for. And it holds you anyway. I'm pretty sure that's the design.

[BEAT]

There's an old passage I kept coming back to all week. One of the oldest cost-benefit notes ever written, and it's about partnership: *two are better than one, because they get a good return for their work. If either of them falls down, one can help the other up. But pity anyone who falls and has no one to help them up. Though one may be overpowered, two can defend themselves. And a cord of three strands is not quickly broken.*

If either of them falls. The oldest risk assessment in literature. And it lands exactly where the modern study landed three thousand years later. The deadliest variable isn't the fall. It's falling unheld.

That cord of three strands — you, one real person, and whatever third strand you choose to braid in — that's the architecture. Not built in the canyon. Built before it. Slowly. On ordinary Tuesdays. Fifteen scheduled minutes at a time.

[PAUSE]

You were never supposed to be the only one holding the rope.

---

So before you close this. Two things.

Down in the comments, tell me one line. Who is the person who knows your real route? If a name came, type it — first name, initials, whatever's safe. And if no name came, type that too. Just "no name yet." I read them. I'm not asking from above this. I'm asking from inside it.

And then this week, make one of those calls. The fifteen-minute kind. Scheduled. Expected. Then come back and tell me what it did to your week to know somebody would notice. Not for me. For you.

[BEAT]

And if somebody you love is clearly climber number one — going into every canyon alone, telling no one — don't lecture them. Just send them this. Sometimes that's the quietest way to say: I'd notice if you went missing.

Go braid the cord.

[ROOM TONE — the pad fades. A breath. Out.]
