We'll get there.

First — the receipts. Because what makes height the perfect specimen isn't that the advantage exists — everybody's always suspected it exists. It's that scientists actually *measured* it. In dollars. With controls. Peer-reviewed.

## THE RECEIPTS

So, 2004. Two researchers — Timothy Judge and Daniel Cable — gather up decades of workplace data, income data, longitudinal studies, the works. They control for age. They control for gender. They control for weight. And they ask one question: does height, by itself, predict earnings?

It does. The number that fell out of that research is one of those numbers you hear once and never forget: roughly **$789 per inch, per year.**
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Judge & Cable — 'The Effect of Physical Height on Workplace Success and Income,' J. Applied Psychology, 2004.]

[PAUSE]

Per inch. Think about what that means in a real life. Two people, same talent, same work ethic, same field — and one of them is six inches taller, so one of them collects something like an extra four or five grand a year. Every year. For decades. Compounding. There are people who got a raise for being tall every single year of their career and never once knew it. Their height has made more consistent money than most people's investment accounts. The inches never missed a quarter. The inches never had a down year. The inches don't even know they're employed.

And it climbs all the way to the top. Malcolm Gladwell, researching his book *Blink*, surveyed the CEOs of the Fortune 500 — the most fought-over chairs in American business. In the general population, about fourteen and a half percent of American men are six feet or taller. Among those CEOs? Fifty-eight percent. One in seven on the street; three in five in the corner office. And nobody — nobody — on any board, in any search committee, ever wrote down "height" as a criterion. They didn't have to. The bias doesn't need a memo. It's wired in.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Malcolm Gladwell — Blink (Fortune 500 CEO height survey), 2005.]

Here's the wiring, because there's a name for it and it's a hundred years old. In 1920, a psychologist named Edward Thorndike noticed something broken in how army officers rated their soldiers: the men rated as physically impressive also got rated as smarter, more loyal, better character — one trait bleeding into all the others, like the form only had one real question on it. He called it the **halo effect.** Your brain takes the one thing it can verify in two seconds — tall, handsome, deep voice — and autofills everything it *can't* verify: capable, trustworthy, leader. For most of human history, following the biggest one in the group was honestly a decent survival strategy. So the wiring stayed. And now it sits in boardrooms, handing out corner offices to altitude.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Thorndike — 'A Constant Error in Psychological Ratings' (the halo effect), J. Applied Psychology, 1920.]

And before the tall guys get too comfortable — it's not just you. The same machine pays out everywhere. Researchers found people with easier-to-pronounce *names* get judged more trustworthy and climb law firms faster. Economists found a measurable beauty premium across ordinary jobs — and its evil twin, which they actually named the "plainness penalty," which might be the coldest phrase in all of economics. There's even a study on CEO *voices* — the deeper the pitch, the bigger the company, on average. Names, faces, voices, inches. A whole stock market of things nobody earned, trading around the clock.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Hamermesh & Biddle — 'Beauty and the Labor Market,' American Economic Review, 1994.]

## THE PART WHERE I LET IT BE UNFAIR

Now. Here's where most episodes about this stuff lose me, so I want to do this part differently.

Most takes on this go one of two ways. Way one: "so the game is rigged, burn it down" — pure grievance, no exit. Way two — and this one's worse — some guru looks into the camera and says "winners don't make excuses!" and just... skips past the data. Pretends the $789 isn't real. Tells the five-foot-six guy he's imagining the headwind.

No. We're not doing either. The headwind is *real.* It's peer-reviewed. If you've spent your life suspecting that some people get a yes faster than you for reasons that have nothing to do with merit — you were right, and you've always been right, and you deserve to hear someone say it plainly: the game has unearned advantages built into it, the people holding them mostly don't see them, and no amount of hustle-quote energy makes that not true.

[BEAT]

Feel that? Good. That feeling is legitimate. You're allowed about a day of it.

I'm serious — take the day. Be irritated. Text somebody tall something petty. Make them reach for something on a low shelf, I don't know. Get it out of your system, because the day has a hard expiration on it.

Because here's what I've noticed about that feeling, and I say this as someone who has *had* the feeling: it is one hundred percent accurate and zero percent useful. It's a perfectly true fact that pays no bills. You can be completely right about the other guy's inches and go broke being right. The unfairness is real — and it was never the most important fact about your situation. The most important fact is the one we're about to get to.

## THE TURN — WHERE ARE YOU TALL?

[MUSIC FADES IN — one low, warm pad.]

Here's the question this whole episode exists to ask you, and I want you to actually try to answer it before I finish talking:

**Where are you tall?**

[PAUSE]

Because the same lottery that handed out inches handed out everything else, with the same total disregard for fairness. Nobody earned their starting deck — that cuts every direction. Somewhere on your sheet there's an advantage you did nothing to deserve, that other people would pay for, that's been sitting there your whole life — and I'd bet money you've never once counted it, because of a strange rule of human accounting: *we are experts on the advantages we didn't get, and blind to the ones we did.*

You can quote the tall guy's inches from memory. Can you quote your own? Sit with how strange that is — you've audited a stranger's genetics more carefully than your own gifts.

Maybe you're the person strangers tell things to — within five minutes, their whole story, because something in your face says *safe.* Do you know what salespeople, therapists, and leaders would pay for that? Maybe you've got the voice — not the deep one, the *warm* one, the one that makes a voicemail feel like a hand on the shoulder. Maybe you grew up between two worlds — two languages, two cultures, two kinds of rooms — and you can translate between people who can't hear each other; entire careers are built on exactly that. Maybe you're the one who can explain things — who takes the complicated mess and hands it back to people in a sentence they can use; there are companies that exist entirely because one person in a meeting could do that. Maybe your unfair advantage is that you've already survived something, and pressure that breaks other people just feels like Tuesday to you. Maybe it's the energy — you walk in and rooms exhale; nobody trained you to do that, you can't even turn it off. Maybe — and this one's my favorite — your advantage is being *underestimated.* Walking into rooms where nobody expects anything, which means nobody's guarded, everybody talks freely around you, and your wins land at double value because no one saw them coming. Ask anyone who has it: low expectations are a weapon with a lifetime warranty.

And notice something about that whole list — not one of those shows up in the salary studies. You know why? Because economists can measure inches, and they cannot yet measure *warmth per voicemail.* The advantages that got studied are the ones with rulers. Yours might not have a ruler yet. That doesn't make it smaller than height, friend. It makes it unpriced — and any investor will tell you where the real returns hide: in the assets the market hasn't figured out how to value.

None of those were earned either. That's the point. The height tax is real — and so is yours. The difference is the tall guy's getting paid on his, and yours is sitting in the envelope.

## THE EMOTIONAL PART

And let me sit in this honestly for a minute, because I'm not preaching from above it.

I'm building things right now — a company, this show, all of it, from launch stage, which means I spend a lot of time looking at people who are further ahead. And the temptation that lives in that view — every builder knows it — is to do forensic accounting on *their* advantages. He started with money. She knew the right people. They got the timing, the look, the connections. And the thing about that audit is — it's often *correct.* That's what makes it so addictive. You can build an airtight case. I've built several. Beautiful cases. Won them all, in the courtroom of my own head, where the verdict pays nothing.

[BEAT]

And meanwhile — here's the confession — it took me embarrassingly long to do the same audit on myself. To sit down and honestly ask: okay, what did *I* get handed? And the list was longer than I wanted it to be — that's how you know the exercise is working, by the way; it should be a little uncomfortable. I grew up between Kingston and Seattle — two worlds, two musics, two ways of reading a room — and for years I filed that under "complicated" instead of under "assets." I've got a face and an energy that make people open up fast — strangers tell me their business unprompted — and I spent years thinking that was just... a funny thing that happens, instead of the entire foundation of a career built on getting people comfortable on camera. The envelope was sitting right there. I was too busy counting other people's inches to open my own mail.

[PAUSE]

That's the real tax, you feel me? Not the $789 the tall guy collects. The advantage *you* never deploy because you're either blind to it or — and this one's deeper — quietly apologizing for it. Some of us feel *guilty* about our unearned edges, like using them is cheating. Listen: the tall guy is not stooping. Nobody's asking him to slouch through the boardroom. Stand up in whatever way you're tall. That was never cheating. That was the assignment.

[MUSIC FADES OUT — slow.]

## LAND — THE PRACTICE + THE VERSE

So here's the work this week, and it's one conversation and one decision.

The conversation: go to two or three people who know you well and ask them this exact question — "What do I make look easy?" Don't coach the answer. Don't argue with it. Write it down. The unearned advantage is invisible from the inside — from in here it just feels like *normal* — so you need outside eyes to read your own measurements. Whatever they say that makes you go "what? that's nothing" — circle that one. That's the envelope.

The decision: stop apologizing for it, and put it to work — deliberately, this week, once. Lead with the warmth. Use the voice. Walk into the room you've been underestimated out of and let it be a weapon. Stewardship, not slouching.

And one more small thing while you're at it — flip the audit around, just once. The same week you inventory your own advantages, catch yourself paying the height tax *out* to somebody else. The moment you grant extra weight to the tall guy's opinion in the meeting, the confident voice on the call, the polished stranger over the rumpled friend — that's your own halo machine, running the ancient autofill. You don't have to fix the whole species this week. Just catch it once and ask: what's the actual evidence here? Because the deepest move in this whole episode isn't collecting your own premium. It's becoming the rare person in the room who pays attention to what's true instead of what's tall.

[BEAT]

And here's the oldest framing of this I know — there's a story that's been told for two thousand years about three people each handed a different amount of money before a long trip. One gets a lot, one gets some, one gets a little — and listen to the detail everyone glosses over: it's handed out *unequally,* on purpose, with no explanation and no apology. The story just *opens* with unfairness, like it's the weather. Which, as we've established with peer-reviewed receipts, it is.

And here's the part that wrecked me when I really looked at it. At the end, the one who got a lot and the one who got a little — when they put their share to work, they get the *exact same* verdict. Identical. The amount never mattered. The only one who comes out badly is the one who took his portion, however small, and *buried it in the ground* — because he was so busy being resentful and afraid about how little he got that he never used the thing that was actually in his hands.

The judgment was never about the inches. It was about the burial.

So — somebody else got five bags. Somebody got the height, the name, the timing, the head start. Noted. Filed. Day of irritation: taken. Now: what's in *your* hand, and is it in the ground?

Dig it up. While it's still your turn to. That's the episode.

If you know somebody who's been doing forensic accounting on everyone else's advantages — and you do, and they're brilliant, and they're stuck — send them this one. Thanks for being here. MindYourBizniz is a Palmer House conversation. New episode next week.
