# MINDYOURBIZNIZ — "Known by Thousands, Seen by No One"
## Solo Podcast Monologue | ~15–16 min | v3 — KITCHEN TABLE REWRITE
### Rewritten per voice feedback: conversational close-friend delivery, humor throughout, named real examples (Russell Wilson, Taylor Swift, The Office, Tom from MySpace), casual timely anchors. Same science, same spine, new voice.

---

[COLD OPEN]
[ROOM TONE — close, quiet. The mug set down on wood. A breath.]

**JEVOY:**

There's a thing Jim Carrey said in an interview years ago that I've never been able to shake. He said — and I'm paraphrasing, but barely — "I think everybody should get rich and famous and do everything they ever dreamed of... so they can see that it's not the answer."

Now hold that thought, and picture this with me. Imagine a message lands in your inbox — and somewhere, right now, somebody is genuinely typing this to somebody — and it says: "I have thousands of followers... so why do I feel invisible?"

Thousands of followers. Feels invisible.

And be honest about your first reaction, because I'll be honest about mine — I'm over here still building an audience, so some part of me goes, that sounds like a problem I would love to have. Right? That sounds like a rich man complaining his pool is too big. [small laugh]

But sit with it a second longer — the way Carrey's line makes you sit — and it flips. Because this is actually one of the realest problems of our era. There's a specific kind of loneliness — and it's kind of a new one, historically, like our grandparents did not have this problem — where a whole bunch of people know you... and not one of them sees you.

[BEAT]

And before you go "well I don't have an audience so this one's not for me" — hold on, hold on. Stay with me. Because the thousand doesn't have to be followers. It could be your church. It could be your job. It could be the family group chat where you're the strong one, the funny one, the one who's "always good." Everybody in that group chat would swear — swear on something — that they know you. And what they know is a version. The crowd's not the point. The point is lots of people holding a version of you, and nobody holding you.

Now, this is not gonna be one of those episodes where I tell you the internet is fake and real connection only happens at, I don't know, farmers markets and drum circles. Come on now. Some of the realest encouragement I have ever gotten in my life came from a stranger through a screen. I'm not about to sit here on a podcast — on the internet — and tell you internet connection isn't real. That would be crazy.

What I want to do instead is figure out what's actually going on. Because it turns out there's research on this — from 1956, bro. Before social media. Before the internet. Two researchers basically predicted this entire feeling, watching black-and-white television. And I want to walk you through it, and then I want to tell you about the empty theater, and then — because we don't end in the sad place on this show anymore, that's the new rule — I want to take you somewhere kids play. For real. Blanket forts are involved. Let's go.

---

## CHAPTER 2: THE MECHANISM

Alright, so. 1956.

I want you to picture this, because I think it's funny. Television is brand new. Like, your neighbor having a TV is still a flex. And families are gathering around this glowing box every night, and two researchers — Donald Horton and Richard Wohl — start noticing something... weird.

People are forming relationships with the TV hosts.

Not "I enjoy this program." I mean relationships. People would talk about the host like he was a family friend. They knew his jokes, his moods, his little mannerisms — honestly, better than they knew the actual man living next door. And when a show got cancelled? People grieved. Like, genuinely grieved, for a man who had no idea they were alive.

So Horton and Wohl gave it a name: parasocial interaction. And the word "para" is doing all the work there — it means "alongside." Almost. Resembling. It's a relationship with all the feelings of a friendship... running in exactly one direction. You know everything about them. They don't know you exist.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Horton & Wohl — 'Mass Communication and Para-Social Interaction,' Psychiatry, 1956.]

[PAUSE]

And listen — before we get all judgmental about 1956, look around, because we never stopped. You feel me? I live in Seattle. I watched this whole city go through an actual breakup when Russell Wilson got traded. I'm talking grown men in mourning. People were writing goodbye posts like a marriage ended. And I just want to gently point out — Russ was not coming to your barbecue, bro. He was never coming to your barbecue! You did not know him! And yet the grief was real. That's how strong this wiring is.

Or — okay, The Office. You know somebody, you might BE somebody, who has watched The Office all the way through like nine times. Nine times! You didn't forget what happens! That's not entertainment anymore — Michael Scott is your coworker at this point. Those are your work friends. You eat lunch with them. Taylor Swift has grown adults who would genuinely take a bullet for her, and she does not know their names. And my favorite example of all time — Tom from MySpace. Tom was the first friend of an entire generation. Default friend! Everybody had Tom! Tom knew nobody!

[small laugh]

Okay. So that's the funny half. Here's the half that got me.

Back in 1956, only famous people lived on the other side of that glass. The host's side. But now? Now it's teachers. Founders. Pastors. Your cousin with the popular page. Anybody with any audience at all is now living the host's half of that bargain — which means warmth is arriving constantly, every day, in the DMs, in the comments... from people who know your output — your polished, public front-stage self. While the actual person making all of it sits alone in a room with a microphone.

Which, let me look around... yeah. Is exactly what I'm doing right now. [beat] Moving on.

And here's the cruel part, the part that messes people up: the warmth is real. That's what makes it so confusing. Nobody's lying to you. The encouragement is sincere. But there's a psychologist named William Swann who spent his career proving something that sounds wrong until you sit with it — humans don't actually crave being liked. We crave being accurately known. He found people would rather be seen correctly than seen generously. Wild, right? But you already know it's true, because you've felt what happens when somebody praises the version of you that isn't quite you. It doesn't land. It sort of... bounces off. Like a deposit into an account that doesn't exist.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: William Swann — self-verification theory.]

So the math gets dark quietly. The better you perform the version, the more people love the version, the bigger the crowd holding somebody who isn't quite you — and the further you drift from anybody holding who is. The applause lands, bro. It really does land. Just on the costume. I said that a month ago on this show and I have not been able to put it down since.

## CHAPTER 3: THE IMAGE YOU CAN HOLD IN YOUR MIND

No props on a podcast, so just build this with me. You're good at this by now.

A theater. Sold out. Every seat. And the show is great — this isn't a sad show, the show kills. Standing ovation. Real applause, deserved applause. The actor bows, the curtain drops...

...and the actor walks backstage, and backstage is empty.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Erving Goffman — The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life, 1959.]

[BEAT]

No crew. No castmates. Nobody waiting there who knows their actual name. Just costume racks and one of those mirrors with the bulbs around it.

A thousand people out front for the character. Zero people back here for the actor.

And here's what I need you to notice about that picture — nothing in it is fake. Nothing! The play is good. The applause is honest. The theater fills up every single night. If you looked at this actor's life on paper — or let's be honest, on Instagram — you'd say this person is winning. The problem is in the one room the audience can't see and never thinks about. The room where the costume comes off. The crowd can't fill that room. It's not that they don't want to — they structurally can't. They don't know where the door is. They don't know there is a door.

And the wildest part, the part I keep chewing on — the actor built it like this. Not on purpose. One totally reasonable decision at a time. Because every hour you spend making the front stage more lovable is an hour you didn't spend letting one actual person backstage. And the front stage pays! The front stage applauds! The front stage hands you a thousand little warmths a day, and all the backstage offers is one person seeing you out of costume — which is the single scariest transaction on the menu.

So we keep choosing the thousand. Night after night. And the room stays empty. Not because we're fools. Because the math feels right, right up until Sunday evening when it suddenly doesn't.

## CHAPTER 4: THE FRIEND WHO PUSHES BACK

Okay, and here comes my friend. You know the friend — lives in my head, keeps me honest. And today the friend has a real community online, and honestly? Today the friend is a little offended.

"Jevoy. Careful, bro. You're talking about MY people like they're imaginary. My audience HAS seen me. They watched me bomb a launch. They watched me lose my dad — and they showed up. Prayers, DMs, people who'd been through it checking on me at midnight — things my actual neighbors never did. My neighbors waved at me the week my father died, Jevoy. The internet sat with me. And while we're at it — plenty of folks are 'deeply known' by a spouse who hasn't really looked at them since the Obama administration. So don't romanticize the dinner table and slander my comment section. Connection is connection."

[BEAT]

Whew. Okay. That's real, and about eighty percent of it is just... true. So let me give it its due before I tell you where it breaks.

It's true that parasocial bonds can be genuinely good — Horton and Wohl said that themselves in 1956, this isn't new. For isolated people they can be a lifeline. It's true that if you shared real things, real witnessing happened — I've felt it from my own little audience and I won't disrespect it. And the spouse jab? Completely fair. A front-row seat to a person is not the same thing as watching them. Some of the loneliest people I've ever met were married.

But here's where it holds, friend, and it's not about whether the connection is real. It's about diet.

Parasocial warmth is real the way sugar is real. I'm serious — real calories, real energy, genuinely sweet, your body genuinely responds. And you cannot live on it. Because it's missing the one nutrient the soul actually runs on — and this is Swann's whole finding — being known accurately, in both directions, by somebody who can check. Think about it: your audience saw what you chose to share. Even your rawest, most vulnerable post — you wrote it, you read it back, you picked the photo, you posted it after the fact. It was curated grief, bro. The person backstage sees the stuff you didn't choose. The Tuesday version. The before-coffee version. The version that contradicts your content.

A thousand people holding your story is a library. One person holding your gaze is a meal. And listen, I love libraries. Libraries are wonderful. Nobody has ever been fed in one. They've got signs up specifically about that.

## CHAPTER 5: THE FLIP

So here's the flip — and remember the new rule on this show: we don't flip into "here's how to manage your sad thing." We flip somewhere better. Today we're flipping into a blanket fort.

I'm serious. Stay with me.

Go back to that empty backstage, and ask what backstage is actually for. Because Goffman — the sociologist who gave us this whole front stage, backstage idea — he wasn't describing a recovery room. Backstage is where the cast plays. It's where the jokes are worse and louder. It's where the bits live. It's where you rehearse the wild idea before it's ready, where you get to be completely un-impressive at full volume and nobody's grading you.

And nobody — nobody — understands this better than kids. Watch a kid sometime. A kid will perform all day long, happily, to an audience of zero. Narrating their own adventure. Full commitment. And then, the moment the best friend shows up — instant switch — into the fort. The blanket fort. And inside the fort there's a different kid. A sillier one. One with a secret language and worse jokes and bigger dreams. And here's the thing: the kid doesn't experience the fort as hiding from the performance. Are you kidding? The fort is the better game. The fort is where the good stuff is.

[PAUSE]

That's the whole reframe, you feel me? The backstage was never supposed to be the sad empty room where you recover from being a public person. Staffed right — even with just one chair — it's the playroom. The least guarded, most creative, most childlike room in your whole life.

And here's the part I didn't expect when I started living this: when the fort has somebody in it, the front stage changes. The show gets lighter. Because a performer with a real fort isn't performing for survival anymore — they're performing for joy. The stage becomes a place you visit to give something, instead of the only room in the house. The thousand and the one were never competing, bro. They're different rooms. You're allowed to own the whole house.

## CHAPTER 6: THE EMOTIONAL PART

[MUSIC FADES IN — one low, warm pad. Under this chapter only.]

And I'll go first. Like always.

You know I'm building a few things at once right now — the production company, the coaching, this show, the music. And all those versions are true, and all of them are mine — I built every one of those costumes on purpose. I'm not mad at any of it.

But there are weeks — and the only reason I can describe this as precisely as I'm about to is that I know these weeks from the inside — where I can feel the asymmetry building up like weather. You know those weeks where everything is warm? The DMs are kind, a client says thank you and means it, somebody quotes your own line back at you. A week absolutely full of appreciation. And then Sunday evening comes, and I'm sitting there with something hollow in my chest that has no business being there after a week like that. Like checking your bank account after payday and somehow it's empty.

[BEAT]

And when I finally got honest about it, here's what it was. Every single piece of that warmth came addressed to a version. The strategist got thanked. The host got encouraged. The brand got loved. All of them me — but none of them all of me. And it turns out you cannot assemble being seen out of being partially appreciated a thousand times. It doesn't add up. Wrong currency. It's like trying to get warm by collecting photographs of fires, bro. You can have a gorgeous collection. You will still be cold.

[PAUSE]

And then the harder confession underneath it, the one this episode dragged out of me: some part of me has preferred it this way. Because the thousand partial knowings are safe. Every audience holds a piece of me small enough that no piece can really hurt me. But the fort — one person, the whole file, the before-coffee version, the version that contradicts the content — that's the room with actual stakes in it. So I staffed every stage I own and left the fort empty, and I called it being busy. It wasn't busy. It was the library over the meal, every night, and then wondering why I was still hungry.

I'm renovating. That's all I'll say. Present tense. The fort's got a chair in it now. Some weeks, two.

[MUSIC FADES OUT — slow, under the next line.]

---

## CHAPTER 7: THE INVITATION

So here's the work this week — and in the spirit of the flip, it's light. This might genuinely be the lightest homework I've ever given on this show.

Show one person the unimpressive true thing.

Not the struggle — we did that episode already, you can go back, that was the trailer episode. I mean the silly true thing. The weird joy. The thing that doesn't fit the brand. The playlist that would end your credibility. The version of you that only exists alone in the car. And yes, I have mine — and no, you're not getting it today. That's the whole point of a fort, you feel me? But I've got mine, and you've got yours, and you know exactly which one I'm talking about because it came to mind the second I said it. THAT thing. Show one earned person that.

Because here's the secret about being seen that nobody tells you: it doesn't start with your wounds. It starts with your play. Kids know this — the secret language comes before the secrets. The fort gets built out of nonsense first, and then — only then — it can hold weight.

[BEAT]

And there's a line I want to leave you with — it's from the famous passage everybody reads at weddings and nobody reads slowly, because they're all waiting on the "love is patient" part and coast right past the ending: *for now we see only a reflection, as in a mirror; then, face to face. Now I know in part; then I'll know fully — even as I am fully known.*

Fully known. That phrase — and I keep turning it over — points at the exact thing you've been hungry for: the full knowing, no costume, no partial file, no version. Every audience knows you in part. The people in the fort know you whole. And every chair you put in that fort — the friend, the spouse, the person who gets the whole film — all of it is practice for being known all the way through, which is the only thing that ever actually fills the hollow.

So no — you were never actually unseen. Not for one day of your life. Now go staff the fort anyway. That's how we practice.

If that's the kind of conversation you've been needing — stay close. We're just getting started over here.

Thanks for being here. MindYourBizniz is a Palmer House conversation. New episode next week.

[ROOM TONE — the mug lifts. Out.]
