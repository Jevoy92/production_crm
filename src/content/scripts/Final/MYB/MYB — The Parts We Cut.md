What I want to do instead is investigate the *cut itself.* Why we do it. There's a piece of research with the most beautiful name I've ever heard in psychology — the beautiful mess effect — and it proves, with data, that you are wrong about how people see your unedited self. Wrong in a specific, measurable direction. I want to walk through it slow. And then I want to talk about what all this editing is quietly costing us — because I think it's the loneliest tax nobody itemizes.

[BEAT]

Let's sit with it.

## CHAPTER 2: THE MECHANISM

So first — why do we cut?

The fastest answer comes from a man named Leon Festinger, all the way back in 1954. He proposed something so obvious-sounding that it took a genius to write it down: human beings figure out how they're doing by *comparing themselves to other people.* We don't evaluate our lives against the truth. We evaluate them against the available footage of other lives. He called it social comparison theory, and it's been load-bearing in psychology for seventy years.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Festinger — 'A Theory of Social Comparison Processes,' 1954.]

Now hold that next to the world we actually live in. The available footage of other lives used to be your neighbors — full, unedited, three-dimensional people you saw on their bad days, in their driveways, in their arguments. Now? The available footage is everyone's *cut.* The trailer. The launch post, the anniversary photo, the gym selfie at the end of the weight loss — never the middle of it.

So the comparison machine Festinger described — the one running in your head all day, every day, since it was installed — is now doing its math with corrupted inputs. Your raw footage versus everyone else's highlight reel. Your Tuesday versus their trailer.

[PAUSE]

And what does that math return? *You're behind. You're the only one struggling. Everyone else figured something out that you didn't.*

And it's worse than that, because of a detail in Festinger's work that usually gets skipped: we don't compare evenly. We compare *up.* The brain's reference point isn't the average of everybody — it's the people doing a little better, or a lot better. Nobody scrolls past a struggling business and thinks "well, at least I'm ahead of them." We barely register those. We register the one crushing it. So the machine isn't just running on edited footage — it's running on the *best* edited footage available, selected for impressiveness, around the clock.

And so you do the rational thing, bro. You cut your footage too. Not to deceive anybody — to survive the comparison. You're not a liar. You're a contestant in a game where everyone submitted edits, so submitting your raw file feels like showing up to a gunfight with a diary.

And that's the loop. Everybody's cut makes everybody else cut deeper. The feed gets cleaner. The lives behind it don't. The distance between the two — that's where the loneliness lives.

---

## CHAPTER 3: THE STUDY YOU NEED TO HEAR

Okay. Now the research I promised — and I want you to actually hold this one, because it's not a vibe, it's a measured result.

2018. Three researchers — Anna Bruk, Sabine Scholl, Herbert Bless. They ran a series of studies on how people perceive vulnerability — real vulnerability. Admitting a mistake. Asking for help. Confessing feelings. Showing something unfinished. The stuff we cut.

They asked people two kinds of questions. One: imagine *you* showing this mess — how does it look? And two: imagine *someone else* showing you the same mess — how does it look?

[BEAT]

Same mess. Two answers. Completely different answers.

When people imagined their *own* vulnerability, they described it as weakness. Inadequacy. Something that would lower them in others' eyes. When they evaluated the *same act from someone else,* they called it courage. Honesty. Strength. They *admired* it.

The researchers named it the **beautiful mess effect.** Your mess looks beautiful — to everyone but you.

[PAUSE]

Sit with what that means. Every time you've cut something from your story because "people will think less of me" — the data says the audience was going to read it as brave. The harshest review of your raw footage comes from exactly one critic, and that critic has never once been in the audience. The critic lives in the editing room. The critic *is* you.

And there's a older cousin to this finding — 1966, a researcher named Elliot Aronson. He found that competent people who visibly blundered — the famous version is a spilled cup of coffee — became *more* likable, not less. They call it the pratfall effect. Sixty years of data, same direction: the human moment, on top of real substance, doesn't cost you standing. It buys you trust.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Aronson, Willerman & Floyd — the pratfall effect, Psychonomic Science, 1966.]

So the cut isn't protecting you. The cut is withholding the most likable, most trustworthy, most *human* material you have. We've been editing out the best parts and calling it professionalism.

## CHAPTER 4: THE FRIEND WHO PUSHES BACK

Now. The friend in my head has been waiting, and they've got a good one this time. I can hear them lean in:

"Jevoy. Be careful with this. Because some things *should* be cut. Not everybody at the dinner table has earned my raw footage. You want me to process my marriage on the internet? Tell every client about my cash flow? There's a word for people who share everything with everyone, and the word is not 'brave,' it's 'exhausting.' And honestly — half the 'vulnerability' out there is performance anyway. You said it yourself in the cold open. So which is it? Is the cut a sickness, or is the cut wisdom?"

[BEAT]

That's real. And I'm not going to dodge it, because the friend is more than half right.

Discernment is not the disease. Boundaries are not the disease. There's old wisdom about not casting your pearls before swine — about matching the depth of what you share to the trust that's actually in the room. And performed vulnerability — the kind with a ring light and a call to action — that's not the raw footage either. That's just a different edit. A cut that's dressed like an uncut.

So no — the answer is not "share everything with everyone." Here's the distinction I'd offer the friend, and it's the hinge of this whole episode:

The problem isn't *that* we cut. The problem is when there's **no room left where the raw footage plays.** Cut for the feed — fine. Cut for the clients — fine, that's the boundary work we talked about last time. But when you're also cutting for your best friend... your spouse... your small group... when the trailer is the only version *anyone* gets, including God, including the mirror — that's not discernment anymore. That's hiding, wearing discernment's jacket.

The healthy life isn't uncut. The healthy life has *at least one screening room* where the whole film runs, middles and all, for an audience that's earned it.

---

## CHAPTER 5: THE FLIP

So here's the flip — and the beautiful mess data is what makes it more than a nice thought.

We cut our stories because we think the middle is the *liability.* The struggle is the part that disqualifies us. The unfinishedness is the part that, if anyone saw it, would cost us respect, love, belonging.

But run the research backwards. The beautiful mess effect says observers read your mess as courage. The pratfall effect says your blunder, on top of your competence, makes you *more* trusted, not less. Festinger says everyone's comparison machine is starving on a diet of trailers.

Put those three together and you get something almost upside down: **the middle of your story is not your liability. The middle of your story is the part other people need most.**

[PAUSE]

Because nobody has ever been comforted by your highlight reel. Think about it. Nobody ever sat across from a trailer and felt less alone. The moments somebody's story actually reached into your chest — it was the middle. The "me too" parts. The parts that person almost cut.

Your edit is starving the exact people your story was supposed to feed.

## CHAPTER 6: THE EMOTIONAL PART

[MUSIC FADES IN — one low, warm pad. It sits under this chapter only.]

And I'll go first, since I'm the one holding the microphone.

I'm building things right now — you know this — a company, this show, all of it. And the version of that story I'm tempted to give you every week is the trailer. Momentum, lessons, wins with a bow on them. And the real footage has weeks in it that don't fit the trailer at all. Doubt that shows up at 2 AM asking for a meeting. Numbers that came in quieter than I prayed they would. The gap — anybody who's built anything knows this gap — between how it looks when I talk about it and how it feels when I'm in it alone.

[BEAT]

And here's what I've learned about that gap. Every time I cut the hard middle out of the story, the story gets more impressive — and I get more alone inside it. Because then the encouragement, when it comes, lands on the edit. The "you're killing it, bro" lands on the trailer. And the man in the middle — the one who actually needed it — never receives a thing. He wasn't in the version that got published.

There's a version of this where being watched makes us perform — the applause landing on the costume. But this is the same wound from the other side. There, the watching made us perform. Here, nobody's even watching — and we're *still* cutting. The editor stays late even when the audience went home. That's how deep this thing runs.

[PAUSE]

So the practice I'm working on — and I say working, present tense — is one screening room. A few people who get the whole film. Unscored, uncolor-corrected, middles intact. Not the internet. Not the clients. Just a room where the comparison machine finally gets honest footage — in both directions. Because the relief, it turns out, wasn't on the other side of a better trailer. It was on the other side of one room where I didn't need one.

[MUSIC FADES OUT — slow, under the next line.]

---

## CHAPTER 7: THE INVITATION

So here's the work this week, and it's small, and it's not for the feed.

One person. One part of the story you'd normally cut. Not your darkest scene — this isn't shock therapy — just one honest frame from the middle. The "actually, this month was hard." The "I don't know if it's working yet." Say it to somebody who's earned a seat. And then — this is the part I really want you to notice — watch their face. The data says you'll see the opposite of what the editor in your head predicted. You'll see them lean *in.* Half the time, you'll watch them open their own raw file right back. That's the beautiful mess effect, live, at your dinner table.

[BEAT]

There's a verse I kept circling while writing this. Paul — a man with every credential worth bragging on — writes to a church about a weakness he begged God to remove. And the answer he says he got back was: *"My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness."* And then this man — the most quoted résumé in the New Testament — says: fine. *Then I will boast in my weaknesses.*

Boast. In the deleted scenes. He flipped the edit two thousand years before the data caught up: the cut footage wasn't where his shame lived. It was where the power got in.

[PAUSE]

Maybe that's the whole episode. The middle of your story isn't the part that disqualifies you. It's the part that makes you reachable — by people, and maybe by grace too. The trailer impresses. The film connects. And you only get one of those by leaving the middle in.

If that's the kind of conversation you've been needing — stay close. We're just getting started over here.

Thanks for being here. MindYourBizniz is a Palmer House conversation. New episode next week.

[ROOM TONE — the mug lifts. Out.]
