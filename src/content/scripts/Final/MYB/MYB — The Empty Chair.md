# MINDYOURBIZNIZ — "The Empty Chair: What People Assume When You Say Nothing"
## Solo Podcast Monologue · ~15 min · Standalone

---

[COLD OPEN — room tone. A ceramic mug set down on wood. A breath.]

**JEVOY:**

You sent the text an hour ago.

You watched the three little dots appear — they're typing, they're about to answer — and then the dots just... vanished. No reply. Your message sitting there. Delivered. Read. Silent.

And in that silence, what does your brain do? It doesn't shrug. It doesn't move on. It *writes.* "Are they mad at me? Did I say something wrong? Was it the joke from yesterday? Are they done with me?" You build an entire story out of nothing. Out of a blank screen.

[BEAT]

Here's the part nobody says out loud, and it's the whole episode. That exact thing you just did to a blank text? Your customers are doing it to your silence. Your audience is doing it. The people quietly deciding whether to trust you with their money are doing it right now. When you go quiet, they don't put their opinion of you on pause. They *finish the story for you.* And the ending they write is almost never the one you'd have chosen.

[MUSIC FADES IN — a low, slow, barely-there pad.]

---

Picture a meeting. Five people around a table, waiting on a sixth. The chair stays empty. Five minutes. Ten. Still empty.

And watch what the room does — because nobody just lets an empty chair be an empty chair. The explanations start rolling in. "He's probably stuck in traffic, you know how that road gets." Then a beat later: "Honestly, he's been overwhelmed lately, he probably forgot." Then, a little colder: "You know what — I don't think he really wanted to be on this thing anyway. He seemed checked out last time."

Ten minutes. That's all it took for an empty chair to go from a scheduling hiccup to a verdict on the man's entire character. He wasn't even in the building. He got no chance to explain. And the room had already written him — assigned the motive, dug up the past behavior, reached the conclusion. All of it built on nothing. All of it built on a chair.

That's what silence is. It's an empty chair. And human beings cannot leave an empty chair alone. We have to put somebody in it.

So if you're a business owner, a creator, anyone with something to build — and you're not showing up, not speaking, not putting your voice out there — understand what you're actually doing. You're not "staying private." You're leaving an empty chair in a room full of people, and walking out, and letting them decide who sits in it.

You might think you're just taking a breather. Just heads-down on client work. Just "moving in silence." But the market doesn't get your memo. The market just sees the empty chair — and it fills it with whatever's most convenient. And convenient is rarely kind.

---

So why do we do this to each other? Why can't we just see nothing and think nothing?

It's not cruelty. It's wiring. Your brain is a meaning-making machine, and it cannot tolerate a blank. Show it something that happened, it explains it. Show it *nothing* happening, it explains that too. We are physically unable to look at a gap and just say "I don't know why." We have to assign a reason. Psychologists have a whole name for the reflex — but you already know it, because you ran it on those three dots ten minutes ago.

And there's a second gear stacked on top of it, and this is the one that costs you. We don't just fill the blank — we fill it *dark.* We hate not-knowing so much that we'll invent a bad story just to have a story at all. Because a bad story at least has an ending. The unknown doesn't. So given the choice between "I have no idea what's going on with them" and "something's wrong," the brain grabs "something's wrong" every single time. It's more comfortable. It closes the loop.

So when you hand people nothing, they don't hold a warm, patient, neutral space for you. They reach for the nearest explanation lying around — and the nearest one is usually shaped like their own worst fear. If somebody once got burned by a contractor who ghosted them, and then you, their contractor, go quiet for a week — they are not picturing you bent over their blueprints. They're picturing the last guy. Your silence becomes the screen they project their worst memory onto. You didn't write that story. But you left the screen blank, and somebody had to fill it.

And here's the cruelest part of the whole arrangement — you never even find out. Nobody emails you to say, "hey, I noticed you'd gone quiet, figured you'd folded, so I went with somebody else." That verdict gets reached in total silence, on the other side of a screen, filed away, and you never hear the gavel come down. The person who needed exactly what you do scrolls past your three-year-old muffin photo, feels a little "eh, are they even still around," and quietly books the louder option instead. You didn't lose that one in a fair fight. You lost it in an empty chair, to a story you weren't in the room to argue with. That's the real tax on silence, and nobody warns you about it: it's not that people think badly of you. It's that they decide — fully, confidently, permanently — and you never even get the invitation to defend yourself.

---

Let me give you the picture that made this click for me.

Imagine an unclaimed package sitting on a porch. Just a plain brown box. No movement, no label you can read from the street. Just sitting there.

Day one, you walk past and think nothing. "A delivery. Somebody ordered something." Totally neutral.

Day three, same box, still there. Now you start to wonder. "Are they out of town? Did they not see it?"

Day six, still there — and the story has completely changed. "Something's wrong. Did they move? Is somebody hurt? Is that house abandoned?"

Here's what gets me. The box never changed. The box did *nothing.* It is the exact same box it was on day one. But the meaning of the box rotted — purely because of time passing with no action.

Your silence is that box. The longer it sits, the worse the story gets, all on its own, with zero input from you. Week one, "they're busy." Month two, "they're struggling." Month six, "they're gone." And the part that should scare you: you could be doing the best work of your life in that exact stretch. Building something incredible. But if you're not saying so, the story on the porch is rotting while the truth inside the house is thriving. Nobody outside can see the inside. They can only read the box.

---

Now — I've got a friend in my head, and right about here he starts pushing back. Maybe you've got the same one.

He says: "Come on. Sometimes silence is wise. Sometimes you put your head down and let the work talk. Real ones move in silence — I'm not gonna be one of these people posting every time they drink a glass of water. I want my work to speak for itself."

And I respect that. There's real dignity in quiet work. There's a season for the back room, where you're just building and not announcing. I'm not telling you to turn your life into a livestream or do little dances to prove you exist.

But here's the line my friend keeps missing. Silence only works as a strategy when you've already banked enough trust to cover it.

Think about it like this. If a giant company everybody knows goes dark for six months, nobody assumes they died. You assume they're cooking up the next big thing. Why? Because they've got a reservoir — years of evidence sitting behind the silence, doing the talking for them. They earned the right to be quiet.

But if you're early? If you're launching, if you've got a handful of clients, if barely anyone knows your name yet — you don't have that reservoir. There's nothing behind the silence to hold your place. So when you go quiet, you don't look like a genius in the lab. You look like a ghost. You cannot borrow against trust you haven't built yet. If you want the luxury of moving in silence *later*, you have to do the loud, unglamorous work of being visible *now.*

And that reservoir does not fill in one big splash. It fills the boring way — one drop at a time. One post nobody seems to notice. One update sent to four people. One real answer to one question in some comment section at the bottom of the internet. It feels like nothing the day you do it — the same way a single drop of water feels like nothing. But that is exactly how the reservoir behind the silence gets built. Not in a flood. In a drip. The people who supposedly "earned the right to be quiet" did not earn it quietly. They earned it by being loud on a hundred ordinary days when it felt completely pointless — until one day there was finally enough behind them to hold the silence up.

---

So what do we actually do with this?

The flip is realizing that visibility was never about ego. It's about evidence.

We get it twisted. We think showing up means standing on a chair yelling "look at me," and that feels gross, arrogant, needy — so we don't. But that's not the job. The job isn't "look at me." It's "look at *this.*" Here's what I'm building. Here's how I think about your problem. Here's the thing I made. That's not bragging. That's handing somebody the evidence to form an accurate opinion instead of a convenient one.

Because here's the hard truth: you cannot control what people think of you. You will never control that. But you have total control over the *material* they think *with.* And if you don't give them material, they don't stop forming an opinion — they just build it out of scraps. They'll use the website you haven't touched since 2019, the one with the placeholder text still on the About page. They'll use that one photo from a networking thing where you got caught mid-blink. They'll use the fact that the last thing you posted was a picture of a muffin, three years ago. That becomes you. That becomes the whole chair.

Give them better material. That's it. That's the move. And it doesn't mean confessing everything or living out loud — it means leaving enough evidence that the story builds itself in the right direction. If you're a strategist, show how you think. If you build things, show how you build.

And listen — "evidence" is so much smaller than you're picturing. You're imagining a documentary, a viral post, some big polished reveal. It's not that. It's a photo of the thing half-finished on your desk with one honest sentence about why it's hard. It's thirty seconds of you explaining a mistake you almost made this week. It's a before-and-after of one small thing you fixed. Each one is a single brick. Not one of them has to be impressive on its own — they just have to exist, and stack, so that when somebody finally goes looking for who you are, they find a wall instead of a blank wall they have to imagine for themselves. You don't have to fill every silence. You just can't leave the chair empty in the rooms that decide your future.

---

[MUSIC swells slightly — warmer.]

And here's the part that actually gets me, because I know why most of us go quiet. And it's not that we've got nothing to say.

It's fear. We're scared of saying the wrong thing. Scared of being misunderstood. Scared of the one comment, the one wrong read, the one person who decides we're stupid. So we say nothing — and we tell ourselves nothing is safe.

I feel that one in my chest, honestly. I'm building right now — out loud, in public — and there are days the easiest thing on earth would be to go quiet until it's all "perfect." Days I look at something I made and think: is this good enough, what if somebody tears it apart, what if I look like I don't fully know what I'm doing yet.

But here's the trap, and it's airtight: when you go silent out of fear of being misunderstood, you *guarantee* the misunderstanding. You were scared they'd get you wrong — so you gave them nothing — so they filled the blank with their own fear — so they got you wrong. The silence didn't protect you from the thing you feared. It *delivered* it.

Your voice — unpolished, still figuring itself out, mid-blink and imperfect — is worth more than the silence. Every time. Because people don't actually bond with the finished, flawless version. They bond with the one who said "here's what I'm working out right now, come along." That kind of honesty builds more trust than a perfect front ever could. People connect with progress. They connect with a person. And being a person, out loud, requires a voice.

---

[MUSIC OUT. Room tone returns.]

Here's the thing I keep coming back to, and then I'll let you go.

Every single thing humans have ever built started the exact same way. Not with a finished product — with somebody saying it out loud first, before it was safe to. The bridge was a sentence before it was steel. The company was a sentence. The friendship, the team, the whole movement — all of it started as words breaking a silence. Somebody stood in front of a blank and said "here's what I see, here's what I'm making" — and *that*, the saying, is what started it. Nothing real gets built in silence. The blank doesn't organize itself. Somebody has to speak first.

That's the chair. An empty chair always gets filled — that part is not up to you. The only thing you actually get to decide is whether *you* fill it, or whether you walk away and leave it to a roomful of strangers reaching for the most convenient guess they've got.

[PAUSE]

So here's what I'll leave you with — with you, not at you.

This week, break one silence. Post the one thing. Send the one update. Say the one sentence about what you're actually building. It does not have to be polished — it has to *exist.* Then come down to the comments and tell me one line: what story had the silence been quietly writing for you — and what did you put in the chair instead? That's the comment I'm waiting on, and I'll be down there reading them, because I'm filling my own chairs right now too.

And the share. You know someone who's gone quiet — who's "moving in silence" and calling it strategy, while the market quietly writes them off as gone. Send them this. Tell them the chair is already getting filled. The only question left is who's holding the pen.

So fill the chair. Say the first sentence. Then say the next one.

[ROOM TONE — the mug lifts. Out.]
