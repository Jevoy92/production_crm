# MINDYOURBIZNIZ — "The Platform and the Train"
## (take on "Context Doesn't Travel") · Solo Podcast Monologue | ~14 min · ~2,600 spoken words
## The Recorded Animal · Theme 12 · Approach: SINGLE-IMAGE / refrain · Delivery: hushed, second-person, slow, music under most of it
### Voice-calibrated: bro-ban ✓ plain language ✓ friend woven softly ✓ no verdict ✓ one returning image ✓ faith quiet ✓ actionable CTA ✓ · One anchor image, returned to like a chorus

---

[COLD OPEN]
[ROOM TONE — very close, very warm. Almost a whisper. One low pad already breathing underneath, the whole way.]

**JEVOY:**

I want to put you on a train platform.

[BEAT]

It's quiet. Late. You're standing there, and a train is pulling away. And on that train is one sentence. One thing you said. Out loud, on a day you don't even remember, into a camera or a microphone or just a room with a phone in it.

The sentence is leaving. And you're still on the platform.

[BEAT]

Stay there with me. Don't leave that platform. Because the whole episode happens right here — in the space between the sentence that's leaving, and the rest of you, the part that explains it, the part that meant it — standing still, watching it go.

That sentence is going to travel. Tonight, maybe. To people who weren't in the room. People who don't know your voice. People who never heard what you said right before it, or the gentle thing you said right after.

The clip will travel. The context won't. It stays on the platform with you.

[BEAT]

Here's the question I'll leave on the platform, and we'll come back for it. When that sentence reaches a stranger — stripped of everything that made it true — who do they think you are? And the real you, the one still standing here with the whole story... where does that one go?

[BEAT]

You know this feeling, even if you've never had a clip go anywhere. It's older than the internet. It's the sting of someone taking a small piece of you — a comment, a choice, one rushed moment — and drawing a conclusion so far off it would be funny, if it didn't hurt so much. They slot you into a box that doesn't fit. Never fit. And you stand there, holding the rest of the sentence, watching them be sure about a person who isn't you.

That ache has a mechanism. Let me show it to you, gently, so it stops feeling like something wrong with you.

---

I have to tell you about a researcher named danah boyd. She spells her name in lowercase, on purpose, which I respect. And she named the thing that's happening to your sentence right now.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: danah boyd — "context collapse," 2008.]

Around 2008, she described what she called context collapse. And it's simple, if you let it be.

You used to have a different version of yourself for every room. A you for your work. A you for your closest friends. A you for your grandmother. Each room had its own quiet rules. You knew them in your body. You knew what landed at the dinner table and what landed at midnight with the people who really know you.

[BEAT]

Then the walls came down. All at once.

Now the people from every room stand in the same room. They all see the same clip, at the same time. Your boss and your best friend and a stranger in another country. That's context collapse. One thing you said, made for one room, suddenly heard by everyone.

And here's what you feel on the platform. Each of those people brings their own ears. Their own history. Their own bad day. The friend who'd laugh doesn't have the setup. The stranger doesn't have the wink. They just have the words. Bare. On the train. With nobody standing beside them to say, *no — here's what I meant.*

You've felt this in something as small as a text. You write one line to a friend who knows your heart. They read it warm, because they have the room. Then someone screenshots it. Sends it on. And now a person who doesn't know you reads the exact same words and hears something cold, something you never meant. Same words. Different room. The room was doing all the work, and you never even saw it.

That's the first thing the camera quietly took from you. It took the rooms. And without the room, your context has nowhere to ride.

---

There's a second thing, and it's almost unkind how fast it works.

In 2006, two researchers at Princeton — Janine Willis and Alexander Todorov — showed people faces. Strangers. They flashed each face for a tenth of a second. One hundred milliseconds.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Willis & Todorov — "First Impressions: 100-ms Exposure to a Face," Psychological Science, 2006.]

And in that sliver, people had already decided. Is this person trustworthy. Are they kind. Do I like them. A verdict. Faster than a blink.

[PAUSE]

And here's the part that should stop you. When other people got all the time they wanted to look, the slow judgments matched the fast ones. Basically, more time didn't make anyone fairer. It just made them more certain of the call they'd already made in a tenth of a second.

So picture the stranger receiving your sentence. They don't go looking for the platform. They don't ask what came before. Their brain makes a snap call — *oh, that's who she is* — and then, quietly, it spends the rest of the day collecting proof it was right.

You weren't slowly understood and gently judged. You were decided, in a tenth of a second, by someone who never met you. That's not cruelty. That's just a tired brain in a flood of fragments. But it lands on you like cruelty. Because it's you on that train.

---

Now there's a friend I have to let onto the platform. He's been tugging my sleeve this whole time. Let him talk.

"You're being soft," he says, gently. "If you just tell the truth, the context shouldn't matter. People should do the work. They should find the whole conversation before they decide who you are. If your heart is right, that's on them, not on you."

[PAUSE]

And — quietly — part of me loves him for that. Part of me wants to live in his world so badly. A world where everyone is patient. Everyone is curious. Everyone reads the whole paragraph before they sentence you to a conclusion.

I wish we lived there. We don't.

Because his beautiful argument is about what people *should* do. And the two studies just told us what people *actually* do. And you can't build a life on *should.* You have to build it on *do.*

He's right that the stranger is the one doing wrong. Of course they are. But being right doesn't bring the context back. Being right doesn't catch the train. You can stand on that platform, completely honest, completely innocent, and completely misunderstood. Your innocence doesn't travel either.

So both things are true at once. The person clipping you is wrong. And waiting for the world to stop is a plan that has never worked. Two true things, holding hands. That's usually where the real answer is hiding.

---

So come back to the platform, because this is the part I most need you to hear, and I'll say it soft.

For years, the move was to chase the train. To yell after it. *Wait — you don't understand — let me explain.* To be your own lawyer, your own PR firm, your own lifelong archivist, running down the tracks trying to staple the context back on.

And it never worked. You were standing in a hurricane, telling the wind to calm down.

[PAUSE]

You cannot make people seek your context. Not because they're cruel. Because their feed is endless and their brain is full and you are one fragment in a flood. Telling them to dig is telling someone underwater to take notes.

So you stop chasing the train. And you do the only thing that ever works. You build the context *into* yourself.

[MUSIC SWELLS, just slightly.]

You become so clearly, so consistently *you* that even a fragment carries the whole of you. You make the smallest piece of you tell the truth about you. So that when twenty seconds of you reaches a stranger — ripped out, handed over, with no platform attached — it still, somehow, sounds like you.

That's the move. You don't get to hold the scissors. You never will. You stop handing people scissors and begging them not to cut. Instead you become someone the scissors can't lie about.

And this isn't about being loud, or polished, or saying the perfect thing every time. It's quieter than that. It's saying the same true thing, in the same true way, enough times that people start to know the shape of you. So that when one piece floats off without you, the people who matter fill in the rest on their own. They've met you enough that a fragment can't replace you. You stop being a string of disconnected clips. You become a person with a thread running through, and the thread is the context. You carry your own platform now.

[MUSIC settles back under.]

---

And I'll go first, like always.

I've been the one running down the tracks. I've felt that specific, helpless heat — knowing the truth, holding the whole clip in my hand, and watching the fragment get there first, wearing my face, saying things I'd spent the whole sentence arguing against. I learned that being right is not a delivery service. Nobody comes to pick it up.

And — if I'm honest — I've also been the stranger. I've seen a fragment of someone and decided who they were in a tenth of a second, and scrolled on, and never once thought about the platform where their context was standing, watching me get it wrong.

We're all on the train sometimes. And we're all on the platform, watching someone else's sentence leave without them. Same little screen. All day.

[PAUSE]

---

So here's the gentle work, and notice it goes both directions.

First, for the you that gets clipped. Build a room. Not a stage — a room. Keep a place, and a few people, where the whole paragraph of your life gets read. Where nobody's working from the clip. Where you don't have to explain yourself, because they were already there for the setup and they'll stay for the part that comes after. A room where you can say the messy first half of a sentence and trust that someone will wait for the second half.

Because you cannot stay whole as a person who is only ever known in fragments. It hollows you out. You start performing the clip-safe version of yourself everywhere, even at home, even alone. You start editing the sentence before you've said it. Some of you have been doing this for years. That low, constant tiredness you could never quite name — that's it. That's the cost of running down the tracks after every train, trying to staple your context back on for people who were never going to read it anyway.

And second, for the you that does the clipping. The next time a fragment hands you an easy verdict on a human being — go find the platform. Read the paragraph. Be the person you're hoping finds *your* twenty seconds.

[BEAT]

There's an old line I kept circling while I made this. It's six words, and it's two thousand years older than the algorithm. *Stop judging by appearances, and judge correctly.*

And notice — it doesn't say stop judging. It says judge *correctly.* Go get the whole story before you sentence the person. Judging by fragments isn't new. The clip economy didn't invent it. It just industrialized it. And the people who keep this old discipline are going to be the sanest people you know.

[PAUSE]

The train's almost gone now. Your sentence is on it. You're still on the platform, holding the rest of the story — the part that's true, the part that traveled without you.

[BEAT]

You can't run after it. But you can be the one who reads the whole paragraph next time. The one nobody can reduce, because you refused to reduce anyone else.

---

So here's the only thing I'll ask of you, two small things.

This week, the next time a clip hands you a verdict on somebody — a stranger, a public person, someone having their worst nine seconds in your feed — don't pile on. Go find the full thing. Watch the boring part the clipper cut. And then come down to the comments and tell me, in one line, what changed when you read the whole paragraph. Not for me. For you. But I'll be down there reading every one, because I'm still learning this too.

And if someone you love just got reduced to their worst fragment — clipped, misread, decided-on by people who never met them — don't argue with the crowd. Just send them this. Sometimes that's the kindest way to say: *I read the whole paragraph. I still know who you are.*

[ROOM TONE — the pad fades. A breath. Out.]
