# MINDYOURBIZNIZ — "The Clean Lens"
## Solo Podcast Monologue | ~14 min | hushed, second-person, one returning image
## The Recorded Animal · Approach: SINGLE-IMAGE / refrain (the smudge on the lens) · Delivery: quiet, slow, music under most of it
### Voice-calibrated QA: bro-ban ✓ plain language (one idea/sentence) ✓ dual-audience "basically" ✓ sensory beats ✓ friend woven softly ✓ no verdict ✓ one landing ✓ faith quiet, woven ✓ actionable CTA ✓ · One anchor image, returned to like a chorus

---

[COLD OPEN]
[ROOM TONE — very close, very warm. Almost a whisper. One low pad breathing underneath, the whole way.]

**JEVOY:**

I want you to pick up your phone. Don't unlock it. Just turn it over.

[BEAT]

Look at the little camera lens. Actually look at it. There's a smudge on it. There's always a smudge on it. A thumbprint. A little grease from your face, your pocket, your lunch.

And every photo you take this week passes through that. Through a thin film of yesterday. Then you look at the picture and it's a little hazy, a little soft. And you blame the light. You blame the phone. You never think to wipe the glass.

[BEAT]

Stay there with me. Hold that smudge in your mind, because we're going to come back to it the whole way through. That little film of grease is the whole episode. Because the thing you keep trying to fix about how people see you — it was never the light. It was the lens.

[BEAT]

And I'll say the quiet part out loud, because I don't think anybody's said it to you this way. You've been told the reason your content isn't working is that you need a better camera. A better hook. A better edit. A more interesting personality. None of that is the thing. The thing is a smudge most people never think to wipe — and once you see it, you can't unsee it, and your stuff starts landing inside a week.

---

Here's a quiet thing I keep noticing. I'll be scrolling — just looking at how businesses present themselves. Pages, reels, websites.

And I kept feeling this same fog.

The videos were beautiful. The color was perfect. You could feel the effort coming off the screen. Somebody sweated over every frame. It was all so clever. So creative.

And after two whole minutes on one page, I realized something that scared me. I had no idea what they actually did. None.

I didn't know the problem they solved. I didn't know who it was for. I didn't know how to hire them, or why I'd want to. They'd spent everything trying to be admired. And they forgot to be understood.

That's a smudge. It's just not on the glass. It's on the message.

[BEAT]

And maybe — gently — it's on yours too. The way you describe what you do. The way you answer when someone asks. The smudge you can't see, because it's on the side you're looking *through.*

---

## THE MECHANISM

I have to tell you about a study. I'll keep it quiet, I promise. Two psychologists, Rolf Reber and Norbert Schwarz. They spent years on something called processing fluency.
[SOURCE CARD: Reber & Schwarz — processing fluency / judgments of truth.]

Basically — fluency just means how easy something is for your brain to take in. Easy in, easy to trust. That's the whole thing.

But here's the part I can't put down. When something is easy to understand, your brain doesn't just find it easier. It decides it's more *true.* More trustworthy. More real. Easy to understand quietly becomes "I believe you."

They showed it with something tiny. They took the same exact sentence. Printed it in a clean, easy font, and in a hard, messy one. Same words. People rated the easy one as more true. Nothing changed but the smudge on the glass.

[BEAT]

So sit with that for a second, softly. When you make someone work to understand you, you're not just tiring them out. You're quietly lowering how much they trust you. Every second of squinting feels, in their body, like a small risk. And the heart does with risk what it always does. It backs away.

The clean lens was never just prettier. It was more *believed.* And almost nobody is wiping it, because everyone's too busy buying a better camera.

---

## THE SONG IN YOUR HEAD

There's a second smudge, and it's the one you and I can't see, because it's ours.

Let me show you with something small. Picture yourself tapping a song on a table. Happy Birthday, say. Just the rhythm. And someone across from you has to guess it.

In your head, the whole song is playing. The melody, the words, the off-key uncle. It's so obvious you can't believe they don't hear it. But all they hear is knocking on wood.

A researcher named Elizabeth Newton ran exactly this, back in 1990. The tappers guessed half the listeners would get it. The real number was about one in forty.
[SOURCE CARD: Elizabeth Newton — "tappers and listeners," Stanford, 1990.]

Because the tapper can hear the music. And they can't un-hear it. They know the song, so they forget what it's like to not know it.

There's a name for that. Some economists called it the curse of knowledge, back in 1989. Basically — once you know a thing, you lose the memory of not knowing it.
[SOURCE CARD: Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber — "the curse of knowledge," 1989.]

[BEAT]

That's you, talking about the thing you love. You know your work cold. You know the words, the backstory, the reason behind the reason. So when you explain it, you skip the simple part. It feels obvious. It feels boring to say out loud.

So you don't say the plain true sentence. You reach for the bigger words, because the plain one feels too small for how hard you worked. And you walk away thinking you sounded deep. You were tapping a song on a table, alone, wondering why no one sang along.

That's the smudge. And you can't see it. None of us can see our own.

[BEAT]

And here's the part I want to sit in for a second, because it's quieter and it's truer. This isn't only about how you sell something. It's about how you're *known.*

Think about the last time someone asked how you were really doing. And you reached for the foggy answer. "Busy. You know. A lot going on." A smudge. You had something true and specific right there — "I'm scared I'm not going to make it" — and you wiped a little grease over the lens instead, because the clear thing felt like too much. We do it with the people closest to us. We hand them the hazy version of ourselves and then feel lonely that nobody really sees us.

That's the same smudge. It's just not on a product page. It's on you. And the cost isn't a lost sale. It's a person who loves you, squinting, trying to make out who's actually in there.

---

## THE FRIEND

Now there's a friend I have to let in. He loves beautiful work the way I do. And he's been tugging my sleeve this whole time, so let him talk.

"You're about to tell everyone to be boring," he says. "And it's not even true. There's research. In 2011 they gave students harder-to-read material, ugly fonts, real friction. And those students *remembered more.* Sometimes a little difficulty is good. The stuff that sticks is a little weird, a little hard. Don't you tell people to sand off every interesting edge they have."

And — quietly — he's right. I won't bury that. A little friction can make a thing stick. The line you quote from your favorite song is rarely the simplest one.

[PAUSE]

So how can both be true? Easy wins. And hard wins.

Because they're not talking about the same moment. The easy lens wins at the *door* — the first second, where a stranger decides if you're worth any effort at all. The good kind of friction pays off *inside* — after they already trust you, already chose to stay. One gets them in. The other gives them something to chew on once they're in the room. The clarity doesn't kill the art. The clarity buys the ticket for it.

---

## THE FLIP

So come back to the lens, because this is the part I most need you to hear, and I'll say it gently.

You don't have a creativity problem. You have a clarity problem. You've been polishing the light — better video, prettier words, a cleverer way to say it — when the thing in the way was a smudge on the glass the whole time.

And when I get honest about my own smudge, it isn't about art at all. It's fear. I dress the plain truth up in big words because I'm scared the plain truth isn't impressive enough. I'd rather be admired and misunderstood than understood and ordinary.

That's the grease on the lens. It's ego, wearing a nice coat, calling itself craft.

[BEAT]

And here's the part that actually moved the needle for me, when I finally heard it said plain. When you're trying to sound impressive, every single thought in your head is *me, me, me.* What will they think of me. Did that land. Did I look smart. There is no room in that head for the person on the other side. The second you stop trying to be admired and start trying to be *understood,* the camera turns around. The attention goes out instead of in. And the weird thing is, that's also when the nerves drop — because you finally have a job to do that isn't about you.

[BEAT]

Real trust was never built by sounding smart. It's built by being understood. By one clear sentence somebody gets in a single breath, and repeats to a friend. Creativity gets you attention. Clarity gives people sight.

Think about it like a good meal. I love when a chef knows how to use spices. But creativity is the seasoning. Clarity is the meal. And nobody — no matter how hungry — wants to sit down and be served a plate of pure paprika. You have to give them something to actually eat first. The plain, filling, true thing. Then the subtle notes can do their work. We keep serving people seasoning and wondering why they leave the table still hungry.

And here's the quiet irony. The moment you stop trying to look smart, people start trusting you more. You become the one page in the feed that makes sense. And people always come back to the place that makes sense.

There's a kind of rest in it too. If you've been running the treadmill of trying to be the cleverest, the most polished, the most impressive — you're tired. That bar never stops moving. There's always a better camera, a sharper line, a newer trend. But clarity isn't a treadmill. You don't have to be a genius every time you speak. You just have to be a translator. Take the true thing that's locked inside you, and hand it over plainly, so the person who needs it can actually pick it up. That's the whole job. And it's a lighter job than the one you've been doing.

---

[MUSIC SWELLS, just slightly.]

I'll tell you the one that caught me. Somebody asked me at dinner what I do. And I watched myself reach for the cinematic version — "we craft narrative-driven brand worlds." And I saw her eyes do that polite, glazed nod.

And I caught it. I stopped. I said, "We make videos that get small businesses customers." And she lit up. "Oh — my brother needs that." One sentence. The whole conversation changed the second I wiped the glass.

And that's not a story from a book. That's a Tuesday. That same week, two people I'd been talking to for months — who I'd been describing my company to with all the cinematic words — finally booked. Not because anything about the work changed. Because I finally said the plain sentence out loud and let them decide. The smudge had been on my side of the glass the whole time, and the second I wiped it, the people I'd been trying to reach could finally see me.

Clarity isn't dumbing it down. It's the most love you can show your own work — finally letting it be *seen.*

[BEAT]

And turn the phone back over for a second. Look at that little lens again. Here's the gentle truth — the smudge isn't a character flaw. It's just what happens when you carry something through your days. You're going to fog it again next week. So will I. The point was never to have a permanently spotless lens. The point is to *keep a rag nearby.* To check the glass before you blame the light. To make wiping it a small, regular, humble habit, instead of a thing you only do in a crisis. The clearest people aren't the ones who never smudge. They're the ones who wipe early, and often, and without shame.

[MUSIC settles back under.]

---

## THE INVITATION

So here's what I'd ask of you. Two small things. And neither one is "stop being creative." I'd never. It's a balance.

This week, find your one sentence. What you do, who it's for, no big words. Read it out loud to someone who isn't in your head — and watch their face, not their manners. The glaze never lies. That's you, finally hearing your own song the way they hear it.

And don't only do it with your work. Do it with your life. The next time someone asks how you're really doing, try handing them the clear thing instead of the foggy one. One true sentence. "I'm tired and I'm a little scared, but I'm okay." Watch what happens. Watch them lean in instead of nodding past you. Most of the loneliness we carry isn't because no one's there. It's because we keep handing people the smudged version of ourselves, and then wondering why we feel unseen. The clean lens isn't just better marketing. It's how you let yourself be known.

And keep the friction for *inside.* Once someone's in, then go deep. Then get weird and artful and hard. Difficulty is a gift for the people who already trust you. Never a toll at the door.

[BEAT]

There's an old line I keep circling. A vision of the highest stakes imaginable, and the instruction attached to it wasn't "make it grand." It was "make it plain — so the one who reads it can run." Plain. So they can *move* on it. Even the biggest thing anyone ever had to say came with that one quiet note: don't make them squint.

[BEAT]

So here's the only thing I'll really ask. This week, wipe one lens. Pick the foggiest thing you own — your bio, your homepage, the way you answer "what do you do." Strip it to the plain, true sentence. Then come down to the comments and tell me, in one line, what changed when someone finally got it. Not for me. For you. But I'll be down there reading every one, because I'm still wiping my own lens too, and I need to know it's possible.

And if someone you love is brilliant and impossible to understand — don't correct them. Just send them this. Sometimes that's the kindest way to hand a person a clean rag.

[ROOM TONE — the pad fades. A breath. Out.]
