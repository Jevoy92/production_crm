## OPEN — THE TEMPTATION

[VISUAL: Cold open, no music. A laptop screen. A cursor blinks in an "About Us" draft. It types "Our team of experts…" Pause. Delete. Types "I am a…" Pause. Delete. Types "We…" Cursor blinks. Cut to Jevoy in the Palmer House studio.]

Picture a founder at a laptop at midnight. And let's be honest — this founder has been most of us. She's hovering over the most loaded word in small business.

"We."

Two letters. Heaviest word on the page. She's a company of one. Maybe one and a half. Her cousin does the books. And every instinct says type "we." "We deliver." "Our team." "Our offices" — which is a desk in the spare room, with the dog asleep under it.

She's not trying to scam anybody. She's trying to survive something she's already felt in her gut. When she says "I," the client's voice changes. When she says "we," the quote gets approved.

So here's tonight's question. And I'm going to fight it properly, both sides at full strength, because it deserves it. No strawmen in this building. **Should your business look bigger than it is?**

Every founder has stood at this fork. Most picked a side without ever hearing the full case for the other one. Tonight you hear both.

And before you answer too fast, know two things. One — the science behind "look bigger" is real. There's a hundred years of psychology behind it. I'll show you the receipts, including the study that found tall people collect almost eight hundred dollars a year per inch, for nothing. Two — the case against it is also real. And it's not the moral lecture you're expecting. It's a business case, with teeth. Two rounds. A real fight. Let's go.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Judge & Cable — 'The Effect of Physical Height on Workplace Success and Income,' J. Applied Psychology, 2004.]

## ROUND ONE — THE CASE FOR LOOKING BIGGER

[VISUAL: On screen: ROUND 1. The "pro" corner.]

The case for looking bigger opens with biology. Because the bias it's using isn't a marketing trick. It's wiring.

In 2004, two researchers ran the famous height study. Judge and Cable. Basically, every inch of height is worth about eight hundred dollars a year in extra pay. They controlled for age, gender, and weight. Tall people get paid more. Promoted more. Seen as more leader-like. And there's zero evidence they actually perform better.

It goes all the way up. In his book *Blink*, Malcolm Gladwell surveyed Fortune 500 CEOs. About 58% are six feet or taller. In the general male population, it's 14.5%. So the conclusion is hard to dodge. People pay real money for size signals that have nothing to do with substance. The brain reads "big" as "safe, established, capable." It's been doing that since following the biggest one in the group kept you alive.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Malcolm Gladwell — Blink (Fortune 500 CEO height survey), 2005.]

Now translate that to business, says Round One. Your company has a height too. And clients read it in seconds, the same way they read a person. The polish of the website. The look of the video. The confidence of the proposal. The "we." Those are your inches.

And here's the hundred-year-old mechanism behind it. In 1920, a psychologist named Edward Thorndike found the halo effect. Basically, one good impression bleeds into all the others. Officers who rated soldiers as physically impressive also rated them smarter and more loyal. Automatically. One signal, filling in the whole form. So when your business looks tall — polished, substantial — the client doesn't just think "nice website." The halo upgrades everything. More competent. More reliable. Worth more. Size signals are pricing power.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Thorndike — 'A Constant Error in Psychological Ratings' (the halo effect), J. Applied Psychology, 1920.]

And it goes deeper. The signals even change how you see yourself. In 2012, two researchers ran a study that got famous as "enclothed cognition." Adam and Galinsky. They put people in a white lab coat and measured focus on careful tasks. People in the coat did better. But only when they were told it was a doctor's coat. Same coat, called a painter's coat? Effect gone. The meaning upgraded the wearer. Round One's point — when your business wears the bigger suit, you show up different too. The polish isn't just costume. It's posture you start to believe.
[SOURCE CARD on screen: Adam & Galinsky — 'Enclothed Cognition,' J. Experimental Social Psychology, 2012.]

And Round One's closer is blunt. Your competitors are already doing this. The three-person agency calls itself a "collective." The solo consultant has a "team" page with stock photos. The whole economy is playing dress-up. Your honest little "I" is competing against everyone else's "we." Refusing to play isn't integrity, says Round One. It's showing up to a fight with your hands down.

[BEAT]

Strong round. Honestly. Real science, real logic, real money behind it. If I stopped here, you'd go inflate your website tonight and feel smart doing it. Don't stop here. Round Two is where it gets expensive.

## ROUND TWO — THE CASE FOR YOUR ACTUAL SIZE

[VISUAL: ROUND 2. The other corner.]

Round Two doesn't open with ethics. It opens with a question Round One can't answer.

What happens when they find out?

And before you say "they won't" — walk through how small business actually works. Your clients don't experience your website. They experience you. The discovery isn't some investigation. It's a Tuesday.

The "team" cc line that's always the same one email. The "our editor will handle it," followed by footage from your personal Dropbox. The client who calls the office and hears your kids in the background. Small business is intimate by nature. That's literally the product. So the costume has the shortest run on earth. In a big company it might survive a quarter. In your world it survives until the second coffee.

Because they do find out. The "team" of one becomes obvious by the second meeting. The "offices" turn into a Zoom background. And here's the brutal part Round Two wants on the record. The trust you got from looking big was rented. The trust you lose when they catch the inflation is gone.

People forgive small. People do not forgive deceived. A client who learns you're a company of one thinks "scrappy." A client who learns you pretended to be twelve now re-checks every other thing you ever told them. Your testimonials. Your timeline. Your price. One caught lie, and the halo runs in reverse. One bad signal, filling in the whole form with doubt.

And Round Two has a second argument almost nobody makes. It's my favorite. Small sells. Not despite being small. Because of it.

Think about what a client gets from a company of one that no giant can offer. The founder answers the phone. The person who pitched is the person who does the work. A decision takes a conversation, not a committee. The care isn't handed off. There are whole categories of buyer who got burned by exactly the bigness Round One wants you to fake. Passed from account manager to account manager. Billed by a machine. And they are actively hunting for small.

So when you disguise your size, you're hiding the exact feature they came for. You're a corner bakery dressed up as a factory, in a town full of people sick of factories.

And third, the inflation has a maintenance cost. Every meeting becomes a small performance. Every "we'll get the team on it" needs choreography. You took the one thing a small business has more of than anyone — truth, told fast — and turned it into a liability you have to manage. That tax compounds worse than the height premium ever paid.

And if Round Two needs one closing exhibit, recent history wrote it in capital letters. Theranos. A company whose whole art form was scale signals. The gleaming labs. The board full of statesmen. The founder styled down to the black turtleneck. All wrapped around technology that didn't do what the signals promised. For a while it worked. Nine billion dollars. Magazine covers. The halo at full power. Then the truth surfaced. And the collapse wasn't proportional to the lie. It was total. Because that's the asymmetry Round Two has been arguing the whole time. Signals can rent belief. The moment they're exposed as costume, every dollar of borrowed trust converts to contempt, with interest.

## THE RESOLUTION — SIGNALS OF CARE, NOT SIGNALS OF SCALE

[VISUAL: First music — warm. Jevoy steps to center, between the two corners.]

So who wins? Here's the resolution. It's not a compromise. It's a distinction the whole fight was missing.

Both rounds argued about "looking bigger" like it's one thing. It's two things. And they only look alike.

There are signals of scale. Headcount. Offices. "Our team." Years you haven't existed. Those are claims about size.

And there are signals of care. Light. Sound. Clarity. Design. Preparation. The quality of your first three seconds. Those are claims about standards.

Here's the test that splits them. If the client found out the full truth tomorrow, would the signal become a lie?

"We have a team of twelve." Lie. Scale signal. Don't.

"We've been doing this for fifteen years," when it's been two. Lie. Scale signal. Don't.

A beautifully lit video. A clean, clear proposal. A site that loads fast and reads simple. If they find out you're one person — the signal gets stronger. "One person did all this? Hire them today." That's the magic of care signals. They're discovery-proof.

So run everything through the found-out test before you publish it. Would the full truth make this claim collapse, or make it more impressive? Scale signals collapse. Care signals compound. They're not borrowed inches. They're real evidence of how you work, shown instead of claimed. And evidence only gets more convincing the closer you look.

And here's the beautiful part. Go back to the height research. The halo effect never asked for bigness. That was Round One misreading it. It just asks for one trustworthy signal to fill the form from. Care works as well as scale. Better, actually. Because care predicts the client's real experience, and scale doesn't.

So the move isn't to counterfeit an unearned signal. It's to flood the zone with earned ones. Look excellent, not enormous. Be a small business the way a great little camera is small. Nobody picks one up and thinks "cheap." They think "serious."

Let me make that concrete, because "look excellent" is useless without a list. Here are care signals you can deploy this month. Every one honest.

Real light and clean sound on your photos and video. We judge craft by craft. A website that loads fast and reads clear — speed is care you can see. Proposals designed like you'll be judged on them, because you will. Response time — answering in two hours beats any "team" page ever built. Specificity in your materials — real numbers, real process, real faces — because vague reads as small, and specific reads as established, even from a company of one. And the founder, on camera, calm and clear. That one's the biggest signal on the list. Because a big company can't offer the founder at all.

Every item there survives the found-out test. Most cost under a hundred dollars. None require a single fake pronoun.

Let me make it real. Say you're a one-person bakery. The scale move is a stock photo of a storefront you don't own and the word "we." It works until somebody drives by. The care move is one beautiful clip of your hands shaping the dough at 5 a.m. The steam coming off the first tray. Your real voice saying why you started. Same business. One is a costume. The other is evidence. And the second one only gets stronger the closer anyone looks.

So the founder at midnight? Here's her answer. Write "I." Then make every other pixel on that page prove this particular "I" runs at a standard most "we"s can't touch. The pronoun is honest. The presentation is ferocious. That's the whole strategy.

## WHAT PALMER HOUSE DOES + THE INVITATION

[VISUAL: Frame widens — the crew, the studio.]

And this is exactly the work, so let me be plain about where we stand. Palmer House Productions is a young company. We're building in the open, here in the Pacific Northwest. We're a video production company. Though the truer line is this — we're a translation company that happens to own cameras. We take what's actually true about a business and make it visible.

And here's the line we won't cross, and the line we sprint along. We will never make your business look like something it isn't. We will absolutely make it look like everything it is. Which, for almost every business we meet, is way more impressive than their current materials admit.

Because that's the real epidemic. Not small businesses inflating. Small businesses deflating. Phone-propped videos and rushed websites that read as careless — attached to businesses that are actually meticulous. The work is tall. The signals slouch. Our job is posture, not stilts.

And usually one good session flips the whole inventory. Because care signals share a supply chain. There's an old line about being trusted. Whoever can be trusted with a little gets trusted with a lot. And notice — that's not a consolation prize. It's a mechanism. Excellence at your true size is the one thing that reliably grows you to the next size. And it's the only strategy that needs zero pretending, survives every discovery, and compounds instead of taxing you.

Be small out loud. And be excellent louder. The market can hear both. The inflated businesses are everywhere. The excellent-but-invisible ones are everywhere. The honest-and-ferocious lane is wide open. Take it.
