# JEVOY PALMER — "The Camera on Trial"
## Cinematic Investigative Monologue (staged) | ~12 min | Jevoy Palmer main channel
## The Recorded Animal · Trilogy 3 · Approach: COURTROOM conceit, guerrilla staging · Delivery: propulsive, theatrical, sharp
### Voice-calibrated: bro-ban ✓ friend = recurring character (dials in via phone/Zoom) ✓ no verdict — the audience is the jury ✓ actionable CTA ✓ faith quiet ✓

> **STAGING (the gag):** Jevoy is NOT in a suit. T-shirt, shorts, a lav mic, standing on a random street / sidewalk / overpass — completely casual, like he stopped mid-walk to argue a case. The high-stakes courtroom *language* against the low-fi street *visual* is the whole comedic engine. The "friend" (defense) is not in the room — Jevoy **dials him in on his phone / a Zoom call**, holds the screen up to the lens, and the friend argues from a car, a couch, a job site. Friend is a recurring character with unlimited runway — different location, different absurd context every episode.
> **PRODUCTION:** needs a 2nd shoot for the friend's video-call footage (can be filmed separately, screen-captured). Two outfits not required — just the phone. B-roll: street life passing behind him, the phone screen, the camera-roll scroll.

---

[COLD OPEN]
[VISUAL: A busy street. Cars, people, normal day. Jevoy walks into frame in a t-shirt and shorts, stops, looks dead into the lens. No music yet. He holds up his phone — screen toward us — camera roll open.]

**JEVOY:**

I want to put something on trial. Right here. Right now. No robe, no courtroom — I'm in shorts on a Tuesday — but this is a real trial, and you're the jury, so don't go anywhere.

[BEAT]

The defendant has been with you all day. It's in your hand right now — probably in the hand you're not using. It has photographed your kids, your dinners, your worst mornings and your best light. And the charge is theft. Specifically: that it's been stealing your life for years while telling you it was *saving* it.

[VISUAL: He flips his phone around, scrolls his own camera roll on screen.]

Here's exhibit A. Open your camera roll. Scroll back six months and find me one photo you don't remember taking. Take your time — I promise you have one. Probably ten. Look at it: full proof you were there. Timestamped. Geotagged. In focus. And when you close your eyes and reach for the actual moment — the warmth of it, the sound of it — nothing. A thumbnail where a memory should be.

So that's the trial. Did the camera *keep* that moment for you? Or did it take the moment and hand you back the receipt?

[BEAT]

Now — full disclosure, because a good court runs on it: I am not a neutral party. I make my living with cameras. My whole company is rectangles. So I'm not going to hand you a verdict at the end. I'm going to call both sides, full weight — prosecution, defense, the whole thing — and then the verdict is yours. Deal?

Come on now. Court's in session. On a sidewalk. Let's go.

---

## THE PROSECUTION

[VISUAL: He starts walking. Camera tracks. He argues like he's pacing in front of a jury box that isn't there.]

The state calls its first witness. 2014. Linda Henkel, a psychologist at Fairfield University.
[SOURCE CARD: Henkel — 'Point-and-Shoot Memories,' Psychological Science, 2014.]

Dr. Henkel walks a group of students through an art museum. Real museum, real art. One instruction: some objects, just *look at.* Others — look at, *and* photograph. Next day she tests them. What do you remember? What details can you recall?

[BEAT]

The photographed objects lost. Across the board. The students remembered *fewer* of the things they'd photographed — and fewer details about them — than the things they only looked at. She named it: the **photo-taking impairment effect.** The act of capturing the thing quietly weakened the keeping of the thing.

And here's the testimony that does the damage. She called it *cognitive offloading.* The second the shutter clicks, your brain relaxes. It thinks: *the machine has this one, I don't need to carry it.* So it lets go. You didn't take the photo *and* keep the moment. You took the photo *instead* of keeping it. The photo was never a backup. Half the time, it's a replacement.

The state enters the modern math into evidence. The average phone holds ten, twenty thousand photos. We're capturing more moments than any humans who ever lived — and offloading every single one as we go. The camera roll gets fuller as the memory gets thinner. The fullest rolls, the emptiest hands.

[BEAT]

And the state calls one more witness — this one you already know. Think about the last concert you went to. The lights drop. The first song hits. And the whole crowd... lifts their phones. A thousand little glowing screens in the dark — everybody filming the moment instead of being in it, watching the show through a four-inch rectangle held up over their own heads. Now ask any one of them, a week later, to play that song back in their body — the chills, the bass in the chest, the heat of the room. Most of them can't. They've got the footage. They were never really at the show.

That's the impairment effect out in the wild. Basically — and here's the part that should scare you — the more proof you collect that you were somewhere, the less of it you actually keep. The receipt gets longer. The night gets shorter.

[BEAT]

And it gets worse, because the defendant doesn't just steal the moment after it happens. It changes the moment *while* it's happening. The second there's a lens, we start performing. We don't taste the dinner — we plate it for the photo. We don't laugh — we redo the laugh because the first one wasn't framed. We don't take the trip — we go to the spots that photograph well. That's the heaviest charge of all: the defendant quietly rewrites your real life into something staged for an audience that isn't even in the room. We stopped living our moments. We started producing them.

[BEAT]

The state rests.

---

## CROSS-EXAMINATION

[VISUAL: He stops walking. Raises one finger — the "but."]

Before the defense gets up — one question for Dr. Henkel the prosecution would rather I skip. *Was there an exception?*

There was. Buried in her own data. The students who *zoomed in* — who photographed one specific detail, on purpose, paying attention — remembered those just fine. Sometimes better. So it wasn't the camera that broke memory. It was *automatic* camera use that broke memory. Attention was the active ingredient the whole time.

Hold that. Underline it. The defense is about to build its whole case on it.

---

## THE DEFENSE (dials in)

[VISUAL: Jevoy pulls out his phone. "Hang on, let me get my guy." He video-calls his friend. The friend picks up from somewhere absurdly casual — driving, on a couch, in a Costco. Jevoy holds the phone up to the lens; we cut to a picture-in-picture of the friend.]

And now — counsel for the defense. He's the voice in my head that argues with me, except he's real and he's on FaceTime and he is never, ever in a courtroom. But unlike most of his arguments, this time the man brought a study. Go ahead.

[VISUAL: FRIEND, on the phone screen, leaning into his own camera.]

**FRIEND (video call):**
"Thank you. Listener — my client is not a thief.

2016. Diehl, Zauberman, Barasch. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology.* Nine experiments, thousands of people — bus tours, museums, restaurants, real life. And the people who took photos *enjoyed it more* than the people who didn't. More engaged. More immersed. *Not less.* Because taking the photo made them look *harder* — hunting for what was worth keeping. The prosecution wants you scared of your own phone. The biggest study ever run on this says the opposite. For some of us, the camera is *how* we're present. You think Ansel Adams loved the mountains *less* because he photographed them? The man stood waist-deep in freezing rivers before sunrise waiting on one cloud to move. That's not a thief. That's the most present man on the mountain. My client is not a thief. My client is a paintbrush — and my colleague over there in his little shorts is blaming the brush for the painter. The defense rests. I gotta go, I'm in line."
[SOURCE CARD: Diehl, Zauberman & Barasch — 'How Taking Photos Increases Enjoyment,' JPSP, 2016.]

[VISUAL: Jevoy lowers the phone. Beat. To lens.]

And — I hate it — but he's right. That study is real, it's strong, and I'm not going to bury good evidence to keep my case pretty. Photo-taking *can* deepen a moment. Ask any photographer — the camera teaches you to see light, faces, the exact second a thing becomes itself. There's a way of holding a camera that's basically devotion.

So now we've got a problem. Two sworn witnesses. Two real studies. Pointing opposite directions. Somebody has to be lying.

---

## THE RECONCILIATION

[VISUAL: He starts walking again, faster now — he's onto it.]

Except — nobody's lying. And this is where the whole trial turns, so stay with me.

The two studies were never describing the camera. They were describing two different *people* holding the same phone.

Look at the defense's photographers. They were hunting. Choosing the shot. Deciding what mattered. That's the same thing Henkel's zoomed-in students did — full attention, just with a camera in their hands. Now look at the students who forgot. They snapped because someone told them to. No thought. Just reflex. Their attention never showed up.

Picture it on a Tuesday. Two parents at the same school recital — same phone, same kid, same song. One holds her phone up the entire time, eyes locked on the little screen, checking the framing, making sure she *got it* — and feels almost nothing, because she's working. The other lifts her phone for ten seconds, grabs one clip, puts it down, and watches her kid's face find hers in the crowd — and gets absolutely wrecked by it, in the best way. One of them captured the recital. The other one kept it. The phone didn't decide that. Their attention did.

Same phone. Opposite result.

So I have to drop the charge. The camera was never the defendant. We blamed the wrong thing. What actually decides whether a moment gets kept or lost was never the glass in your hand.

It was the attention behind it.

---

## THE TURN (the charge gets refiled)

[VISUAL: He stops at something with a view — a railing, an overlook, a window.]

So let me refile. New defendant. Same street.

We live like capture is *keeping* and presence is *losing.* That's the fear under all of it: "if I don't film it, I'll lose it." The whole reflex runs on the terror of forgetting. But run Henkel backwards and the verdict flips: the unattended capture is the *guaranteed* loss. The file saves. The experience never even got recorded — not in you, where it counts. You didn't insure the moment. You skipped it, and kept the receipt.

[BEAT]

Real memory — the in-your-body kind — was never made by the shutter. It's made by attention. By being so present the moment writes itself into you. Which means presence was never the *risk* of losing the moment. Presence is the only recording device that was ever actually working.

And you can test that right now, in your own head. Think of a moment you *didn't* film — one that matters. Maybe somebody who's gone now. Maybe just a kitchen, a car ride, a laugh. Notice it doesn't play back as a video. It comes back warm — the light, the smell, what they said, how it sat in your chest. Full sensory. No file. Now think of a moment you *did* film and never once watched again. It comes back flat, if it comes back at all — a thumbnail with the sound off. Your best memories were never the recorded ones. They were the attended ones.

The phone keeps the pixels. Only you keep the moment. And only one of those plays back warm.

---

## THE RECUSAL

[VISUAL: First music of the video fades in — low, warm. He leans on the railing. Quieter. The street keeps moving behind him.]

And here's where I have to recuse myself. Because I told you I'm not neutral — and it's worse than that. Some days I'm not the lawyer. I'm the defendant.

I build things with cameras. And a couple years ago I caught the builder's version of this disease — sneakier than the tourist's. Every moment now shows up with a little producer in my head going *this'd be good content.* The real conversation — *clip that.* The morning light in the studio — *B-roll.* My own life, pre-cut into segments before I've finished living it.

And the cost I didn't see coming wasn't that the producer takes notes. It's that he takes the *seat.* The second something real starts, part of me steps out to frame it. I've been, more than I want to admit, a man in the front row of his own life — watching the feed.

And when I get quiet about *why* — under the content excuse, under the memory excuse — there's something more tender. The fear that a moment that isn't recorded doesn't fully count. That with no proof, no post, it just evaporates, and a little of me goes with it. We've stopped trusting our own lives to hold our lives. That's the wound under the camera roll. Twenty thousand photos of not trusting.

[BEAT]

I'll tell you the one that caught me. My wife said something at dinner — I genuinely don't remember what now, which is the whole point — and before I even felt it, my hand was moving to my pocket to grab my phone and capture it. I caught my own hand halfway there. And I realized I'd been about to get up and leave the table to go stand behind a camera that only exists in my head. She was right there. The moment was free. And I almost traded it for a file I'd never open.

So lately I keep a few moments a week with *no* device at all. Not captured, not posted, not proven. Mine either way. Witnessed by nothing but me and God — and it turns out that's enough witnesses.

[VISUAL: Music fades under.]

---

## THE VERDICT IS YOURS  ·  [CTA]

[VISUAL: He pushes off the railing. Direct to lens. Holds his phone up one more time.]

So I'm not banging a gavel. I told you I wouldn't — and anyway, I don't own one, I own rectangles. The verdict is yours. But a jury has to deliberate somewhere, so let's do it right down there in the comments.

Here's your summons — two parts:

**One — cast your vote.** Open your camera roll right now, land on the last photo you took without thinking, and drop one word under this video: **"reflex"** or **"devotion."** That's your verdict on the defendant. I read every single one.

**Two — serve the sentence.** Because a verdict is just talk until somebody serves it: this week, pick one moment you'd normally film... and *don't.* Then come back here and tell me what came back to you when there was no second copy. That comment — that's the one I'm actually waiting on.

[BEAT]

And if there's somebody in your life who watches their whole life through a four-inch screen — don't lecture them. Just send them this. Consider it a subpoena.

There's an old line I keep circling — two sisters, one running around making the visit *count,* the other just sitting down, fully present, doing nothing useful. And the ruling that still gets me: she chose the better thing, *and it could not be taken from her.* Empty hands. Better thing. No platform owns it. Nothing corrupts the file. It never needs a backup.

Court's adjourned. The defendant's in your hand. Your move.

[VISUAL: He pockets the phone, walks out of frame into the street. We hold on the empty sidewalk. Cut to black.]
