# MINDYOURBIZNIZ — "Why being seen feels like danger."
**Format:** The Letter · **Topic 01 — The Camera Freeze (v3)** · **Length target:** 2,500+ spoken words · **Verse:** 1 Samuel 16:7

---

[COLD OPEN. Room tone. The sound of a mug being set down on wood. A small breath.]

Hey. It's Jevoy.

I'm gonna do this episode a little different. I'm gonna talk to one person tonight. Not all of you. One. If you're not that one — stay anyway. You might know her. You might love him. You might *be* him in six months and not realize it yet. But the words tonight are for the one.

So. Here we go.

[PAUSE. A long one.]

I'm writing this to the person who deleted last night's take.

You know the one. You sat down at your desk after the kids were in bed. You set the phone up against the stack of books on your shelf. You hit record. You said your first sentence. And before you got to the second one, you'd already decided you hated the sound of your own voice — and you hit stop, and you watched the playback once, and you deleted it before anyone could find it on your camera roll.

You did that. Right? You did that last night.

I know because I did it too. Three weeks ago. I won't tell you what about. But the take is gone. The shelf is still there. The books are still there. I haven't tried again yet.

[Quiet.]

I want to talk to you about why that happens. And I don't want to do it the way most people do it, where they tell you that you just need more confidence, or that you should *get out of your own way*, or that successful people just *push through the fear*. I think most of that advice is well-meaning and basically useless. Because it treats the part of you that flinched as the enemy. And I don't think she's the enemy. And I don't think he's the enemy. I think that part of you is actually doing something kind of beautiful, and if you understood what she was doing you'd stop trying to fight her, and you'd let her have her seat at the table, and you'd record the next take with her sitting right there next to you.

Let me try to explain.

[BEAT.]

When you spoke to the camera last night, two of you were in the room. There was the *you* that was trying to talk. And there was the *you* that was watching the one trying to talk. Two psychologists figured out the name of this in 1972 — Duval and Wicklund. They called it objective self-awareness. But all those words mean is the thing you already felt, which is: when the camera came on, you split.

Most of the time, you're just doing the thing. Cooking dinner. Talking to your sister. Driving home. There's no one watching, so there's no one to perform for, so there's no monitor in your head deciding whether you're doing the *being yourself* part well. You just are.

The second you turned the camera on — the monitor woke up. And the monitor's job is to compare you to *who you should be*. And the monitor is a critic. The monitor doesn't think your laugh is cute, the monitor thinks your laugh is too loud. The monitor doesn't think your earnest pause is wise, the monitor thinks the pause was awkward and dead air. The monitor played it back and went *that doesn't even sound like me*, and you said *yeah it doesn't*, and you deleted it.

But here's the thing I want you to sit with for a second.

That voice on the playback — that voice you didn't recognize, the one that sounded nasal and small and not like the one inside your head — that voice is the voice every person who loves you has been hearing your whole life.

There's a study, 1966, Holzman and Rousey. They proved that the voice you hear when you talk isn't the voice that comes out of you. When you speak, the sound travels through your skull bones before it gets to your ears, and the bones make it lower and warmer and fuller. When you hear yourself on a recording, the bones aren't there, so what you hear is the version that everyone else has always heard.

They love that voice. You hate it. They've been listening to that voice tell them they're loved, tell them dinner's ready, tell them happy birthday. To them, that voice is *home*. To you, it's a stranger.

So when you said *that doesn't sound like me* and deleted the take — what you were deleting was the version of you that your husband fell in love with. The version your kids fall asleep to. The version your best friend recognized from across the parking lot at the grocery store.

That voice isn't a misrepresentation of you. *That voice is you.* The one in your head, the one with the skull bones, is the prettier version. The recording is the honest one.

[Pause.]

I want you to sit with that for a second. Because I think it changes what the deletion was.

You weren't deleting a bad video. You were deleting yourself. The version of you the world has always known and loved. You were deleting it because it didn't match the version *you* know.

And I think the reason that hurts so much is that for most of your life, you got to be the curator. You got to decide which version of you showed up where. You picked the photo. You wrote the caption. You chose your words in the message before you hit send. You had all this *control* over the gap between the version of you that lives in your head and the version of you the world meets.

A camera takes the curator away.

A camera says: no, the version you can edit isn't the one I'm gonna show. I'm gonna show the one that *happened*. Your hands and your hair and the fact that you said *um* twice and laughed at your own thing before you got to the punchline. That one. That one's the one going on the internet.

And the part of you that flinched is the curator panicking. Because the curator's whole job, for thirty or forty or fifty years, has been to keep the inside-you and the outside-you within shouting distance of each other. And the camera just shoved them into the same frame, and they don't match, and the curator does the only thing she knows how to do.

She deletes.

[BEAT. A single low pad note enters here — held, soft.]

I want to tell you something my pastor said once. He said the hardest people to love are the ones who don't know they're already loved.

I sat with that for like a week. Because what he was describing is a person who's never met themselves through someone else's eyes. They keep the curated version up. They never let the recording exist. And so the people around them love a *version* of them. A version they can sustain. A version that doesn't include the laugh that's too loud or the pause that's awkward or the voice that doesn't sound right on the playback.

The people around them never get to love the *actual* them — because the actual them never sat down in the chair without the curator.

I think the deleted takes are connected to this. I think every time we delete the take, we're rehearsing a kind of loneliness. We're rehearsing *only being loved for the version of me I can control*. And the version we can control gets smaller every year. It gets more rehearsed. More careful. More polished. More dead.

The unguarded version of you — the one the camera caught — is the one that has actually been knocking on the door of the people you love your whole life. And every time you delete the take, you keep her in the hallway.

[BEAT.]

OK. I can already hear the friend who pushes back. Because I have one of those. And she'd be saying right now — Jevoy, slow down. There are real reasons not to put yourself on the internet. There are stalkers and bosses and exes and people who screenshot stuff and weaponize it. *Boundaries* are not the same as *fear*. Some takes *should* get deleted. Some moments are not for the world.

And she's right. She's all the way right.

I am not telling you to post everything you record. I am not telling you the answer is to override your discernment about who gets to see what. There is a real and holy difference between protecting a moment because it's sacred to you and protecting a moment because the monitor said *you sound stupid*.

The question I want you to learn to ask, before you hit delete, is: am I deleting this because *the wrong people would see it*, or am I deleting this because *the right people might*?

Because those are completely different reasons. And in my experience — and you can argue with this, you should argue with this — the second one is the one that runs the show.

You deleted last night's take. Be honest with yourself. *Who* did you imagine watching it that made you delete it? Was it a hater? A boss? An ex? Or was it your own internal voice playing the part of *people in general*, judging you on behalf of a crowd that hasn't actually showed up yet?

If it was the second one — if you deleted on behalf of an audience that doesn't exist — I want you to consider that you're letting a crowd that isn't real take away access to a person who is.

[Pad swells just slightly.]

Here's what I think the work is.

I think the work isn't to silence the monitor. I think the work is to stop confusing the monitor with you. The monitor isn't you. The monitor is a function. The monitor's job is to predict how a group might react so that you don't get cast out of the group. That's a really old job. A hundred and fifty thousand years old. The monitor saved your great-great-great-grandmother's life a hundred times in the village. The monitor was good for her.

But the monitor doesn't know that you live in a world now where the village is the internet, and the internet is a billion villages, and you can't possibly be cast out of all of them, and the cost of letting the monitor decide what version of you exists in the world is — quietly, slowly, over years — the loss of being known.

You can thank the monitor for trying. You can let her speak. And then you can put the phone back up against the stack of books. And you can hit record. And you can let the version of you that the people you love already love show up on the screen. And you can not delete it.

That's the move.

[Pause. Pad still under.]

There's a verse I want to leave you with. 1 Samuel 16:7. *Man looketh on the outward appearance, but the Lord looketh on the heart.*

I think most people read that verse one way — that God sees past the outside to the inside. And that's true. But I want to read it back to you the other way tonight.

The camera looks at the outside. The camera will never see your heart. The thing you played back, the thing you didn't recognize, the thing you deleted — that was the outside. The footage doesn't know you. The footage will never know you.

The people who know you — your husband, your sister, your best friend, your kid — they see the outside *and* the heart. They have always seen both. The take you deleted last night wouldn't have surprised them. They already knew. They knew the laugh, they knew the pause, they knew the voice through the speaker. They've been listening to it the whole time.

The footage is the worst-resolution version of you that exists. The people who love you have always seen the high-resolution one.

The next time you want to delete the take — don't delete it because the footage didn't capture you. Of course it didn't. The footage can't. The footage isn't for you to recognize yourself in. The footage is for someone who hasn't met you yet to get the first hint that there's a person worth knowing here.

You're allowed to be that hint.

[Pad fades.]

That's it. That's the episode.

If this hit somebody — if you've got one person in your life who's been deleting their own takes, whether or not it's literal takes, whether they're a creator or a dad or a daughter or somebody who hasn't said the true thing in five years because the monitor wouldn't let them — send this to them. Just send it. No caption. Just the link. They'll know.

And then, sometime this week — try this with me. Pick one thing you'd normally hide. Doesn't have to be on camera. Could be a voice note. Could be a text. Could be saying the actual thing across the dinner table instead of the rehearsed thing. And let the unedited version exist. Don't delete it.

See what happens.

I'd love to know.

[Long breath. Mug picked up. Held.]

Thanks for being here. MindYourBizniz is a Palmer House conversation. New episode next week.

[Mug set down. Pad out. End.]

## CLIP MAP — 10 standalone 30–60s segments

1. **The address** — "I'm writing this to the person who deleted last night's take." Cold open + premise.
2. **The two yous** — Duval & Wicklund 1972, the split, in conversational language.
3. **The voice that's actually you** — Holzman & Rousey 1966, skull-bones, the recording is the honest one.
4. **What you deleted was the version your husband fell in love with** — the gut-punch line.
5. **The curator panic** — what a camera takes away from a lifetime of editing.
6. **The hardest people to love** — the pastor line, the loneliness of staying curated.
7. **The friend who pushes back** — boundaries vs. fear; the question you ask before you hit delete.
8. **The audience that doesn't exist yet** — letting a crowd that isn't real take away access to a person who is.
9. **The monitor isn't you** — the reframe; thank her, don't obey her.
10. **The footage is the worst-resolution version of you** — 1 Samuel 16:7, the close.
