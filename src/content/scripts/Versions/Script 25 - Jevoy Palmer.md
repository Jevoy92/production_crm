# JEVOY PALMER — "A photo of eyes made strangers 3x more honest. That's why you freeze on camera."
**Format:** The Experiment · **Topic 01 — The Camera Freeze (v3)** · **Length target:** 2,500+ spoken words · **Verse:** Psalm 139:14

---

[COLD OPEN — phone on a tripod, record light blinks on. Jevoy looks at it. Five-second silence. Half-smile, looks away, looks back.]

You see that? You just watched it happen. Camera came on, and for like two seconds I forgot how to be a person. I do this for a living. I own a production company. I just lost the ability to make my own face do the thing it does for free when nobody's watching.

I want to investigate why.

This is not the video where I tell you to fake it till you make it. This is not the video where the answer is confidence. The answer is older than confidence. The answer is older than language. The answer is a piece of machinery in your head from when your survival depended on whether the village was watching you, and we just handed that same machinery a phone with a glowing red dot.

Here's what I'm gonna do. I'm gonna show you a study with a coffee tin that should not work but does. I'm gonna show you a 1972 paper that explains what's happening inside your skull the second a lens turns on. I'm gonna run an experiment on myself, right here, that I genuinely don't know how is gonna land. And then I'm gonna tell you the one thing that actually makes the freeze go away — and it has nothing to do with believing in yourself, which is the advice I'm honestly tired of hearing.

Stay with me. There's a coffee tin coming.

[BEAT. Music does not come in. Music doesn't come in for a while.]

So in 2006 there are these three researchers at Newcastle. Bateson, Nettle, and Roberts. They have a coffee station in their psych department, you know the kind — honesty box, drop in your coin, take your cup. Standard British university passive-aggressive trust system.

They put a little sign above the box telling people how much to pay. And every week they swapped out the image on the sign. One week, flowers. Next week, a pair of human eyes looking right at you. Then flowers. Then eyes. Just printed on paper. Black and white. Not a real human. Not a camera. Just *eyes on a page* above a coffee tin.

The weeks where the eyes were watching? People paid almost three times more.

[Holds up a printed pair of eyes from off-frame. Tapes them to the wall.]

Now I have to tell you the truth — when other researchers tried to repeat this exact study, they got mixed results. Some replications worked. Some didn't. Real science cuts both ways and I'm not gonna lie to you about that. But what nobody disputes is that *something* in your brain reacts to the suggestion of being watched. Even a cartoon. Even a printout. Your body changes — your blood pressure changes, your heart rate changes — when you think eyes are on you, whether they're real or not.

Which means the thing freezing you up on camera right now? That thing was watching you long before phones existed.

OK. Experiment time. I told you I'd run one. Here it is.

[Sits. Looks at camera. Long pause.]

Right now I'm gonna try to do something I do every single day. I'm gonna explain what my company does to my best friend. Like he asked. At dinner. I've done this a thousand times. It takes about ninety seconds. Watch.

[Pause. Tries to start. Stops. Laughs.]

See? I just lost it again. Because it's not my best friend. It's a camera. And my brain knows the difference.

Now I'm gonna do something different. Camera's still on. But I'm gonna pick a coffee mug up off this desk and walk you through the same explanation while I'm pouring a cup. Watch how my mouth gets unstuck the second my body has a job.

[Picks up mug. Pours. Talks fluidly for 15 seconds about Palmer House. Sets mug down. Re-freezes immediately.]

Same brain. Same words. Same camera. The only thing that changed was whether my body had something to do that wasn't *be watched*.

[Sets coffee down. Looks dead at lens.]

This is the part where I get to tell you why.

In 1972, two psychologists, Shelley Duval and Robert Wicklund, published a theory they called objective self-awareness. The short version: most of the day, your attention is pointed outward. At your kid. At your screen. At your coffee. You're the *actor* in your own life. You're doing the doing.

But the second something turns your attention inward — a mirror, an audience, a camera, a comment that lands wrong — you split. Half of you keeps acting. The other half starts watching. The monitor wakes up.

The monitor's job is to compare what you're doing to what you *should* be doing. And the monitor is brutal. The monitor notices the catch in your voice. The monitor notices the weird thing your left hand is doing. The monitor sees your face in the self-view square in the corner of the Zoom call and goes, hold on, that's what my chin looks like? That's not what my chin looks like.

[Beat.]

Jeremy Bailenson at Stanford — guy who basically invented the academic study of virtual reality — he published a paper in 2021 about Zoom fatigue. One of his four main causes? The mirror. He said: imagine carrying a hand mirror around with you all day, holding it up next to your face during every conversation. You'd be exhausted by lunch. That's what self-view does. That's the monitor, switched on, all day long.

And that's *just* the mirror version. Now add a camera, which records. The monitor isn't just watching anymore. The monitor is realizing this footage will exist forever. The monitor is calculating who might see it. The monitor is rehearsing the version of you that won't embarrass the version of you in six months. The monitor is doing math that the actor cannot work around.

So you freeze.

[Picks up mug again. Sips. Sets it down.]

You wanna hear something funny? There's a study from 1966 — Holzman and Rousey — where they recorded people's voices and played them back. Almost everyone said the same thing. *That doesn't sound like me.* And it doesn't, to you. Because when you talk, you hear your voice through your skull bones. The sound is lower, warmer, fuller. Then you hear it played back through a speaker and it sounds nasal and tinny and like a stranger pretending to be you.

The version of your voice the camera captures is the version everyone else has been hearing your whole life. They love it. You hate it. That's not a flaw in you. That's an acoustics problem. But your brain doesn't know acoustics. Your brain just knows: that's not me. And the freeze deepens.

Now, here's where the smart person in the comments goes — hold on. I'm fine on stage. Three hundred people in a room and I'm great. One iPhone on a tripod and I crumble. That can't be the same mechanism.

And they're right. Sort of. There's a thing Robert Zajonc proved in 1965 called social facilitation. Audiences *help* you do things you've already mastered. Things your muscle memory owns. Audiences *wreck* you on tasks you haven't done a thousand times.

So the person who's been giving the same toast since college can crush a wedding. Try to get them to record a thirty-second LinkedIn intro and they're sweating bullets. Same brain. The toast is practiced. The video is new. And the camera doesn't help you on new — the camera *amplifies* you on new. Whatever was a little awkward becomes a lot awkward. Whatever was a little uncertain becomes paralysis.

The skeptic's right that the stage is different. But the difference isn't your courage. It's your reps.

[BEAT. Long one. Music finally enters here — quiet pad, single note.]

Here's the turn.

You are not broken. The version of you that talks easy at the dinner table and locks up the second the lens is on — that's not two different yous. That's one you, with one piece of survival hardware doing exactly what it was built to do.

For most of human history, being watched mattered. A lot. The village seeing you mattered because if the village turned on you, you died. Alone, in the cold, with no one to share food with. The watching wasn't entertainment. The watching was the entire infrastructure of staying alive. So your brain evolved a piece of machinery that lights up the second you sense eyes on you, and that machinery's whole job is *do not get cast out*.

That machinery has been running for a hundred and fifty thousand years. The phone in your hand has existed for fifteen. The selfie camera, less. The expectation that you make daily content of your face for a global audience of strangers? That's, what, ten years old?

Your brain didn't get a software update. You're running ancient firmware. And the firmware is doing its job. It's just doing it about the wrong thing.

So when you freeze, you are not weak. You are not faking. You are not a coward. You are a human animal whose protective system can't tell the difference between a hostile tribe and a 4K sensor.

The freeze is the alarm working. The freeze is *care*.

[Beat.]

OK so what do you do.

You can't argue with the firmware. You've tried. You've read the books. You've done the breathing. The firmware doesn't listen to logic. The firmware listens to *familiarity*.

There's another effect — it's called the Hawthorne effect, named after a factory study from the 1920s. The original data has been argued about for decades, I'll be honest about that. But the core observation has held up in a hundred other places: when you start being observed, your behavior changes. And then, slowly, as the observation becomes the new normal, your behavior drifts back toward yourself.

The watched you and the natural you are not enemies. They're a stranger you and a future-friend you. And the only thing that closes the gap is hours.

So the practical answer — the thing I actually do, the thing I tell every founder I work with — is this: record until it's boring. Not until it's good. Not until you don't freeze. Until you *don't notice*. Until the camera becomes furniture. The way a microphone becomes furniture for a radio host. The way a steering wheel becomes furniture for you on a drive you've done a thousand times.

You're not trying to win a war with the firmware. You're trying to teach the firmware that this particular shape, the small black rectangle with the glowing dot, is not a threat. And the only way to teach it is reps. Cheap reps. Bad reps. Throwaway reps. Reps that no one will ever see. Reps you delete before bed.

The version of you that's easy on camera doesn't exist yet. She's a stranger. He's a stranger. You build that stranger by showing up enough times that your firmware shrugs and goes, oh. This guy again. Cool.

[Music swells slightly.]

I want to leave you with one more thing.

There's a verse I keep coming back to with this. Psalm 139, verse 14. *I am fearfully and wonderfully made.* That's the famous line. But the part that hits me on this topic is what comes right before — *I will praise thee, for I am fearfully and wonderfully made.* The being-made is the worship. The fact that you have a piece of machinery in your head, running ancient code, designed to keep you safe in a world that no longer exists — that's not a bug to fix. That's a creature to know.

Your freeze isn't a verdict on you. It's evidence that you were built for something real. You were built to be seen by *people*, in *rooms*, in *small numbers*, by *eyes that loved you*. And the modern world is asking you to be seen by everyone, all the time, by lenses that don't love you back.

Of course you flinch. The flinch is the proof you were built for connection.

So go easy on the part of you that locks up. That part isn't broken. That part is doing exactly what it was made to do. Your job isn't to silence it. Your job is to take it for enough walks past the camera that it stops barking.

That's it. That's the video.

If this question lives in you too — if you're tired of being told to just be confident when your body is yelling something completely different — stay close. We talk about stuff like this on The Next Step every Friday. It's free, it's live, and you can ask me anything. I'd love to see you there.

[Music out. Record light off. Beat.]

[End.]
