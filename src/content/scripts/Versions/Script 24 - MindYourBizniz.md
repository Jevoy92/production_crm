# MINDYOURBIZNIZ — "Known by Thousands, Seen by No One"
## Solo Podcast Monologue | ~15 min | Read-Ready | No props, no acting, no scene cuts
## The Recorded Animal · Week 1: Seen but not known · Altitude: the inner life

[COLD OPEN]
[ROOM TONE — close, quiet. The mug set down on wood. A breath.]

**JEVOY:**

I want to describe a specific kind of loneliness today. It's a new one — historically speaking, it barely existed before this century — and the people who have it usually can't complain about it, because from the outside it looks exactly like winning.

It's the loneliness of being known by thousands... and seen by no one.

[BEAT]

And before you say "Jevoy, I don't have an audience, this isn't my episode" — stay with me, because the thousand doesn't have to be followers. It can be a congregation. A company. A client list. A group chat. A family reunion's worth of people who all know *the version* — the strong one, the funny one, the together one — and would all swear they know you. The crowd isn't the point. The *asymmetry* is the point: lots of people holding a version of you, nobody holding you.

Now — this is not the episode where I tell you social media connection is fake and real connection happens at farmers markets. Come on now. Some of the realest encouragement I've ever received came from strangers through a screen, and I will not pretend otherwise.

What I want to do instead is investigate the asymmetry properly. There's research from 1956 — *before the internet, before social media* — that named exactly what a watching crowd feels toward a person, and what it can never feel. There's a finding about why being adored as the wrong person starves you worse than being ignored. And then, per the new rule on this show, we're not landing this plane in the sad field. We're landing somewhere kids play. Let's go.

---

## CHAPTER 2: THE MECHANISM

So, 1956. Two researchers — Donald Horton and Richard Wohl — are studying the strange new intimacy of television. And they notice viewers developing real relationships with TV hosts. Real loyalty, real affection, real grief when a show ended. The viewer knows the host's mannerisms, jokes, moods — knows them better than they know their neighbors.

They named it **parasocial interaction**, and the prefix carries the whole diagnosis: *para* — alongside, almost, resembling. A relationship with all the feeling of friendship running in exactly one direction. The viewer knows the host completely. The host doesn't know the viewer exists.

[PAUSE]

Now here's what nobody in 1956 could have predicted: that one day, *ordinary people* would live on the host's side of the glass. Not celebrities — teachers, founders, pastors, that one cousin with the popular page. Anyone with any audience at all now experiences the host's half of the parasocial bargain: warmth arriving constantly, from people who know your *output* — your front stage, as this week's main investigation put it — while the person generating the output sits alone in a room with a microphone.

And here's the cruelty in the mechanics, you feel me: the warmth is *real.* That's what makes it confusing. The encouragement is sincere, the gratitude is sincere — and it's all addressed to the character. We talked about this on the main channel this week — the psychologist William Swann showed that humans don't primarily crave being *liked*; we crave being *accurately known.* Praise addressed to a self you know isn't quite you doesn't deposit. It bounces. The account it was sent to doesn't exist.

So run the math of the maintained self. The more successfully you perform the version — the more people who love the version — the *larger* the crowd holding somebody who isn't quite you, and the further you drift from anyone holding who is. The applause genuinely lands. Just on the costume. We said that line a month ago and I haven't put it down since.

---

## CHAPTER 3: THE IMAGE YOU CAN HOLD IN YOUR MIND

No props on a podcast. Just hold this with me.

A sold-out theater. Every seat full. And the show ends, and the applause is enormous — real, warm, deserved. The actor bows. And then the curtain drops, and the actor walks backstage... and the backstage is *empty.* No crew. No castmates. No one waiting with their real name on their lips. Just costume racks and a mirror with bulbs around it.

[BEAT]

A thousand people out front who came for the character. Zero people back here for the actor.

That's the architecture of this particular loneliness, and notice — *nothing about it is fake.* The play was good. The applause was honest. The theater is full every night. By every visible metric this is a triumph. The vacancy is in the one room the audience can't see and never thinks about — the room Goffman called backstage, the room where the costume comes off. The crowd can't staff that room. It's not their job, and structurally, they couldn't even find the door.

And the wildest part: the actor built it this way. One reasonable decision at a time. Every hour spent making the front stage more lovable was an hour not spent letting any single person backstage. Not because the actor is foolish — because the front stage *pays*, the front stage applauds, the front stage gives you a thousand small warmths a day, and the backstage asks for the one thing the front stage never requires: being seen out of costume, with the risk that carries.

A thousand shallow warmths can be arranged on demand. The one deep warmth can't. So we keep choosing the thousand. And the room stays empty.

---

## CHAPTER 4: THE FRIEND WHO PUSHES BACK

And here comes my friend — and today the friend is somebody with a real community online, and they're a little offended, honestly:

"Jevoy, careful. You're describing MY people like they're a hallucination. My audience HAS seen me — they've watched me fail launches, lose weight, lose my dad. They showed up with prayers and DMs that carried me through, things my own neighbors never did. Parasocial isn't fake — even your 1956 guys said these bonds can be healthy and real. And let's be honest about the other side: plenty of people are 'deeply known' by a spouse who hasn't actually *looked* at them in ten years. Proximity isn't intimacy either, bro. So don't romanticize the dinner table and slander the comment section. Connection is connection."

[BEAT]

That deserves a real answer, because most of it is true.

It's true that parasocial bonds are real and can be genuinely good — Horton and Wohl themselves said they're not pathological; for isolated people they can be a lifeline. It's true that audiences can witness real things — if you've shared real things, real witnessing happened; I won't take that from you, and I've *felt* it from my own. And the spouse point — completely fair. Symmetry on paper means nothing without attention. A front-row seat to a person is not the same as watching them.

But here's the line that holds, friend, and it's not about realness — it's about *diet.* Parasocial warmth is real the way sugar is real. Actual calories, actual lift, actually sweet. And it is structurally missing the one nutrient the soul runs on, the Swann nutrient: *being known accurately, specifically, in both directions, by someone who can check.* Your audience saw what you chose to share — even your rawest post was curated by you, after the fact, framed. The person backstage sees what you *didn't* choose. The Tuesday version. The before-coffee version. The version that contradicts the content. A thousand people holding your story is a library. One person holding your gaze is a meal. Libraries are wonderful, bro. Nobody ever got fed in one.

---

## CHAPTER 5: THE FLIP

So here's the flip — and per the new rule of this whole month, we're not flipping into "manage your parasocial risk." We're flipping into the playroom.

Because go back to that empty backstage — and now remember what backstage *is for.* Not just recovery. Goffman's backstage is where the cast plays. It's where the jokes are worse and louder, where the bits happen, where you rehearse wild ideas before they're ready, where you're allowed to be *un-impressive at full volume.* Watch kids — they have this completely figured out. A kid performs all day, happily, for invisible crowds — and then flips, instantly, into the fort, the blanket fort with the one best friend, where a different and *sillier* self comes out, the one with the secret language. Kids don't experience the fort as hiding from the performance. The fort is the *better game.*

[PAUSE]

That's the reframe, you feel me? The backstage isn't the sad empty room where you recover from being known by thousands. Staffed right, it's the *playroom* — the most creative, least guarded, most childlike room in your life. And here's what happens when you build it: the front stage *changes.* The show gets lighter. Because a performer with a real fort performs for *joy* — the stage becomes a place you visit to give something, instead of the only room in the house, the one you can never leave because leaving means no one's holding you at all.

Known by thousands is not the disease. Known by thousands, *seen by no one* — that's the disease. Keep the thousands. Love the thousands. Just staff the fort. The thousand and the one were never competitors, bro. They're different rooms in a house you're allowed to fully own.

---

## CHAPTER 6: THE EMOTIONAL PART

[MUSIC FADES IN — one low, warm pad. Under this chapter only.]

And I'll go first. Like always.

I'm a man building multiple public selves at once — you know this. There's the production company voice, the coaching voice, this voice, the creative one with the music. All of them true. All of them *mine* — I authored every costume, like the main channel said to. And still, some weeks, I can feel the asymmetry building like weather. A week where the DMs are warm and the comments are kind and clients are grateful — a week absolutely *full* of being appreciated — and I get to Sunday evening and notice something hollow that has no right to be there given the week I just had.

[BEAT]

And when I sit with it honestly, here's what it is. Every piece of that warmth came addressed to a version. The strategist got thanked. The host got encouraged. The brand got loved. And they're all me — but no single one of them is *all* of me, and you cannot assemble being seen out of being partially appreciated a thousand times. It doesn't stack, bro. It's the wrong currency. It's like trying to get warm by collecting photographs of fires.

[PAUSE]

And the conviction underneath, the one this episode made me say out loud: I have, at times, *preferred* it this way. The thousand partial knowings are safer. Every audience holds a piece small enough that no piece can hurt me. The fort — the one person, the full file, the before-coffee version — that's the room with actual stakes in it. So I've staffed every stage and left the fort understaffed, and called it being busy. It wasn't being busy. It was choosing the library over the meal, every night, and wondering why I was hungry.

I'm renovating, is what I'll say. Present tense. The fort has a chair in it now. Some weeks two.

[MUSIC FADES OUT — slow, under the next line.]

---

## CHAPTER 7: THE INVITATION

So here's the work this week, and in the spirit of the flip — it's not heavy. It's actually the lightest assignment I've ever given.

Show someone the unimpressive true thing. Not the struggle — we did confession season already, that was the trailer episode. I mean the *silly* true thing. The weird joy. The thing you love that doesn't fit the brand. The playlist that would confuse your followers. The dumb bit you do alone in the car. Invite one earned person into the fort and show them *that* — because here's the secret about being seen: it doesn't start with your wounds. It starts with your play. Kids know it: the secret language comes before the secrets. The fort is built out of nonsense first, and *then* it can hold weight.

[BEAT]

There's a line from Paul — first letter to the Corinthians, the love chapter, the one everybody reads at weddings and nobody reads slowly. Near the end he writes: *"For now we see only a reflection, as in a mirror; then we shall see face to face. Now I know in part; then I shall know fully, even as I am fully known."*

Even as I am *fully known.* Present tense. Paul's claim is that the deepest version of the thing you're hungry for — full knowing, no costume, no partial file — already exists, is already happening, is already *aimed at you.* Every audience knows you in part. One Audience doesn't. And everything we build in the fort — the friend, the spouse, the chair that now has someone in it — all of it is practice, a rehearsal in miniature, for the face-to-face that's already true.

You are not actually unseen, bro. You never were. Now go staff the fort anyway — it's how we practice.

If that's the kind of conversation you've been needing — stay close. We're just getting started over here.

Thanks for being here. MindYourBizniz is a Palmer House conversation. New episode next week.

[ROOM TONE — the mug lifts. Out.]
